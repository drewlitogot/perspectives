You are sitting across from someone you like. They are funny, thoughtful, and genuinely interested in what you have to say.

Unfortunately, the restaurant has other plans.

Music is playing above your table. Someone nearby is laughing loudly. A server keeps passing behind your chair. Every few minutes, dishes crash together somewhere in the kitchen. The overhead lights are too bright, and the person at the next table is wearing enough perfume to announce their arrival from across the room.

Your date asks a question. You heard the words, but you cannot quite put them together.

You smile, try to concentrate, and wonder why something that should be enjoyable feels so difficult.

By the time dinner ends, all you want is to go home.

Was the date bad? Did you dislike the person? Were you nervous? Or were you spending so much effort dealing with the restaurant that you had little attention left for the person sitting across from you?

Those are different possibilities. Learning to recognize them can change how you approach dating.

You cannot always control what a date feels like. You can, however, learn which environments help you relax, which sensations make things harder, and what you need to communicate before a small discomfort becomes a major problem.

That knowledge is your sensory profile.

The world doesn’t feel the same to everyone

Think back to the sculpture-and-glasses picture introduced earlier in this book. Two people can stand in the same place and experience it differently.

That includes the sounds, lights, smells, textures, temperatures, and physical sensations around them.

Consider a busy coffee shop.

One person finds the background conversation comforting. They like hearing people talk, watching customers come and go, and having something to look at when the conversation pauses.

Another person finds that same environment exhausting. Every voice competes for attention. The coffee grinder interrupts their thoughts. They keep losing their place in the conversation.

Neither person is imagining the environment. Neither is experiencing it incorrectly.

They are simply having different experiences in the same room.

Your sensory profile describes how you respond to different kinds of input. It includes what feels comfortable, what becomes uncomfortable, what you barely notice, and what you actively enjoy.

There are three useful patterns to recognize.

Some sensations feel unusually intense. A sound that barely registers for another person might be painful or distracting to you. Bright lights, certain food textures, strong fragrances, or unexpected touch might be difficult to tolerate.

Some sensations are less noticeable. You might not register someone calling your name in a noisy room, or you might need stronger physical input before a sensation becomes noticeable.

Some sensations feel especially enjoyable. You might love deep pressure, dancing, particular textures, rhythmic movement, or the physical closeness of someone you trust.

These patterns can exist together.

You might love loud music through your own headphones but dislike several conversations happening around you. You might enjoy a firm hug but find a light touch on your shoulder unpleasant. You might love spicy food and dislike the texture of mushrooms.

That combination does not have to make sense to anyone else. It only has to describe your experience accurately.

A 2019 meta-analysis of 55 studies found substantial average differences in sensory responses among autistic participants compared with non-autistic comparison groups, including stronger reactions, weaker reactions, and sensory seeking. Most participants were children, however, so the findings should not be treated as a precise description of autistic adults (Ben-Sasson et al., 2019).

A smaller study of autistic adults also found considerable differences between individuals. There was no single sensory pattern shared by everyone in the group (Crane et al., 2009).

The practical lesson is that a diagnosis cannot tell you exactly which sensations another person will enjoy.

Your preferences have to be learned individually.

You don’t need a diagnosis to have sensory needs

If noise makes conversation difficult, that matters whether you are autistic, have ADHD, have experienced trauma, or have never considered any of those explanations.

Research has found sensory differences among adults with ADHD, too.

One Dutch study of 116 adults with ADHD found greater sensitivity and reduced sensitivity in several areas compared with population norms, particularly around sound and activity levels (Bijlenga et al., 2017).

A larger 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 30 studies involving 5,374 participants. On average, participants with ADHD showed differences across several sensory measures compared with control groups. But most studies involved children, results differed considerably between studies, and not every adult comparison showed a clear difference (Jurek et al., 2025).

A separate small Swedish experiment found differences in reported touch sensitivity and physiological responses to touch among young adults with ADHD (Frost-Karlsson et al., 2024).

None of these findings means that everybody with ADHD dislikes noise or enjoys particular kinds of touch.

Experiences involving trauma can also matter.

A 2024 research review described associations between post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and changes in how people respond to sensory information, including sounds, sights, touch, and perceived threats. Researchers have not established whether all these differences develop after trauma, existed beforehand, or involve both possibilities (Fleming et al., 2024).

A sudden sound might be uncomfortable for one person because of its physical intensity. For another, it might also be associated with a frightening experience.

You do not have to determine the cause before deciding that a particular environment is unsuitable for you.

Nor should you assume someone else’s history from their reaction.

A person who dislikes being touched unexpectedly is not necessarily traumatized. A person who enjoys crowded concerts is not necessarily comfortable in crowded restaurants.

Sensory differences are experiences to understand, not clues from which to guess someone’s diagnosis.

If particular reactions are severe, new, or interfering substantially with everyday life, a qualified healthcare or mental health professional may be able to help you explore them. A dating handbook cannot determine their cause.

The setting can change the date

The location of a date is often treated as a minor detail.

You decide what sounds fun, pick somewhere convenient, and assume the interesting part will be the person you meet.

But the location influences how easily you can hear, concentrate, speak, move, and enjoy yourself.

For some people, those conditions can determine whether an otherwise pleasant experience is manageable.

In a UK study involving 24 autistic adults, participants described restaurants, cafés, pubs, supermarkets, public transportation, and busy urban areas as environments that could be especially difficult.

Some participants found outdoor spaces, museums, and cinemas easier. But experiences varied, and even places that seem intense, such as concerts, could be enjoyable for particular individuals.

The researchers identified several features that influenced people’s experiences: the sensory conditions, the available physical space, predictability, understanding from others, opportunities to make adjustments, and chances to recover (MacLennan et al., 2023).

This was a small qualitative study of public places, not a study of successful dating locations. It does not establish that museums make better dates than restaurants.

It does suggest a useful question:

What would make this particular place comfortable enough for you to get to know someone?

A restaurant is not just a restaurant

Consider two restaurants.

The first has dim lighting, closely packed tables, loud music, and servers moving continuously between customers. The second has softer background noise, more space between tables, and a quieter area away from the kitchen.

Both serve dinner. Both might be perfectly good restaurants.

But they offer different experiences.

Now imagine you are choosing between them for a first date.

If you struggle to follow conversation when several people are speaking nearby, the second restaurant may give you a better opportunity to pay attention.

If you dislike very quiet spaces because every conversational pause feels noticeable, you might prefer somewhere with some background activity.

Neither preference means you are dating correctly or incorrectly.

The goal is not to find the universally best location. It is to choose a place where both people have a reasonable chance to feel comfortable.

Predictability can be as important as intensity

Suppose you enjoy live music.

You choose a concert, know the artist, recognize the songs, and expect the volume. You can prepare for the experience.

Now suppose you are having lunch when a restaurant unexpectedly begins playing very loud music.

Even if the volume is similar, the experience might be different because you did not choose it or anticipate it.

In a study based on responses from autistic adults, participants described how control, predictability, stress, tiredness, and other people’s understanding affected their sensory experiences (MacLennan et al., 2022).

This suggests that choosing a date is not necessarily about finding the quietest or least stimulating activity.

You might enjoy something intense when you have chosen it, know what to expect, and can leave when you need to.

A crowded festival could be a wonderful date for one person and an unbearable one for another.

The important detail is whether the experience suits the people involved.

Comfortable does not mean isolated

When looking for quieter environments, remember that comfort and personal safety both matter.

For a first meeting with someone you do not know well, consider a public place where other people are present, you can arrange your own transportation, and either person can leave independently.

A quiet table in a public café may offer more comfort than a busy restaurant. A familiar public park during daylight hours might work well if the location is accessible and neither person has to depend on the other for transportation.

An empty house or secluded location should not automatically become the solution to sensory discomfort.

You can seek an environment with less noise or crowding without giving up your ability to leave.

When the environment uses up your attention

Imagine that you are trying to read a difficult paragraph while someone plays music, another person asks you questions, and an alarm rings repeatedly.

You might still be capable of understanding the paragraph. But understanding it under those conditions becomes much harder.

Dating can involve a similar problem.

You may be trying to listen, notice what someone is saying, think of your response, and understand whether you enjoy their company.

At the same time, your attention may be occupied by uncomfortable lights, noise, smells, movement, or physical sensations.

When more is coming in than you can handle at once, that is overload.

Overload can involve sensory input, questions, social demands, or several things together.

You might find it harder to follow conversation. You might become irritated without immediately knowing why. You might want to escape the environment even though nothing obvious has gone wrong between you and your date.

Sometimes, after overload, a person experiences shutdown: their brain and body pull inward and slow down or stop. Speaking may become very difficult.

In qualitative research, autistic adults have described both overload and pulling inward or disengaging in challenging sensory environments (MacLennan et al., 2022).

These experiences are important because other people cannot necessarily see what is happening.

Your date might notice that you have become quieter.

They might wonder whether they said something wrong.

You might wonder the same thing.

Without communication, two people can develop completely different explanations for the same moment.

Don’t confuse the room with the relationship

At 38, Daniel goes on a first date with Marcus.

They meet at a popular restaurant on a Friday evening.

The conversation begins well. Marcus asks thoughtful questions, remembers details from their messages, and seems genuinely interested.

But the restaurant gets louder.

Daniel has increasing difficulty concentrating. He begins giving shorter answers. Eventually, he feels an intense desire to leave.

When he gets home, his strongest memory of the date is relief that it ended.

He considers telling Marcus that they are not compatible.

But Daniel also remembers enjoying the conversation before the restaurant became crowded. He wonders whether the setting, rather than Marcus, accounted for some of his discomfort.

He decides to suggest a shorter second meeting at a quieter public location.

That is not a requirement to give someone another chance. Daniel is free to decide that he does not want another date.

It is simply an example of how considering the environment might help him make a more informed decision.

There is no validated test that can tell you whether an unpleasant feeling on a date comes from sensory conditions, nervousness, attraction, or incompatibility. Those possibilities can overlap.

What you can do is notice what happened.

Did the environment change before you became uncomfortable? Did conversation become easier after moving somewhere quieter? Did you feel more comfortable when an irritating sound stopped?

Those observations cannot settle every question about a relationship. They can help you avoid treating every unpleasant experience as evidence that the person was wrong for you.

The reverse matters just as much.

If someone dismisses your discomfort, ignores a clear boundary, or pressures you to stay somewhere you want to leave, the problem is not simply the environment.

How someone responds to your needs is part of what you are learning about them.

Touch is not one simple preference

Physical affection deserves particular attention because it involves another person’s body and consent.

Someone who dislikes a casual hug might enjoy holding hands with a romantic partner.

Someone who loves cuddling might dislike being touched while concentrating on another activity.

Someone might enjoy firm pressure but dislike light, unexpected contact.

And someone might prefer little physical touch, including in a romantic relationship.

These are different preferences. None can be inferred reliably from a diagnosis.

In a qualitative study of autistic people’s accounts of sexuality and relationships, participants described sensory experiences that made intimacy more enjoyable as well as experiences that made it difficult. Touch, sound, smell, and visual sensations could all matter (Gray et al., 2021).

A 2026 study compared 31 autistic adults with 43 non-autistic adults, most of them based in the UK.

On average, autistic participants expressed less favorable attitudes toward social touch and reported more touch-related difficulties in romantic relationships.

However, the groups did not differ significantly in their reported frequency of or satisfaction with social touch. They also rated touch in hypothetical intimate situations similarly.

The study was small, included more women than men, and cannot establish that everyone in either group feels the same way (Li et al., 2026).

The distinction is important.

Disliking certain kinds of touch does not necessarily mean disliking affection, romance, or physical closeness.

And enjoying affection does not mean enjoying every possible kind of touch.

The person and the situation both matter

Suppose you dislike being hugged by acquaintances.

Your partner might conclude that you dislike hugs entirely.

But perhaps you enjoy being held by someone you know well, provided you can anticipate the contact.

Or perhaps you dislike hugs but love leaning against your partner while watching a movie.

Your experience might depend on who is touching you, how, when, and whether you have agreed to it.

Rather than assuming, describe the specific preference.

You might say:

“I like holding hands, but sudden hugs catch me off guard. Could you ask first?”

Or:

“I enjoy cuddling, but sometimes I get uncomfortable after a while. I might need to move.”

These statements are not apologies.

They help another person understand what you enjoy and what you do not.

Your date may have their own preferences. Perhaps they dislike holding hands in public but enjoy physical affection in private. Perhaps they prefer more personal space than you do.

The point is to understand each other, not to find the person whose preferences are supposedly normal.

Consent always comes first

Sensory preferences and consent are related, but they are not the same thing.

You might enjoy a particular kind of touch and still not want it at a particular moment.

You might have agreed to hold someone’s hand earlier and not want to now.

You might enjoy kissing but not want a kiss from this person, in this place, or at this time.

Consent means freely agreeing to a particular activity. It must be ongoing, and it can be withdrawn.

RAINN’s US educational guidance emphasizes that consent is specific and freely given. Silence, freezing, withdrawal, or uncertainty should not be treated as permission to continue (RAINN, 2026).

If you are unsure whether someone wants physical contact, ask or wait.

If they say no, stop.

If they pull away, become silent, or cannot clearly agree, stop and check in without pressuring them.

Do not interpret a person’s sensory preferences as an invitation to test what they will tolerate.

And if you recognize that you have been pushing past someone’s discomfort, stop that behavior. Their willingness to remain near you is not permission to continue.

A person’s comfort is not something you have to persuade them to surrender.

If a place can use up everything you have

Some rooms don’t just feel unpleasant, they consume the attention you were going to spend on the person in front of you. By the time you notice, you may have little left for conversation.

Decide the setting before you’re in it, when choosing is still easy: “I’m much better company somewhere quieter. Could we do the coffee place on the corner instead?” That is a preference, not a confession.

Leaving early because a room became unmanageable says nothing about whether you liked the person. You can say both things at once: “I need to head off, and I’d like to do this again.”

When two sensory profiles don’t match

You do not need identical preferences to enjoy dating someone.

But you do need to take both people’s experiences seriously.

Consider Maya and Olivia, two autistic women in their early forties who have begun dating.

Maya loves crowded concerts. She enjoys the energy of the audience, the vibration of the music, and singing along.

Olivia prefers quieter activities. She enjoys galleries, long walks, and conversations where there is little competing noise.

Both are autistic. Their sensory preferences are different.

Neither needs to convince the other that her preferences are more authentically autistic or more reasonable.

Instead, they discuss which experiences they could enjoy together.

They might choose a small concert with seating and an easy way to leave. They might find a quieter music venue. They might decide that some concerts are activities Maya enjoys with friends while they choose other activities as a couple.

That does not automatically represent a problem in the relationship.

Two people can care about each other without enjoying every activity together.

Of course, sometimes the differences are harder to accommodate.

If one person finds crowded nightlife essential to their social life and the other finds those environments consistently intolerable, they may need to consider how much shared time they want in those settings.

Neither person has to abandon an important preference. But the relationship may require choices, alternatives, or compromises that both freely accept.

A compromise is not simply one person enduring discomfort so that the other gets what they want.

It is an arrangement both people can genuinely accept.

Don’t make a sensory preference personal

Imagine that your partner tells you they cannot tolerate your favorite restaurant.

It might be tempting to hear that as criticism of your taste.

But they may be describing the physical environment rather than judging you.

You can ask what makes the place difficult. Perhaps the seating is cramped, the music is loud, or the food has a texture they dislike.

You are allowed to have different preferences.

What matters is whether you can discuss them respectfully.

Likewise, if your partner says they do not want to be touched, do not automatically interpret that as a measure of their attraction or affection.

Take the answer seriously.

You can discuss what kinds of closeness each of you enjoys, but no one owes physical contact to reassure another person.

The same standard applies if the roles are reversed.

How to talk about your sensory needs

You do not have to introduce yourself to a date by explaining your brain, medical history, or every uncomfortable experience you have ever had.

Sometimes a simple description is enough.

“Loud restaurants make it hard for me to follow a conversation.”

“I do better somewhere without really bright lighting.”

“I like having a chance to step outside if a place gets crowded.”

These statements give another person useful information without asking them to make assumptions about you.

They also provide an opportunity for that person to share their preferences.

Perhaps your date loves quiet places. Perhaps they would rather be somewhere lively. Perhaps they do not have a strong preference either way.

You can figure that out together.

A request is different from a boundary

It helps to distinguish between something you are asking someone else to do and a limit you are setting for yourself.

A request asks something of another person. They are allowed to say no.

For example:

“Could we sit away from the speakers?”

A boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept.

For example:

“If the music gets too loud for me, I’ll step outside for a while.”

You can make a request and set a boundary in the same conversation.

If someone cannot accommodate a request, you can decide what you need to do.

Perhaps they cannot change the music. Perhaps the restaurant has no other tables. Perhaps your date has an equally important reason for preferring that location.

You can look for an alternative.

A respectful response does not require another person to satisfy every preference you have. It does require taking a clear limit seriously.

You also have responsibilities.

If your date tells you that an activity makes them uncomfortable, do not mock their preference or pressure them to continue.

You might not understand their experience. You do not have to experience the same sensation to respect what they are telling you.

You can explain a need without explaining its origin

Someone might ask why you dislike a particular sensation.

Sometimes they are genuinely curious.

You can explain if you want to.

You can also say, “It’s just something I find uncomfortable.”

That may be all the information they need.

A person does not need to disclose autism, ADHD, PTSD, or any other personal history to deserve respect for a physical boundary.

If you eventually want to share more about how your brain works or experiences that shaped your preferences, that can be a separate conversation.

For now, it is enough to communicate what helps you participate comfortably.

If you're dating someone who asks to change the plan

A request to move somewhere quieter, sit differently, or leave earlier than expected is usually about the room, not about you. It often costs something to ask, so it tends to mean the evening matters.

Treat it as ordinary information: “Thanks for telling me. Where would be better?” You don’t need to work out the reason, apologize for your original suggestion, or start screening every venue on your own.

What helps most is making the request cheap to repeat. If saying it once is met easily, it gets said earlier next time, before anyone’s evening is spoiled.

Your sensory map

You do not need a formal assessment to begin noticing your preferences.

Try making a simple sensory map. It is a personal reflection exercise, not a diagnostic test or a way to determine which people you should date.

Set aside ten minutes and consider the following:

  1. Think about the sensations around you. Consider sound, lighting, smell, taste and food texture, touch, movement, temperature, and crowding. Which ones do you notice most often?

  2. Sort them into three categories. What draws you in? What wears you out? What depends on the situation? You can put the same sensation in more than one category if the context changes your experience.

  3. Describe the conditions. Instead of writing “I hate noise,” try something more specific: “I enjoy loud music when I choose it, but overlapping conversations make it hard to concentrate.” Specific descriptions are more useful than broad labels.

  4. Choose one adjustment. Think of a public place you might enjoy for a date and one thing that would make it easier. Perhaps you would prefer a quieter hour, a table away from the speakers, an outdoor seating area, or a shorter visit with the option to leave.

  5. Practice saying it aloud. Use one ordinary sentence you would feel comfortable telling another person: “I hear people better in quieter places. Could we meet somewhere with less background music?”

You do not need to complete every category perfectly.

You are learning about yourself, not taking an exam.

A sample sensory map

Here is what one person’s notes might look like.

Sensation Draws me in Wears me out Depends on conditions
Sound Familiar music Several conversations at once Live music
Lighting Natural daylight Flickering lights Dim restaurants
Touch Firm hugs from someone trusted Unexpected touch Holding hands
Food Crunchy textures Certain slippery textures Trying unfamiliar dishes
Movement Walking outdoors Being jostled in crowds Dancing
Temperature Cool rooms Hot, crowded spaces Outdoor activities

This is not a universal pattern, and there is no correct combination of answers.

A different person might love everything in the middle column and dislike everything in the first.

The purpose is to notice patterns that you can use when making choices.

You might discover that your main difficulty is not noise itself, but noise you cannot predict. Or that you like physical affection but dislike being surprised by it.

You may also discover pleasant experiences you have been overlooking.

Your profile should include what makes life enjoyable, not just what you want to avoid.

Try a small experiment on your next date

Once you know something about your preferences, you can use that information.

Suppose you have often chosen busy restaurants because you assumed that was what adults were supposed to do on dates.

For your next date, you could try something different.

Choose a public location where you expect to be more comfortable. Let the other person know what you have in mind. Make sure they have a genuine opportunity to suggest something else.

During the date, notice whether the conditions make conversation easier or harder.

If something becomes uncomfortable, make an adjustment when possible.

You might move to another table, take a brief break, or agree to finish earlier.

You do not need to make the entire outing about sensory needs. The point is to create conditions where you have more opportunity to enjoy the person you are meeting.

Afterward, consider two separate questions:

How did I feel about the environment?

What did I learn about the person?

You might have enjoyed both.

You might have liked the person but disliked the setting.

You might have loved the activity but realized you were not particularly interested in getting to know your date again.

Or you might still be unsure.

That is fine. One date does not have to answer every question.

And a more comfortable setting does not guarantee attraction or compatibility. Research has not established that particular sensory adjustments improve dating success. The adjustments described here are practical choices informed by studies of sensory experiences, not proven methods of improving relationship outcomes.

Their immediate purpose is simpler: to make it easier for you and another person to participate comfortably and freely.

Your needs matter, and so do theirs

Knowing your sensory profile can help you make better choices, but it does not give you exclusive control over a shared experience.

Your date has a body, preferences, limits, and experiences of their own.

They may want something different from you.

You can ask for a quieter restaurant. They can say they would prefer somewhere else. You can suggest an alternative. Either of you can decide that a particular activity is not right.

You are responsible for expressing your needs as clearly as you reasonably can. You are not responsible for making another person experience the world exactly as you do.

Likewise, you cannot expect your date to know what you need without being told, particularly when your reactions are not visible.

If you are dating someone whose sensory experiences differ from yours, curiosity is more useful than guessing.

Ask what helps. Listen to the answer. Share your own experience.

And remember that the answer may change with the circumstances.

A person might enjoy a place one day and find it difficult another day. They might want closeness in one moment and space in the next.

Consent and respect still apply every time.

You do not have to find someone whose sensory map matches yours perfectly.

You need enough understanding, consideration, and willingness to find experiences that work for both of you.

Your sensory profile is not a defect to conceal, a label to prove, or a list of demands that another person must satisfy. It is information about the conditions in which you can participate, connect, and enjoy yourself.

Knowing that information gives you more meaningful choices.

You can choose places where conversation comes more easily. You can explain discomfort before assuming it means a relationship is wrong. You can recognize what kinds of affection you enjoy and communicate what you do not want.

And you can offer another person the same courtesy.
