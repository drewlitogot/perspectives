Chapter 14: Realistic expectations

You meet someone who makes you laugh. You like their face, their voice, and the way they remember something you mentioned last Tuesday. They enjoy the same obscure television show. Your conversations are easy.

By the third date, you are imagining your first vacation together. By the fifth, you have wondered what living together might look like. You have even pictured introducing them to your family.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying those thoughts. The trouble begins when the future you imagined starts feeling like something the other person has already promised.

You know a few things about them. Your imagination has supplied the rest.

Or perhaps your expectations work in the opposite direction. You meet someone kind and attractive, but they do not resemble the person you always pictured. They wear the wrong clothes, have an ordinary job, or don’t share your favorite hobby. You dismiss them before finding out whether you actually enjoy being together.

Both patterns have something in common: an imagined relationship has begun making decisions about a real person.

Realistic expectations do not mean giving up romance, accepting mistreatment, or settling for somebody you do not want. They mean allowing another person to be real, including the parts you cannot predict.

The love story you learned before you ever dated

Long before your first relationship, you probably encountered hundreds of fictional ones.

Two characters lock eyes across a room. Their attraction is immediate. Obstacles appear, but the story assures you that these people belong together. After an argument, one delivers a perfect speech. A misunderstanding disappears. The music rises.

The credits roll before they have to discuss who forgot to pay the electricity bill.

Stories are designed to hold your attention. They select the most meaningful moments and arrange them into something satisfying.

A real relationship includes the moments that would get cut from the movie.

Someone has a headache. Someone needs an evening alone. One person enjoys elaborate birthday celebrations; the other would rather eat takeout in pajamas. A difficult conversation takes three attempts because neither person knows exactly what to say.

None of those things necessarily means the romance has failed.

In a study of popular romantic comedies, researchers found that romantic ideals appeared frequently, especially ideas about soulmates. In a survey of 335 American college students, watching more romantic comedies was associated with slightly stronger beliefs about idealizing a partner, but not with stronger beliefs in every romantic ideal the researchers measured. Students who watched romantic films specifically to learn about relationships tended to endorse more idealized beliefs. The study could not establish that watching the films caused those expectations (Hefner & Wilson, 2013).

That distinction matters.

Enjoying a romantic film is different from treating it as a relationship instruction manual.

You can love the scene where somebody makes a grand declaration in the rain without expecting your date to demonstrate affection through dramatic gestures. You can enjoy a fictional couple who seem to understand each other instantly without expecting a real person to read your mind.

The useful question is not, “Should I stop watching romantic stories?”

It is, “Am I expecting a real person to behave like a character whose entire life was written to produce a satisfying ending?”

Social media shows the highlights, not the relationship

Social media adds another kind of romantic story.

A couple posts photographs from a beautiful vacation. Someone shares a video of an elaborate proposal. Another person describes a partner who always knows exactly what to say.

You see the restaurant, the flowers, and the smiling faces.

You do not see every ordinary Tuesday.

In interviews with 118 Americans aged 18–25, some participants described social media as presenting glamorous relationship ideals that encouraged comparison. Their accounts show that this experience exists, although the study did not establish how common it is across the population or prove that viewing such posts causes relationship dissatisfaction (Munshi et al., 2024).

A photograph may show a genuinely happy moment. It just cannot tell you everything about the relationship behind it.

The couple in matching sweaters might have a wonderful relationship. They might also disagree about money, chores, or family obligations. Both things can be true.

If somebody else’s posts make your own dating life seem disappointing, ask what information is missing.

You know what the couple chose to share. You do not know how they handle disagreement, whether both people feel respected, or whether they are happy when nobody is watching.

The same caution applies to relationship advice packaged as a short video. A confident person speaking into a camera may offer a memorable sentence, but that does not make the sentence a universal rule.

The next chapter examines harmful dating advice in more detail. For now, remember that a convincing story is not the same thing as evidence about your relationship.

There may be many people you could love well

One of the most persistent romantic ideas is that somewhere in the world there is exactly one person meant for you.

If you meet them, everything will make sense. If a relationship becomes difficult, perhaps they were not the one after all.

Researchers distinguish two broad ways of thinking about relationships.

Destiny beliefs treat compatibility as something largely discovered. A relationship is meant to work or it is not.

Growth beliefs treat a relationship as something two people can develop through mutual effort, communication, and adaptation.

These are not necessarily opposite categories. Someone can believe that compatibility matters enormously while also believing that a good relationship requires effort.

A scholarly review describes destiny beliefs as emphasizing evaluation and growth beliefs as emphasizing cultivation. Earlier studies found that destiny beliefs were associated with different responses to relationship difficulties, including a greater tendency toward avoidance coping in one study (Knee, 1998; Knee et al., 2003).

Another study found that people with stronger soulmate beliefs placed greater importance on whether their current partner seemed to match their ideal. That perception was more strongly associated with satisfaction and relationship longevity for them than for people with a stronger work-it-out orientation. These were largely college-student samples, so they do not establish what will happen in every relationship (Franiuk et al., 2002).

Consider two people who have been dating for four months.

Nia enjoys spending time with Omar, but they have different preferences about planning. Nia likes knowing weekend arrangements several days in advance. Omar often makes plans spontaneously.

One interpretation is, “If we were really right for each other, this would happen naturally.”

Another is, “This difference matters. Could we agree on a way of planning that works for both of us?”

The second interpretation creates room to find out whether the problem is manageable.

Maybe Omar agrees to confirm weekend plans by Thursday. Maybe Nia becomes comfortable with some spontaneous activities when she knows what to expect. Perhaps they discover that their preferences are too different to make either person happy.

All three outcomes provide useful information.

Growth does not guarantee compatibility. It gives willing people a chance to discover what they can build together.

Effort is not a substitute for consent

There is an important limit to the idea that relationships require work.

You cannot build a healthy relationship by working hard enough for two unwilling people.

If somebody repeatedly ignores your boundaries, lies to you, treats you with contempt, or does not want a relationship, your effort cannot supply the missing consent or respect.

Likewise, if someone has told you they are not romantically interested, that answer is not an obstacle to overcome. It is their decision.

The useful version of growth thinking is:

“This relationship has a problem. Do both people want to address it, and are both willing to act?”

It is not:

“If I become sufficiently loving, patient, or impressive, this person will eventually become the partner I imagined.”

You can be flexible about preferences without being flexible about whether you are treated with respect.

Hope is not the problem

Some people hear “be realistic” as “stop expecting anything wonderful.”

That would be an unnecessary loss.

You can want affection, excitement, sex, companionship, commitment, humor, and a shared future. You can hope to find someone who feels delighted to see you.

Research does not support treating romantic hope itself as harmful.

In a Canadian study of 270 young adults who were already dating, romantic beliefs were associated with greater relationship satisfaction and commitment. Unmet expectations, meanwhile, were associated with lower satisfaction and commitment. Importantly, holding romantic beliefs was not itself associated with having more unmet expectations (Vannier & O’Sullivan, 2017).

The distinction is between hoping for something and insisting that reality must match a particular imagined version of it.

Compare these expectations:

“I want a partner who cares about me.”

“My partner should always know what I need without my having to explain.”

The first describes a reasonable relationship goal. The second demands a specific ability that another person may not have.

Or compare:

“I want affection.”

“If someone really loves me, they will want physical affection whenever I do.”

Wanting affection is fine. Expecting another person to have exactly the same needs and desires at every moment is not. Affection still requires mutual interest and consent.

Realistic expectations protect your hopes by allowing you to pursue them without confusing them with promises.

Look at behavior, not just potential

Positive expectations can help or hurt depending on the relationship.

A study following American newlywed couples over four years found that positive expectations were more beneficial when couples demonstrated stronger relationship interaction skills. When those skills were weaker, positive expectations were associated with sharper declines in satisfaction (McNulty & Karney, 2004).

These were married couples, not strangers meeting for coffee. Still, the distinction suggests a useful question.

Is your confidence based on what the two of you actually do, or on what you imagine might eventually happen?

Suppose your partner says, “Communication matters to me.”

That is encouraging.

Now suppose you disagree about something. Does your partner listen? Can they explain what they mean? Do they recognize your point of view even when they disagree? Can you do the same?

Those observations tell you more than an attractive statement about the importance of communication.

There is also a difference between seeing the good in someone you know and inventing good qualities in someone you barely know.

A longitudinal study of existing dating couples found that positive perceptions of a partner were associated with later relationship satisfaction and stability (Murray et al., 1996).

So realism does not require making a list of someone’s flaws or constantly questioning their good intentions.

You can appreciate the actual strengths of a partner. You can even be generous when interpreting an ordinary mistake.

What you should avoid is substituting imagined strengths for evidence.

If someone regularly follows through on commitments, trust can develop.

If you have seen them follow through once, you have one encouraging observation.

Those are not the same thing.

Your partner is a person, not an entire life

Romantic stories sometimes present a relationship as the answer to every important human need.

The right partner becomes your best friend, primary source of entertainment, emotional support, adventure companion, intellectual equal, and reason to get out of bed.

It can sound beautiful. It is also a tremendous amount to ask of one person.

A historical and theoretical analysis by Finkel and colleagues argues that modern American marriages are often expected to provide personal growth and self-fulfillment alongside intimacy and companionship. The authors suggest that these ambitions demand substantial investment from partners. This is an explanatory framework, not proof that modern expectations cause relationship unhappiness (Finkel et al., 2014).

Consider what you might expect from a partner.

If you enjoy museums but your partner prefers hiking, does that mean something essential is missing?

Maybe. If spending every weekend together doing the same activities is important to both of you, the difference deserves attention.

But perhaps you visit museums with a friend and hike together occasionally. Your partner may share your values and enjoy your company without sharing every hobby.

You can also maintain friendships, interests, family connections where healthy, and activities that belong to you alone.

A relationship can become an important part of your life without having to replace the rest of it.

The goal is not to make your expectations small.

It is to decide which expectations actually belong to a romantic relationship.

The perfect-partner checklist

Imagine writing down everything you want in a partner.

They should be attractive to you, funny, emotionally mature, financially responsible, adventurous, tidy, affectionate, independent, and interested in your favorite activities.

Perhaps you also want a particular height, career, clothing style, musical taste, political outlook, educational background, and way of texting.

Soon you have thirty requirements.

Some are essential. Some are reasonable preferences. Some are details borrowed from a picture in your head.

The difficulty is that a list can make every item seem equally important.

Someone could be caring, interesting, reliable, attractive to you, and compatible with your major life goals, yet fail because they prefer country music and have never watched your favorite science-fiction series.

This is not an argument for abandoning preferences.

It is an invitation to examine which preferences genuinely matter.

What the research says about lists

Research has tested whether people’s stated ideal-partner preferences predict their romantic evaluations.

A 2014 review and meta-analysis examined differences between what people say they want, what they respond to in hypothetical partners, and what seems relevant in actual romantic evaluations (Eastwick et al., 2014).

A much larger international study published in 2025 included 10,358 participants across 43 countries and 22 languages. It found that matching people’s stated ideal-partner preferences did predict romantic evaluations to a statistically significant but generally modest degree. The results varied depending on how matching was measured (Eastwick et al., 2025).

In plain language, your preferences contain some useful information, but your checklist cannot reliably calculate whom you will love.

Someone who looks ideal on paper might feel uncomfortable to spend time with. Someone who lacks several preferred traits might turn out to be a wonderful companion.

Neither possibility means you should ignore attraction or fundamental incompatibility.

It means an actual interaction provides information that a list cannot.

A useful way to examine your list

Take your imagined partner checklist and sort it into three categories.

  1. Essential: Things necessary for a relationship you can freely choose and enjoy. These might include mutual respect, compatible goals about having children, honesty, or an important religious or lifestyle commitment.
  2. Preferred: Things that would make a relationship more enjoyable but could reasonably be different. These might include hobbies, clothing styles, educational background, or particular shared activities.
  3. Imagined scenes: Specific pictures of romance, such as someone leaving handwritten notes every morning, proposing in a particular location, or spending every free evening together.

The third category is especially revealing.

Sometimes the scene represents a reasonable underlying need.

Wanting handwritten notes might really mean wanting thoughtful gestures.

Wanting daily messages might mean wanting dependable communication.

Wanting elaborate dates might mean wanting someone to invest time and attention in the relationship.

Once you understand the need underneath the scene, you can recognize more than one way a partner might meet it.

For example, one person expresses care through frequent messages. Another remembers that your work presentation is on Friday and asks how it went.

Neither form of attention automatically means more. You can discuss which forms matter to you and see whether both people are willing and able to accommodate each other.

You are not required to accept every form of affection as sufficient. You are simply allowing the possibility that care may appear differently from the picture you rehearsed.

And if an essential requirement is truly essential, keep it.

Being realistic does not mean dating somebody who fundamentally wants a different life.

When you fall for the person you imagine

Calvin, 38, meets Eli through a dating app.

They exchange messages for a week. Eli is funny, thoughtful, and interested in the same music. They have one enjoyable video call.

Calvin starts imagining them traveling together. He thinks about what Eli would be like as a long-term partner. He wonders whether this is finally the relationship he has been looking for.

Then Eli says they are enjoying the conversation but would like to get to know each other slowly.

Calvin feels unexpectedly disappointed.

Nothing terrible has happened. Eli has been straightforward. But Calvin is responding partly to the loss of a future that existed only in his imagination.

That is what makes early idealization difficult to recognize.

You may genuinely like the person in front of you. At the same time, you may be filling in details you have not learned.

Perhaps somebody enjoys spending time with children, so you assume they want to become a parent.

Perhaps they listen carefully during a first date, so you assume every disagreement will be easy.

Perhaps you both experienced difficult childhoods, so you assume you will understand each other perfectly.

Each observation might be meaningful. None proves the conclusion.

There is no research in this chapter’s evidence establishing a specific number of dates after which you can accurately know someone’s character. The practical goal is not to suppress excitement. It is to keep your picture of the person open to new information.

Separate what you know from what you hope

When you notice yourself getting attached to a possible future, try three questions.

What have I actually observed?

“Eli asks thoughtful questions and remembers things I say.”

What am I assuming?

“Eli will be emotionally available in a long-term relationship.”

What do I still need to learn?

“How does Eli communicate when something is difficult? What kind of relationship does Eli want? Do our daily lives fit together?”

This is not an interrogation checklist for your next date. You do not have to ask every question immediately.

It is a way to distinguish evidence from imagination.

You can enjoy the feeling of possibility and say, “I like getting to know you. I’d enjoy seeing you again.”

You do not have to pretend the relationship is already more established than it is.

And remember that the other person is also learning about you. Their interest may grow, change, or end. Yours may do the same.

Someone can like you sincerely without promising a future together.

You can also discover that someone you initially adored is not right for you. Changing your mind as you learn more is not a failure.

Your dating reality is not your human value

Dating can feel particularly difficult when you are attracted to people who do not return your interest.

You may begin to wonder whether there is something fundamentally wrong with you.

If you use dating apps, the experience can be especially stark.

You see a photograph and a short description. You express interest. Perhaps nothing happens. Perhaps you get a friendly reply that leads nowhere.

After enough disappointing experiences, it is tempting to imagine that everyone has been assigned a ranking and yours is too low.

That is not a useful description of your worth.

What online-dating data actually measures

A 2018 study examined messaging behavior on one large online dating platform in four American cities during one month in 2014.

The researchers created a ranking based on whom users messaged and how those interactions occurred. People of both sexes tended, on average, to contact people ranked above themselves in this particular messaging network. Larger gaps were associated with lower chances of receiving a reply.

However, the most common ranking gap was close to zero. The study does not show that everybody was exclusively pursuing the most sought-after users (Bruch & Newman, 2018).

More importantly, the ranking described attention within that dating platform.

It did not measure whether somebody would be a thoughtful partner, a dependable parent, a good friend, or a person worth loving.

The research also concerned heterosexual exchanges on a particular platform in four cities. It cannot describe every queer dating environment, rural community, current app, or face-to-face encounter.

Another set of studies found that while people sometimes agree about who seems attractive at first, romantic evaluations have a substantial individual component. As people become more familiar with each other, consensus about desirability tends to matter less (Eastwick & Hunt, 2014).

This means that first impressions and personal connection are not identical.

You might feel indifferent toward someone whom many other people find attractive. You might be strongly attracted to someone your friends barely notice.

A dating app cannot predict every connection that could develop.

What you can change, and what you cannot

Realistic dating expectations include accepting that there are practical constraints.

Not everyone you find attractive will be attracted to you.

Not everyone you meet will be single, looking for the same kind of relationship, or interested in dating at that time.

And even when two people are attracted to each other, they may want incompatible futures.

You cannot control another person’s feelings or choices.

You can decide how honestly you present yourself, whether you initiate respectful conversations, how you respond to rejection, and whether you give potentially compatible people a chance to become more familiar.

If you repeatedly find that your approach is not producing meaningful connections, you can examine it without attacking yourself.

For example, are you choosing potential dates almost entirely from photographs, despite caring deeply about how someone communicates? Are you ruling out people because they do not match an imagined aesthetic? Are you spending all your dating time in environments where you cannot comfortably have a conversation?

Those are choices worth examining.

The answer is not automatically to pursue someone you do not find attractive. Nor is it to calculate your supposed rank and seek someone below it.

The goal is mutual attraction and compatibility, not winning a competition for the most desired person.

Another person’s lack of interest is not a verdict on your value. Your lack of interest in someone else is not a verdict on theirs.

Both people get to choose.

Perspective: Men

If the dating messages you have absorbed make romance feel like a competition in which you must prove your worth, consider what happens when you stop treating every interaction as a test of your ranking.

You can express interest plainly, accept a no, and look for someone who is interested in you too. You do not need to demonstrate superiority over other people to deserve a caring relationship. And another person’s rejection does not make them an enemy.

Perspective: Women

If you have learned that a good relationship should involve grand romantic gestures or someone who anticipates your every need, consider whether those expectations leave room for an ordinary, respectful person who communicates differently.

You can ask for affection, initiative, and thoughtfulness without expecting somebody to perform a particular romantic script. You do not have to accept neglect or disrespect just because a person is otherwise kind.

Perspective: Non-binary

If dating sometimes makes you feel that being understood or accepted is such a major achievement that you should be grateful for any interest, remember that recognition is only a starting point.

You are allowed to want attraction, warmth, shared goals, reliability, and a relationship in which your boundaries matter. Someone respecting your identity does not create an obligation to date them, and neither does someone expressing interest in you.

These are situations a person might recognize, not rules about what any gender thinks or wants.

Different minds, different romantic scripts

Sometimes two people genuinely want a relationship but have learned different definitions of what romance should look like.

One person expects frequent texting. Another enjoys exchanging thoughtful messages but finds constant notifications exhausting.

One feels cared for when their partner organizes a surprise. Another prefers being consulted because unexpected changes create overload.

One imagines the ideal date as a crowded restaurant and a late night out. Another imagines sitting together in a quiet room, talking about a shared interest.

Neither preference is automatically more romantic.

The problem comes when someone assumes their preferred expression of love is the only legitimate one.

Suppose Avery enjoys spontaneous outings, while their partner Jules prefers advance plans and clear start and end times.

Avery might initially think, “Jules doesn’t seem excited about spending time together.”

Jules might think, “Avery doesn’t take my needs seriously.”

If neither describes what is happening, both may misunderstand the other.

They can begin with direct explanations:

“I enjoy surprising people, but I didn’t realize surprise plans were stressful for you.”

“I like spending time together. I just enjoy it more when I know what to expect.”

From there, they can discuss whether there is a version of their time together that both genuinely enjoy.

Research on relationships involving one autistic and one non-autistic partner offers some support for attending to these differences. In a small Australian qualitative study involving 13 participants, interviewees described mutual care and complementary strengths alongside challenges in communication and interpreting emotions (Smith et al., 2021).

The study was too small to establish universal patterns. It also did not test beliefs about soulmates or romantic media.

The useful lesson is to avoid assuming that your personal interpretation of an interaction is the only possible one.

This connects to the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book. Two people may be looking at the same relationship from different positions and with different experiences shaping what they notice.

Describing your view, then asking about theirs, gives both people information they would otherwise be missing.

The same principle applies when both partners are autistic, both have ADHD, or have other similarities in how they process the world.

For example, two autistic people might both prefer explicit communication but have very different needs for solitude, physical affection, or spontaneity.

Sharing a label does not mean sharing every preference.

Likewise, a neurotypical person dating a neurodivergent partner is not automatically the person who understands relationships correctly. Both people have experiences, limits, preferences, and responsibilities.

Neither needs to abandon their needs to prove affection.

A relationship becomes more workable when each person can describe what matters to them, hear what matters to the other, and choose freely whether they can accommodate those differences.

What a realistic relationship looks like

A realistic relationship is not one without disappointment.

People forget things. They misunderstand each other. They have bad days. Their preferences differ.

The important questions are how those differences are handled and whether the relationship remains something both people want.

A realistic expectation might sound like this:

“I want somebody who enjoys being with me, but I don’t expect them to be available every hour.”

“I want honest communication, but I understand we may sometimes need time to find the right words.”

“I want affection, but I respect that another person’s desire and consent may differ from mine.”

“I want a partner who takes my needs seriously, and I will take theirs seriously too.”

“I want to feel attracted to someone, and I am open to discovering attraction through getting to know them.”

“I hope this relationship becomes something meaningful, but I don’t need to decide its entire future on the first date.”

Notice what these expectations have in common.

They allow room for two separate people.

That is the part romantic stories sometimes skip. Two people can love each other deeply and still have different thoughts, desires, energy levels, histories, and ways of approaching the world.

Neither person becomes a supporting character in the other’s life.

You are looking for someone who wants to build a relationship with you, not someone whose job is to fulfill a role you wrote before meeting them.

A small exercise: keep the hope, check the story

Think about someone you are interested in, someone you dated in the past, or an imaginary person who matches your ideal.

Complete these three sentences:

1. “The relationship I hope to have is…”

Describe the experience, not just the person’s attributes.

For example: “I want to feel comfortable talking honestly, enjoy doing ordinary things together, and know that we both want to spend time with each other.”

2. “The romantic story I might be expecting is…”

Be specific.

For example: “I expect that when I meet the right person, conversation will always be effortless and neither of us will need to ask for reassurance.”

Or: “I imagine that someone who really loves me will want to spend almost all their free time with me.”

3. “A more realistic way to pursue the same hope is…”

Try: “I can look for someone who communicates willingly, while accepting that some conversations will be awkward.”

Or: “I can look for someone who makes time for me without expecting our schedules and social needs to be identical.”

You do not need to abandon the desire beneath the story.

You are replacing an unnecessarily narrow script with something that two real people could actually agree to.

If you notice that an expectation requires someone to ignore their own needs, change their feelings, or provide affection without freely choosing it, that expectation needs revision.

If you notice that you have been dismissing people for details that do not really affect your life together, consider allowing more room for discovery.

And if you notice that somebody consistently fails to meet an essential need, you do not have to keep waiting for the relationship you imagined.

Give reality a fair chance

The point of dating is not to find a flawless person.

It is not to collect proof that you are desirable, compete with other people, or recreate the most romantic scene you have ever watched.

It is to discover whether you and another person enjoy each other’s company, want compatible things, and can treat each other well.

Sometimes you will meet someone wonderful who is not right for you. Sometimes a person who looks unimpressive on paper will surprise you. Sometimes the attraction is real but the timing, priorities, or willingness to build a relationship do not match.

You can be disappointed without deciding you failed.

You can want a great deal from love without demanding perfection.

And you can let another person become more interesting as you learn who they actually are, instead of needing them to fit the character you imagined.

A relationship worth having should leave room for both people to be known.
