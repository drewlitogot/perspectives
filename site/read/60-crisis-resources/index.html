If you are in immediate physical danger, someone has seriously injured you, or a suicide attempt is underway, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. You do not need to finish reading this chapter first.

If you are thinking about suicide, afraid you might hurt yourself, or experiencing emotional distress that feels impossible to manage alone, call or text 988. You do not have to be suicidal to contact 988. You can also use it when you are worried about someone else (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, n.d.; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, n.d.).

Sometimes a relationship problem is no longer something you can solve through better communication, clearer boundaries, or another conversation.

Perhaps a breakup has left you frightened by your own thoughts. Perhaps someone you are dating has threatened you. Perhaps you experienced sexual assault and cannot decide what to do next. Or perhaps a person you care about has said something that makes you worry about their safety.

You might be uncertain whether your situation counts as a crisis. You might have trouble explaining what happened. You might know that something is wrong but need hours to identify what you are feeling.

You can ask for help before you have all the answers.

This chapter is a directory and a guide to using it. It explains which services handle which situations, what to expect when contacting them, and what their limitations are. Keep it somewhere you can find it again.

Which kind of help do you need?

The first decision is not which organization is best. It is whether someone needs immediate physical protection, crisis counseling, or practical assistance.

When someone is in immediate danger: 911

Call 911 for an immediate threat to life or physical safety, such as serious injury, a suspected overdose, violence in progress, or a suicide attempt underway.

Emergency services can send responders, including medical personnel, firefighters, or police, depending on the situation. An emergency department can assess urgent medical and psychiatric needs (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, n.d.).

If you are unsure whether an injury or immediate threat needs emergency care, you can explain what is happening to the emergency dispatcher.

You do not have to determine a diagnosis, predict the person’s intentions, or prove that the situation is serious enough.

When someone needs emotional crisis support: 988

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available 24 hours a day, seven days a week, across the United States.

It is not only for people who have decided to end their lives. It also supports people experiencing emotional distress involving relationships, loneliness, substance use, financial problems, or other difficult situations (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, n.d.; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, n.d.).

For example, imagine that Marcus, 46, has recently separated from his husband. He has not made a plan to hurt himself, but he has barely slept and keeps thinking that life has no future.

Marcus does not have to decide whether those thoughts meet a particular medical definition. He can contact 988, describe what is happening, and ask for help.

The same service is available to someone who is worried about a partner, friend, or family member.

When the immediate problem is practical: 211

Suppose a relationship has ended and you have nowhere affordable to stay next month. You need information about housing assistance, food, transportation, or local services.

211 connects people with community resources. It is an information and referral service, rather than a replacement for emergency medical care or crisis counseling (United Way 211, n.d.).

A person can need more than one kind of support. You might contact 988 because you are struggling emotionally and later use 211 to explore local assistance.

If you are facing abuse, however, finding somewhere safe to stay may require specialized safety planning. A domestic-violence advocate is a better starting point for that particular concern.

What actually happens when you contact 988?

Making the first contact can feel harder when you do not know what comes next.

When you call 988, you generally encounter an initial routing process before reaching a crisis counselor. With text or online chat, you may answer introductory questions before being connected.

A counselor can ask about your immediate safety, listen to what is happening, and help you consider next steps. The service aims to provide support through conversation whenever that is sufficient (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, n.d.).

You do not need to prepare a complete account of your relationship history.

You might begin with:

“I broke up with someone, and I’m having thoughts about hurting myself. I don’t know what to do next.”

Or:

“I’m not suicidal, but I’m very distressed and don’t feel able to manage this alone.”

You can say that you need more time to answer questions, that you are finding it difficult to speak, or that you do not know how to describe your feelings.

Is contacting 988 confidential?

988 is confidential, but it is not completely anonymous.

You are not generally required to provide your name or location to receive support. However, conversations may be documented, technical information may be collected, and records can be retained.

Information can also be disclosed under certain circumstances, including an imminent safety threat or a legal requirement. Practices and legal obligations can differ by state and crisis center (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 2026).

This does not mean every call results in police involvement.

988’s stated approach is to use the least restrictive response consistent with safety. Emergency intervention, including contacting 911, can occur when someone faces imminent danger and the risk cannot be addressed through conversation (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, n.d.).

Do not rely on either extreme assumption: that calling automatically summons police, or that emergency responders can never become involved.

If you are worried about confidentiality, you can ask before explaining personal details:

“Can you tell me what information you keep and when you might need to contact emergency services?”

You deserve an understandable explanation of the service’s limits.

When the person frightening you is someone you’re dating

Fear can become part of a relationship gradually.

You might notice that you are constantly worried about upsetting your partner. You might feel unable to end the relationship safely. Perhaps someone has threatened you, tried to isolate you, or made you afraid of what will happen if you disagree.

You do not have to settle the question of whether the relationship meets a legal definition of domestic violence before contacting an advocate.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline provides confidential advocacy and support throughout the United States:

800-799-7233 (800-799-SAFE)

Text START to 88788, or use the chat service at thehotline.org.

The service operates 24 hours a day and can help you understand options, locate resources, and develop an individualized safety plan. A safety plan is a set of decisions and arrangements intended to reduce danger in a particular person’s circumstances (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.a, n.d.c).

An advocate does not have to make the relationship decision for you.

For example, Omar, 52, is frightened by his girlfriend’s escalating threats. He does not know whether he wants to leave immediately, whether he can afford another place to live, or how to discuss what is happening with his family.

He can contact an advocate even while those questions remain unanswered.

Domestic and dating abuse can affect people of any gender or sexual orientation. A person does not need to fit a familiar picture of an abuse survivor to ask for support.

Why a personal safety plan matters

A simple instruction such as “just leave” can ignore important dangers.

Someone might share a home, finances, transportation, or parenting responsibilities with an abusive partner. Someone else might face threats after attempting to separate.

The Hotline recommends individualized planning because a single checklist cannot account for every person’s circumstances (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.c).

If you think you are being monitored, that matters too. The Hotline warns that phones, accounts, and internet activity can be monitored, and that private browsing does not guarantee privacy (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.b).

If you are concerned that seeking help on your usual device could put you in danger, consider contacting an advocate through a device or communication method you believe is safer. A trusted person may be able to help you arrange that contact.

Do not assume that deleting browser history makes a monitored device safe.

For immediate physical danger, contact emergency services rather than relying on a website or a delayed response from someone you know.

You are also allowed to contact an advocate if the person experiencing abuse is someone you care about. You can ask how to support them without taking control of their decisions.

If you recognize yourself behaving harmfully

If you have threatened, intimidated, monitored, or hurt a partner, stop the harmful behavior. Do not treat their fear as a communication problem they must help you solve.

Respect their boundaries, including any decision to end contact. Seek qualified professional help to address your behavior without placing responsibility for your safety or change on your partner.

If someone faces immediate physical danger from your actions, emergency assistance takes priority.

After sexual assault

Sexual assault can leave you dealing with several decisions at once.

You may need medical attention. You may be frightened of the person who hurt you. You may be uncertain about reporting to police. You may not yet have a clear name for what happened.

You do not need to resolve all of those questions before seeking support.

The RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline is available 24 hours a day:

800-656-4673 (800-656-HOPE)

Text HOPE to 64673, or use online chat at rainn.org/help-and-healing/hotline.

RAINN provides emotional support and can connect you with local sexual-assault services, medical resources, and information about your options (Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network, n.d.).

If you have serious injuries or are in immediate danger, seek emergency medical attention first.

Medical care and reporting are different questions

You may have heard the term sexual assault forensic exam, sometimes shortened to SAFE.

This is an examination conducted by a qualified health-care professional that can address medical concerns and collect possible evidence of an assault.

RAINN explains that an examination may be available even if you have not decided whether to report to police. During an exam, you can ask questions, decline individual procedures, request a pause, or stop the examination. You can also ask about having an advocate or support person present (Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network, 2026).

Medical care can matter even if you do not want evidence collected.

It is best to seek advice promptly because some medical needs and evidence-collection opportunities are time-sensitive. The relevant time windows depend on the circumstances.

Reporting obligations, evidence retention, and other legal requirements vary among states. Special rules can apply when a minor or a vulnerable adult is involved (Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network, 2026).

Ask a local advocate or qualified health-care professional what applies where you live.

RAINN also explains limits to hotline confidentiality, including circumstances involving safety concerns or mandatory reporting. If that worries you, ask about those limits before sharing identifying information (Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network, n.d.).

Needing information does not obligate you to make every decision immediately.

When talking is difficult

Not everyone can make an ordinary telephone call during a crisis.

You might be crying, shaking, or unable to organize your thoughts. You might be in sensory overload. During a shutdown, finding or speaking words can become extremely difficult.

You might understand what is happening but need additional time to answer. Or you might simply prefer writing.

These experiences do not make your need for help less legitimate.

988 offers calling, texting, and online chat, so a spoken telephone conversation is not the only route (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, n.d.).

Crisis Text Line is another option. In the United States, texting HOME to 741741 connects you with a trained volunteer crisis counselor. Its volunteers are not medical professionals, and this service does not replace emergency medical care (Crisis Text Line, n.d.).

You can keep your first message short:

“I need help. I am having trouble speaking. Please ask me one question at a time.”

Or:

“I’m frightened and confused. I can text, but I need extra time to answer.”

You are not required to disclose autism, ADHD, a trauma history, or any other diagnosis to explain a communication need.

If previous attempts to get help went badly

One study asked 200 autistic adults about their experiences obtaining mental-health support. Participants described barriers such as professionals not understanding their needs and services that were poorly suited to them. Individually tailored support was something participants valued and wanted (Camm-Crosbie et al., 2019).

The study used an online survey of a self-selected group of UK adults, most of whom were women. It did not test American crisis services or establish that any particular hotline works better for autistic people.

Still, the experiences deserve attention.

You may have previously been misunderstood when you explained distress. Someone may have mistaken a flat tone for calmness, assumed you were fine because you could answer factual questions, or focused on a diagnosis instead of what you were saying.

A previous disappointing experience does not mean your current safety concern should go unanswered.

You can describe your needs plainly:

“I may sound calm, but I am not safe.”

“I need direct questions. I find it difficult to explain what I’m feeling.”

“Please explain what will happen next before asking me to decide.”

If you cannot get the help you need through one communication route, another available route or service may be more workable. No particular hotline can be guaranteed to provide the right response every time.

Language and accessibility options

988 provides additional access routes (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, n.d.):

  • Spanish: Call 988 and press 2, or text AYUDA to 988. Spanish-language chat is also available.
  • Deaf or hard-of-hearing users: ASL videophone access is available through the 988 website, alongside TTY and relay options, including 711 then 988.
  • Veterans, service members, and loved ones: Call 988 and press 1, or text 838255 for the Veterans Crisis Line.

If you need another accommodation, say what would help when possible. Availability of particular interpreters, local responders, and specialized services may vary.

If speaking on the phone is not something you can do

Needing help and being unable to make a phone call is a common combination, and it is not a reason to go without. Every service listed here has a text or chat route, and using it is not a lesser version of asking.

Work it out now rather than in the moment. Save the text numbers into your phone today, while this is theoretical, so that a future version of you doesn’t have to research anything.

If writing is also hard, a prepared sentence helps: “I need help and I find talking difficult.” Sending that is enough to begin.

Specialized support for LGBTQ+ young people

The Trevor Project provides 24-hour crisis counseling for LGBTQ+ young people in the United States.

Call 866-488-7386, text START to 678678, or use online chat at thetrevorproject.org/get-help (The Trevor Project, n.d.).

The Trevor Project is specifically designed for young people. An LGBTQ+ adult outside its intended age group can contact the broader services listed earlier, including 988, without needing to explain or justify their identity.

There is also a specialized 988 Press 3 option for LGBTQ+ youth.

One important detail: this service ended on July 17, 2025, but was restored on September 30, 2026, according to The Trevor Project’s October 1 announcement and the current 988 service information (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, n.d.; The Trevor Project, 2026).

That means an older website may incorrectly say the option is unavailable. The restoration was verified for this chapter on November 21, 2026, but its status should be checked again before publication.

Specialized services can make it easier to find support intended for a particular group. Their availability, however, is not proof that every caller will have the same experience or outcome.

When someone you care about might be suicidal

Perhaps your partner has said they do not want to live. Perhaps a friend has started talking about death in a way that frightens you.

You may worry that asking directly about suicide will give the person an idea.

The National Institute of Mental Health advises asking directly and explains that asking about suicide does not create suicidal thoughts (National Institute of Mental Health, 2024).

You do not need an elaborate script.

“Are you thinking about killing yourself?”

Ask clearly, then listen to the answer.

Do not argue about whether the person has enough reasons to feel distressed. Do not promise secrecy that could prevent you from getting emergency help.

NIMH identifies five useful actions (National Institute of Mental Health, 2024):

  1. Ask. Find out directly whether the person is thinking about suicide.
  2. Be there. Listen without judgment and take the person’s words seriously.
  3. Help keep them safe. If there is immediate danger, seek emergency assistance. When safe and appropriate, help reduce immediate risks while professional support is arranged.
  4. Help them connect. Encourage contact with 988, appropriate health-care professionals, or other dependable support.
  5. Follow up. Check on them after the immediate crisis and encourage continued care.

You can contact 988 yourself to discuss concern about another person. You do not have to wait until the person agrees to make the call (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, n.d.).

Supporting a partner without becoming their only source of help

There is a difference between caring for someone and becoming responsible for managing their entire crisis.

Imagine that Eli, 29, is worried about his autistic boyfriend, who has withdrawn following a difficult week. Eli wants to help but is unsure whether his partner needs quiet, emotional support, or urgent intervention.

He can ask directly about safety instead of interpreting silence as proof that everything is fine or proof that something terrible will happen.

If his boyfriend is safe but needs time to communicate, Eli can respect that need while helping him consider support. If there is immediate danger, Eli should not delay emergency assistance because he is worried about upsetting his partner.

The same principle applies regardless of either person’s neurotype.

You can care about a partner’s preferences while recognizing that a life-threatening emergency requires immediate action.

When suicide threats happen during a breakup

There is an especially difficult situation: someone says they will hurt or kill themselves if you end the relationship, refuse contact, or decline something they want.

Take the safety concern seriously. Do not attempt to determine whether the threat is genuine by arguing, testing the person, or bargaining.

You can contact 988 for guidance, or 911 when there is immediate danger.

You do not have to agree to a relationship, sex, reconciliation, or continued personal contact in exchange for another person’s safety.

A crisis service or qualified professional can help address the safety risk. Your boundaries still matter.

If the person has also frightened, threatened, or harmed you, a domestic-violence advocate can help you consider your own safety.

If you are the person making such threats, stop using the relationship as the condition for your safety. Seek crisis support directly. Your partner cannot be required to remain with you to keep you alive.

If someone you're dating tells you they're in crisis

You do not have to know what to say. Staying, listening, and taking it seriously matter far more than finding the right words, and there is no phrasing that will make this worse if you’re kind.

Ask plainly and offer the practical step: “Are you safe right now?” and then “Can I sit with you while you call or text 988?” Doing it alongside them is easier than telling them to do it.

Don’t carry this by yourself or promise secrecy you shouldn’t keep. If you believe someone is in immediate danger, call 911 or get them to emergency care, and tell them you’re doing it.

What happens after the immediate crisis?

A crisis call is an entry point, not necessarily the end of the problem.

You might feel safer after talking with someone but still face the same housing problem, painful breakup, or mental-health difficulty the following morning.

The next step may involve medical care, community support, a follow-up appointment, or an individualized safety plan.

There is some evidence for structured follow-up after suicidal crises.

A 2018 study examined patients discharged from nine US Veterans Health Administration emergency departments. It compared 1,186 patients who received a clinician-delivered Safety Planning Intervention plus follow-up contacts with 454 who received usual care.

Over six months, documented suicidal behavior occurred in 3.03% of the intervention group compared with 5.29% of the usual-care group. That represented an approximately 45% lower relative risk associated with the combined intervention. Engagement with outpatient care also improved (Stanley et al., 2018).

This was an observational study, not a randomized trial. Most participants were men, and all came from a Veterans Health Administration setting. The findings cannot establish that a single hotline call, a self-written plan, or the same approach for every person will produce those results.

What the study does support is taking continued contact and professional follow-up seriously.

A suicide safety plan and a domestic-violence safety plan also serve different purposes.

For suicidal crises, a clinician may help identify warning signs, sources of support, and ways to reduce immediate risk. For domestic violence, an advocate considers risks related to another person’s threatening or abusive behavior. The plans should not be treated as interchangeable (Stanley et al., 2018; The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.c).

Chapter 59 explains how to find ongoing therapy and support. You can return there when the immediate danger has passed.

If you are preparing for a difficult period

You do not need to be in crisis to make help easier to reach.

Imagine you know that an upcoming breakup, family conversation, or major life change may be difficult. You already know that when you are distressed, searching the internet and making decisions becomes harder.

You can prepare a small personal reference in advance.

Keep the information simple enough that you can use it when concentrating is difficult:

My help plan

If someone is in immediate physical danger: 911.

If I need emotional crisis support: 988 by call or text.

If the problem involves abuse or assault: The relevant specialist hotline from this chapter.

One person I can contact: ____________________

The communication method I find easiest: ____________________

A sentence I can use when I cannot explain much:

“I’m having a hard time staying safe. I need help with the next step.”

You could save this in your phone, write it on paper, or keep it somewhere else you can access safely.

If privacy or monitoring is a concern, choose a way of keeping this information that does not increase your risk.

This is a practical reminder, not a substitute for a clinician-created suicide safety plan or an advocate-developed domestic-violence safety plan.

Helping someone does not require perfect words

A friend may tell you about an assault. A partner may admit they are frightened by their own thoughts. Someone may describe abuse while insisting that they are not ready to leave.

You might be tempted to find the perfect response.

There usually isn’t one sentence that settles everything.

You can listen, take the concern seriously, ask about immediate safety, and help the person reach an appropriate service. If they are not in immediate danger, you can respect their decisions about what support to accept.

Try a straightforward question:

“Would you like me to sit with you while you contact someone who can help?”

Or:

“Do you want help finding the right service, or would you rather contact them privately?”

You can also say:

“I care about you, and I don’t know how to handle this safely on my own. I think someone trained for this situation should help.”

You do not need to become the other person’s therapist, emergency dispatcher, or legal adviser.

And if the person needing support is you, you do not need to become an expert in crisis services before asking for help.

When the book is not enough

Throughout this handbook, you have learned about communication, consent, attraction, boundaries, dating, relationships, and their difficulties.

Those skills matter. They can help you recognize what you need, say it clearly, understand another person’s perspective, and make decisions with greater care.

But they do not make every situation manageable alone.

A frightening relationship is not always a misunderstanding. A suicidal crisis is not a problem that better dating skills can solve. Sexual assault is not an ordinary relationship conflict.

Sometimes the right next step is to stop trying to solve the problem between the two people involved and bring in someone qualified to help.

That might mean calling 911, contacting a crisis counselor, speaking with an advocate, or getting medical care.

You can make that decision even when you are uncertain, embarrassed, frightened, or unable to explain everything.

The point of learning relationship skills is not to become someone who never needs help. It is to become better able to recognize what is happening, understand your choices, and treat yourself and others with care.

When things are safe enough, you can return to the beginning of this book with what you have learned.

Dating is a skill you keep practicing. Knowing when to ask for help belongs to that skill, too.
