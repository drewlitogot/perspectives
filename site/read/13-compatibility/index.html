Two people can enjoy the same movies, laugh at the same jokes, find each other attractive, and still be wrong for each other.

Two other people can have completely different hobbies, personalities, and ways of thinking, yet build a relationship that works.

The difference is compatibility: whether two people can build a life together that meets both people’s important needs, respects their limits, and allows them to be themselves.

Compatibility is not the number of things you have in common. It is not a personality-test score, a shared diagnosis, or how exciting the first date feels.

It is how your lives, needs, choices, and ways of treating each other fit together.

And it has to work for both people.

You are not looking for another version of yourself

Imagine two people meeting for coffee.

Priya, 34, loves science fiction, board games, and quiet weekends. Jordan, 37, enjoys the same things. They talk for three hours. Neither wants the date to end.

There is one substantial difference: Priya definitely wants to raise children. Jordan definitely does not.

They might enjoy several more dates. They might develop strong feelings. But unless one of them genuinely changes their own wishes, there is no future in which both get the family life they want together.

Now consider another couple.

Marcus, 41, loves live music and meeting friends. Daniel, 39, prefers reading, cooking, and spending evenings at home. Marcus enjoys spontaneous plans. Daniel likes knowing what is happening in advance.

Their interests and habits differ. But they both want a committed relationship, agree about where to live, respect each other’s independence, and enjoy the time they spend together. Marcus goes to some concerts with friends. Daniel joins the quieter ones. They plan shared evenings while leaving room for separate activities.

Their differences do not disappear. They find arrangements that both genuinely like.

Which couple is more compatible?

The second couple has a more workable foundation, even though the first appears to have more in common.

Research gives some support to this distinction.

A large study of married couples in Australia, Germany, and the United Kingdom found that having similar personalities explained very little of their reported relationship satisfaction. Individual personality characteristics mattered more than personality resemblance (Dyrenforth et al., 2010).

Another study, following heterosexual couples in Germany, found small positive associations between similarity in some life goals, values, and personality characteristics and relationship satisfaction. The study also examined stability over a year. The findings suggest that some kinds of similarity matter, but they do not establish that resembling your partner guarantees a successful relationship (Arránz Becker, 2013).

These studies examined established couples, not people deciding whether to go on a second date.

Still, they offer a useful warning: having a lot in common is not the same as wanting the same life.

Three kinds of compatibility

When getting to know someone, it helps to distinguish three categories.

Category What it means Example
Must agree A difference that would prevent both people from having an acceptable relationship One person definitely wants children; the other definitely does not
Can negotiate A difference that may have a workable arrangement One person likes frequent social activities; the other needs more quiet time
Nice to share Something that adds enjoyment but is not necessary Both love gardening, horror movies, or the same music

The categories are personal. A detail that one person can negotiate may be essential to someone else.

For example, sharing a religion might be important to one person, while another is comfortable dating someone with different beliefs. Neither preference automatically makes someone unreasonable.

The question is not whether a difference should matter to everyone.

The question is whether it matters to you, and whether the two people involved can freely agree on how to live with it.

Must agree: your real nonnegotiables

A dealbreaker is a condition that makes a relationship unacceptable or unworkable for you.

It does not necessarily mean the other person has done anything wrong.

Some common areas worth discussing include:

  • Whether you want children, and whether you want to raise children already in the family.
  • Whether you want a committed relationship, a casual relationship, or another clearly agreed arrangement.
  • Whether the relationship will be monogamous or follow another mutually agreed structure.
  • Where you want to live and whether you would consider relocating.
  • Religious beliefs or practices that affect everyday life.
  • Attitudes toward alcohol or other substance use.
  • Major expectations about money, work, and shared responsibilities.

In six studies involving more than 6,500 participants, researchers found that people considered undesirable characteristics and incompatible intentions when evaluating potential partners. Dealbreakers were particularly important in judgments about long-term relationships. Much of this research used hypothetical choices, however, so it cannot tell you exactly what someone will reject in real life (Jonason et al., 2015).

A dealbreaker is not a demand that someone become different.

Suppose Avery wants to live near family in Ohio. Taylor is determined to move overseas and does not want a long-distance relationship.

Avery can say:

“I want to stay close to my family. Moving overseas isn’t something I want for my future.”

Taylor can answer:

“Living overseas is important to me. I don’t think I would be happy staying here.”

Neither person needs to be persuaded out of an important life goal.

They can enjoy having met each other and still decide not to continue dating.

That is a successful use of dating: learning that a relationship would not be right for either person before making larger commitments.

Can negotiate: differences that need an arrangement

Many differences are not dealbreakers.

They become problems when both people assume their preferred way of doing things is the only reasonable one.

Consider two people who enjoy spending time together but have different expectations about communication.

One likes sending messages throughout the day. The other works in a busy environment, rarely checks their phone, and prefers talking in the evening.

They could interpret this difference as a lack of interest.

Or they could discuss it.

“I enjoy hearing from you during the day. I don’t need constant messages, but I like knowing we’re still connected.”

“I like hearing from you too. I usually can’t concentrate on messages at work. Would a call after dinner work?”

A workable agreement might be an occasional daytime message and a longer evening conversation.

But perhaps the first person needs more frequent contact than the second is comfortable giving. If neither is happy with the available arrangements, that difference may become a dealbreaker.

A compromise works only when both people can genuinely accept it.

An arrangement where one person repeatedly ignores their own needs to prevent the other from leaving is not a healthy solution.

Nice to share: the extras

Shared interests can make getting to know someone easier.

If you both enjoy hiking, cooking, or video games, you already have activities to discuss and possibly enjoy together.

Research combining many studies has found that both actual and perceived similarity are associated with interpersonal attraction. Perceived similarity, meaning how similar someone believes another person to be, also matters in existing relationships (Montoya et al., 2008).

But attraction is not the same outcome as long-term satisfaction.

Someone who likes every band on your playlist might still be unreliable, dismissive, or uninterested in the life you want.

Someone who has never heard of your favorite band might listen with genuine curiosity because it matters to you.

You do not have to share every interest to appreciate one another’s enjoyment.

What do you want an ordinary Tuesday to look like?

Compatibility is often easier to recognize in ordinary life than on an exciting date.

A special evening tells you whether you enjoy a special evening together. It may tell you less about the routine you would eventually share.

Consider a Tuesday after work.

Do you want to cook together? Order food? Spend an hour alone? Visit friends? Exercise? Watch television? Work on separate hobbies in the same room?

How much conversation feels comfortable? How much quiet? How much notice do you need before plans change?

There are no universally correct answers.

But there are answers that fit together more easily than others.

Time, routines, and energy

Suppose Elena, 29, enjoys social gatherings and regularly invites friends over. Her girlfriend, Nia, 31, likes those friends but needs quiet after several hours of conversation.

They could arrange to host gatherings less often, agree on an ending time, or make it comfortable for Nia to leave the gathering and recharge.

Those arrangements work only if Elena does not resent every limit and Nia does not feel required to endure overload to demonstrate affection.

Neither has to become a different person.

The same applies to sleep schedules, cleanliness, spending habits, food preferences, and how people organize their homes.

Someone who wants a carefully maintained home may be compatible with someone who is less naturally organized if they can agree on a fair arrangement and both follow through.

But if one partner expects the other to manage every chore, reminder, appointment, and consequence, the disagreement is no longer just about personality.

It concerns responsibility and whether the arrangement works for both people.

There is limited direct research establishing how much similarity in sleep, household routines, or social energy predicts relationship success for new couples. Treat these as practical questions to explore rather than a scientifically validated compatibility checklist.

When keeping track of life is difficult

If you forget appointments, lose track of time, or struggle to move between activities, dating someone who prefers a tightly organized routine may require some planning.

That does not automatically mean you are incompatible.

You might agree to use shared calendars, send reminders, or divide tasks according to what each person can manage reliably.

The important distinction is between explaining a difficulty and refusing responsibility for its effects.

A small Turkish study compared 28 married couples in which one partner had an ADHD diagnosis with 28 comparison couples. The ADHD group reported more marital conflict and lower marital adjustment. Because the study was small, clinic-based, and cross-sectional, it cannot establish that ADHD caused these problems or predict the outcome of any individual relationship (Kahveci Öncü, 2022).

For either partner, the useful question is not “Does this person have ADHD?”

It is “Can both people recognize what is difficult, discuss its effects, and agree on arrangements they actually use?”

If something repeatedly fails, an agreement may need to change. Neither person should have to pretend the problem does not exist.

If your daily life has very little slack in it

If recovery time, quiet and predictability are what keep you functioning, they aren’t preferences to trade away for the right person. They’re the conditions under which there is a you to date.

That makes some mismatches structural rather than solvable. Someone who needs spontaneity and constant company isn’t wrong, and neither are you, but goodwill won’t close that gap.

Work this out early, while it’s an interesting difference rather than an accumulated resentment. “Here’s what my ordinary week has to contain” is a fair thing to say on a third date.

How do you need to communicate?

Two people can care deeply about each other and still misunderstand one another.

One person communicates through hints and changes in tone. Another relies on explicit words.

One prefers to think before answering. Another likes resolving questions immediately.

One considers a quiet evening together a sign of closeness. Another expects conversation throughout the evening.

None of these differences automatically determines compatibility.

What matters is whether both people can communicate in a way the other can understand and accept.

The sculpture-and-glasses picture from earlier in the book applies here. You can experience the same moment differently without either perspective being dishonest.

For example, Sam says:

“I had a really good time. I’d like to see you again.”

Riley answers:

“That sounds nice.”

Sam is uncertain whether Riley means yes.

Rather than treating that response as a definite agreement or rejection, Sam can ask:

“Would you like to make plans for another date, or would you rather leave it there?”

Riley is free to answer either way.

Direct questions are useful because they allow people to clarify meaning. They are not a tool for obtaining an answer someone does not want to give.

A qualitative study of autistic and nonautistic adults found that participants in both groups described sharing, similarity, and respect as important to intimacy. Autistic participants also described the importance of clarity and difficulties arising from uncertainty. The study involved only 57 adults and cannot establish that everyone in either group communicates in a particular way (Sala et al., 2020).

A systematic review of research on romantic relationships involving autistic people also identified communication and partner support among factors associated with relationship experiences. But it found a small and varied evidence base, so its findings should not be treated as a universal formula (Yew et al., 2021).

Watch how you handle small misunderstandings

You do not need to wait for a major disagreement to learn something about communication compatibility.

Notice what happens when one person misunderstands a joke, needs to change a plan, or asks for clarification.

Can both people say what they meant?

Does either person mock the other’s need for explanation?

Can someone acknowledge a mistake without making the other person responsible for comforting them?

Can a request be declined without punishment?

Imagine Morgan saying:

“When plans change at the last minute, I get stressed. Could you tell me as early as possible when something changes?”

Casey replies:

“Yes. Sometimes things change unexpectedly, but I can tell you as soon as I know.”

That conversation does not guarantee future success. It does show two people trying to make their expectations understandable.

Now imagine Casey repeatedly changing plans without explanation, then ridiculing Morgan for asking about it.

That is useful information too.

The issue is not that one person likes spontaneity and the other likes planning. The issue is how the difference is treated.

A relationship in which you can explain a need without being mocked offers something very different from one where you must constantly guess whether speaking honestly will cause trouble.

Your sensory needs and theirs both matter

Imagine enjoying someone’s company at a restaurant while struggling to hear them over the music.

The lights are bright. People keep brushing past your chair. You want to focus on the conversation, but the environment is demanding more attention than you can comfortably give.

Your date notices that you seem distracted.

They might conclude that you are not interested.

You might conclude that you are bad at dating.

Neither conclusion needs to be true.

A quieter place might make the same conversation much easier.

If noise, light, crowds, scents, or unexpected touch affect your comfort, those experiences deserve consideration when choosing activities and discussing physical closeness.

This matters whether you identify as autistic, have another neurotype, or simply know what environments suit you.

Affection does not feel the same to everyone

Some people enjoy frequent hugs, hand-holding, cuddling, and other physical affection.

Others enjoy affection only in particular circumstances. A person might love holding hands but dislike surprise hugs. Someone may enjoy cuddling for ten minutes and then need physical space.

In qualitative research with autistic and nonautistic adults, participants described different experiences and meanings of physical intimacy. Some autistic participants reported sensory discomfort with particular forms of touch, while others valued touch and closeness (Sala et al., 2024).

The study involved a small, self-selected sample. It also analyzed the same participants as the earlier intimacy study (Sala et al., 2020), so the two papers do not represent independent confirmation.

A separate exploratory study interviewing eight young autistic adults also described the relevance of predictability, pacing, physical environments, and permission to pause during affection (Kanellopoulou et al., 2026).

These findings do not establish a single autistic preference for touch.

The practical principle applies to everyone: affection should be mutually wanted, not endured as proof of love.

For example:

“I like cuddling, but sometimes I get uncomfortable after a while. Can I tell you when I need space?”

“Yes. I enjoy cuddling too, and I want us both to be comfortable.”

Or:

“I enjoy holding hands, but I’m not comfortable with kissing yet.”

“Thanks for telling me. We can take our time.”

No one owes physical affection to preserve a relationship. And a person who needs more affection than the other comfortably wants may eventually decide that the relationship does not meet their needs.

Both positions can be legitimate.

The solution is not to pressure someone into touch or to conceal discomfort.

It is to discover whether both people can enjoy the kinds of closeness available to them.

Does your brain need to work like your partner’s?

If you struggle with unspoken expectations, it can be tempting to believe that dating someone whose brain works like yours would solve everything.

There may be advantages to sharing experiences.

An autistic person might find another autistic person’s need for advance planning immediately understandable. Someone who struggles with attention or organization might appreciate a partner who knows what those difficulties feel like.

But sharing a neurotype does not guarantee shared needs, values, communication styles, or habits.

Two autistic people can have very different sensory preferences. Two people with ADHD can have conflicting routines. Two people with similar childhood experiences may want very different kinds of closeness.

And two people with different neurotypes can learn to communicate well together.

What the research actually tells us

One German survey found higher reported relationship satisfaction among autistic participants who described their partners as autistic than among those who described their partners as nonautistic. However, this was a cross-sectional study using participants’ reports of their partners’ status. It cannot show that sharing a neurotype caused greater satisfaction (Strunz et al., 2017).

A small US laboratory study examined conversations between autistic and nonautistic strangers. Autistic adults disclosed more information when paired with autistic partners and showed a trend toward preferring future interaction with autistic partners (Morrison et al., 2020).

Those conversations lasted five minutes. The participants were strangers, not necessarily potential romantic partners.

A larger preregistered study published in 2025 examined how information passed through groups of autistic people, nonautistic people, and mixed groups. Unlike an earlier smaller experiment, it did not find a statistically significant difference in information transfer between the three group types. Some differences in reported rapport remained (Crompton et al., 2025).

This study examined a laboratory communication task, not romantic relationships.

Taken together, these findings do not justify declaring any neurotype pairing inherently better.

They suggest a more useful question:

Can these two particular people understand, respect, and accommodate each other’s ways of experiencing the world?

A neurotype label may help explain experiences. It cannot make the decision for you.

If both of you are autistic

You may recognize experiences in one another that other partners have found confusing.

But there can still be conflicts.

Suppose one partner feels comfortable with constant background music, while the other needs silence to concentrate.

Both experiences matter.

Or one partner needs a detailed plan before going out, while the other finds making decisions exhausting and prefers someone else to choose.

The solution is not to assume that being autistic makes one person’s needs automatically understandable or compatible with the other’s.

You still need to discuss what each person wants, what is difficult, and what arrangements both can live with.

If your partner’s brain works differently

You might find a behavior confusing without it being intended to hurt you.

Perhaps your partner needs extra time to answer an emotionally important question. Perhaps you find long silences uncomfortable.

You can explain both experiences without deciding that either person is defective.

“I need time to think about important questions. I can usually give you a better answer if we come back to it later.”

“I can give you time. It would help me to know when we plan to talk again.”

That is cooperation, provided both people follow through.

However, a difference in processing style does not excuse repeated disregard for agreements or another person’s boundaries.

Understanding why something happens can make it easier to address. It does not mean its effects must be accepted without limit.

If you're dating someone whose routines aren't negotiable

A routine that looks rigid from outside is often doing real work: it’s what makes the rest of the week possible. Treating it as something to loosen up usually costs more than it gains.

Ask what a given routine protects before suggesting changes to it. Some are genuinely flexible, some are load-bearing, and the difference isn’t visible from the outside.

Plenty of room usually remains once you know which is which. The question is where the flexibility actually is, not whether the person is willing to be flexible.

What matters more than a label

It is easy to create an imaginary picture of what another person will be like based on a diagnosis, identity, background, or past relationship.

Someone might assume an autistic person will always be honest, an ADHD person will always be spontaneous, or a person with a difficult childhood will necessarily struggle with commitment.

Those assumptions skip the actual person.

A small Dutch study comparing autistic and nonautistic adolescent boys found that both groups valued qualities such as trustworthiness in prospective friends and romantic partners, with no group differences in desired characteristics detected. The findings were limited to 76 teenage boys and cannot establish what every autistic adult wants (Dekker et al., 2023).

Interviews with 16 autistic adults who identified as gender- and/or sexually diverse also illustrated how individual preferences and explicit mutual understanding shaped experiences of romantic relationships. Their experiences should not be turned into assumptions about all LGBTQ+ or autistic people (Ciric et al., 2026).

No diagnosis or identity tells you whether someone wants children, enjoys physical affection, prefers monogamy, needs daily contact, or would make a compatible partner for you.

Ask.

Listen.

Observe whether their actions match what they tell you.

You are dating a person, not a category.

The same applies to someone who has experienced trauma. A person can describe wanting predictability, patience, or emotional safety without being required to disclose a personal history. You do not need to know the origin of every preference to respect it.

Compatibility includes how you treat each other

Two people can agree about children, money, living arrangements, and religion while still making each other miserable.

They might share every hobby but constantly dismiss one another.

They might make an excellent-looking couple in photographs while privately feeling unable to discuss their needs.

That is why compatibility cannot be reduced to a list of matching answers.

A major analysis combined 43 longitudinal datasets involving 11,196 couples. Among the strongest correlates of reported relationship quality were people’s perceptions of their partner’s commitment, appreciation, satisfaction, and the amount of conflict in the relationship. The analysis was much less successful at predicting how satisfaction would change over time (Joel et al., 2020).

In other words, the way people experience their relationship matters enormously, while predicting its future remains difficult.

This does not mean you can accurately judge an entire relationship from one date.

It means that as you get to know someone, you should pay attention to more than their stated characteristics.

Do they show interest in what matters to you?

Do you feel able to say what you think?

When they promise something reasonable, do they usually follow through?

Can both people disagree without treating disagreement as an attack?

Do they accept that you may want different things?

Does spending time together generally add something worthwhile to both lives?

And just as importantly, are you offering those things in return?

Compatibility is mutual. It is not a test in which you examine another person’s behavior while treating your own as irrelevant.

Respect is not negotiable

A difference in preferences can be discussed.

A difference in how much time you want together can be negotiated.

A difference in the amount of physical affection you want may or may not have a workable answer.

But consent and basic respect are not rewards that someone earns by being a sufficiently good partner.

If someone says no to physical contact, sex, continuing to date, or further communication, respect that answer.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly pushing for a different answer, stop. A person’s uncertainty or refusal is not permission to keep pressing.

If you are the person receiving pressure, you do not have to prove that your reasons are good enough.

Someone who respects your decisions may feel disappointed. Disappointment is not the same as permission to ignore your choices.

Do not classify repeated boundary violations as a harmless difference in communication style.

A practical compatibility check

You do not need a forty-question questionnaire or a complete account of someone’s life before going on a second date.

You do need to become curious about more than attraction.

Try this exercise before you start dating someone seriously, or while you are getting to know them.

Step 1: Name your own needs

Write down your answers to these questions:

  1. What kind of relationship do you actually want? Consider commitment, exclusivity or another agreed structure, and the amount of independence you want.
  2. What future choices matter most? Think about children, location, work, faith, and important responsibilities.
  3. What helps an ordinary week feel good? Consider social time, quiet time, routines, and shared activities.
  4. How do you prefer to communicate? What helps when you need clarity, time to think, or a change of plans?
  5. What would make a relationship unacceptable for you? Separate genuine dealbreakers from preferences that would merely be nice to have.

You can write simple answers.

“Someone who wants children.”

“Someone comfortable with a quiet home.”

“Someone who can talk directly about problems.”

“Someone who respects my need for time alone.”

These are more useful than a vague wish for someone who is perfect, attractive, kind, funny, successful, and always available.

They describe a life you might actually share.

Step 2: Sort the answers

Make three small groups:

Must agree: What would make the relationship impossible or unacceptable if you wanted different things?

Can negotiate: Where could different needs fit together through a fair arrangement?

Nice to share: What would make dating more enjoyable without determining whether a relationship works?

Be selective about what you put under “must agree,” but do not remove an important need merely because it limits your dating options.

For example, you might prefer someone who lives within twenty minutes of your home. If you could happily date someone an hour away, that is a preference.

But if your responsibilities make traveling more than twenty minutes impossible, distance may be a genuine practical limit.

The same difference can belong to different categories for different people.

Step 3: Find out through conversation

You do not need to deliver all your questions at once.

In fact, asking them all during the first half hour may make an ordinary date feel more like a job interview.

Let the conversation develop, share your own answers, and make room for the other person to decide what they want to discuss.

Useful questions include:

  • “What does a good ordinary Tuesday look like for you?”
  • “Do you like making plans in advance, or do you prefer deciding as you go?”
  • “How much time do you usually like spending with a partner?”
  • “Do you see yourself wanting children?”
  • “What do you do when you’ve had a really tiring day?”
  • “When something bothers you, do you prefer discussing it immediately or having time to think?”
  • “Are there things you need in a relationship that you haven’t always found easy to explain?”

You can also volunteer information rather than making every topic a question.

“I really enjoy spending time with someone, but I also need evenings to myself.”

“I prefer clear plans. If something changes, telling me directly helps.”

“I want children someday, so that’s important to me when I’m considering a serious relationship.”

Someone does not have to answer every personal question immediately. Respecting privacy and taking time to know each other are compatible with eventually discussing important expectations.

If you need an answer before becoming more committed, you can say so without demanding an immediate disclosure.

Step 4: Compare words with experience

After a few dates, consider what you have actually learned.

A person may describe themselves as respectful but repeatedly dismiss your preferences.

Someone may say they are unreliable with messages yet consistently make and keep clear plans in a way that suits you.

Pay attention to the whole pattern.

One canceled date does not automatically mean someone is unreliable. One awkward silence does not mean you have nothing in common.

But repeated behavior is information.

And check your own behavior too. If you say you value honesty but avoid admitting a major difference in life goals because you fear losing the relationship, you are making it harder for both people to make an informed choice.

Three reasonable answers

After learning about a potential partner, you do not have to reach one dramatic conclusion.

There are three ordinary possibilities.

Yes: there is enough here to explore

You like each other. Your important goals appear compatible. Your differences seem manageable, and you both want to continue.

You do not need certainty about the future.

You can keep dating and learning.

Maybe: you need more information

You enjoy each other’s company but do not yet know how an important difference would work.

Perhaps you have different social needs. Perhaps one person wants to relocate someday but has not decided where. Perhaps you are still learning how each other communicates.

Ask respectfully, observe what happens, and allow time for a clearer answer.

Uncertainty is a reason to learn more, not to presume agreement.

No: an important need cannot be met

You may genuinely like someone and still decide not to continue.

Suppose a 45-year-old who enjoys a peaceful, independent life meets someone who wants to combine households quickly and spend nearly every free hour together.

Both might be kind, attractive, and interesting.

Neither is required to abandon the life they want.

A clear ending could sound like:

“I’ve enjoyed getting to know you, but I don’t think we’re looking for the same kind of relationship. I don’t want to continue dating.”

You do not need to argue your case. The other person does not have to agree that your reasons are sufficient.

And if someone gives you that answer, accept it.

Being incompatible with one person does not make either person unworthy of affection.

It means the relationship you could build together is not one that both people want.

Finding the fit that works

Compatibility is not the absence of differences.

It is the combination of shared essential goals, differences that can be handled fairly, mutual interest, and treatment that allows both people to remain themselves.

You do not need someone who matches every part of you. You need someone whose choices, needs, and behavior can fit with yours, and who wants that fit too.

You can learn some of this by thinking about what matters to you. The rest becomes visible through conversations, everyday experiences, and how both people respond when things do not go exactly as expected.

No checklist can promise you a lasting relationship.

But knowing your needs, listening to another person’s, and taking both seriously can help you recognize a relationship worth exploring, or leave one that is not right for you.
