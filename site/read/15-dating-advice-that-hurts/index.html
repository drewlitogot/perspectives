Chapter 15: Dating advice that hurts

Daniel is 34 and has never been in a serious relationship. He’s tried dating apps, asked people out, and watched friends find partners. He’s not sure what he’s doing wrong.

When he asks for advice, people tell him to be confident, be himself, or stop trying so hard.

None of that tells him what to do differently.

Then he finds a video. The speaker seems to have answers to everything. They explain why people get rejected, what potential partners supposedly want, and which mistakes Daniel has been making. There are rules, categories, and confident predictions about how people behave.

He watches another video. Then another.

Soon he has an explanation for every disappointing experience. Someone didn’t reply because of a rule he learned. Someone declined a date because of another. Even people he’s never met seem predictable.

For the first time, he feels as though he understands dating.

But there’s a problem: Daniel has learned a great deal about what strangers claim other people think. He hasn’t learned much about getting to know an actual person.

Some dating advice helps you communicate, recognize compatibility, and make choices that respect both people. Other advice teaches you to distrust, rank, or control the people you’re hoping to connect with.

Advice that makes you feel more confident isn’t necessarily advice that helps you build a good relationship.

Why bad advice can feel helpful

Dating is full of uncertainty.

You can take care of your appearance, practice conversation, treat someone kindly, and express interest respectfully. The other person can still say no.

You might enjoy a date and discover that the other person doesn’t want another. Sometimes you won’t know why. Sometimes nobody has done anything wrong.

If you’ve never dated, that can be particularly frustrating. You’re trying to learn a skill without knowing how to tell whether you’ve improved.

Perhaps you grew up somewhere that relationships weren’t discussed openly. Maybe romantic signals are difficult for you to interpret. Or perhaps you’ve been rejected enough times that another rejection feels like confirmation of something terrible about yourself.

Wanting clear instructions is reasonable.

A confident speaker offering definite explanations may feel like exactly what you’ve been missing.

A 2024 study interviewed 21 former members of online incel communities. Incel originally meant “involuntarily celibate,” although some communities using that label developed hostile beliefs about women and relationships.

Participants described loneliness, shame about romantic inexperience, fear of judgment, and searching for explanations. The communities could offer belonging and apparent certainty while reinforcing beliefs that made constructive change more difficult (Maryn et al., 2024).

This was a small study of self-selected former members, not a description of everyone who struggles with dating.

But it illustrates an important distinction: a harmful explanation can meet a genuine need.

Daniel doesn’t want to spend his life angry. He wants affection, companionship, and a chance to experience something that seems ordinary for everyone around him.

The videos give him an explanation for why he hasn’t found those things.

Instead of considering several possibilities, including compatibility, limited opportunities to meet people, or skills he might develop, he can blame an entire category of people.

That explanation might provide temporary relief. It also gives him fewer reasons to change his approach.

When he eventually meets someone who might enjoy his company, he approaches them expecting dishonesty or rejection. He looks for evidence that the videos were correct instead of learning who the person is.

He has gained certainty without necessarily gaining understanding.

Online recommendations can also lead people toward material they didn’t deliberately seek. A study of historical YouTube recommendations found that simulated browsing paths could connect unrelated content to incel-related videos. It did not establish what actual viewers experienced, whether watching the content changed their beliefs, or how the platform operates in 2026 (Papadamou et al., 2021).

You don’t have to understand recommendation systems to notice the problem.

Hearing the same explanation repeatedly doesn’t make it true.

And finding people who share your frustration doesn’t mean their explanation is correct.

When dating becomes a competition

Harmful dating advice comes in several forms. Pickup-artist content, “high-value” videos, and bitter online communities aren’t identical.

Some sell courses. Some focus on status. Others offer belonging to people who feel rejected or mistreated.

The warning sign isn’t a particular label. It’s the way the advice treats other people.

Does it encourage you to understand potential partners as individuals? Or does it turn them into opponents, prizes, or categories?

Pickup-artist advice: the promise of getting a result

There’s nothing wrong with wanting to improve your social skills.

You can learn to begin conversations, express interest, dress in ways that suit you, and manage nervousness. Those are legitimate areas for personal growth.

The problem begins when another person’s freedom to choose is treated as an obstacle.

Consider the difference between learning how to invite someone on a date and learning how to make someone agree to one.

The first concerns something you can control: how you communicate.

The second assumes you should be able to control someone else’s answer.

Suppose you ask someone out and they decline.

If your goal was to obtain a date, you might interpret that as failure.

But if your goal was to express interest and discover whether it was mutual, you’ve accomplished something important.

You made your interest clear. The other person understood and made a choice. You respected their answer.

You learned that the interest wasn’t mutual.

That’s disappointing, but it isn’t evidence that you did something wrong.

Dating isn’t an exam where the correct performance earns you another person.

The purpose of an invitation isn’t to produce a yes. It’s to give someone a real choice.

Someone who doesn’t want to date you isn’t a puzzle you failed to solve.

They are a person making a decision about their own life.

“High-value” advice: ranking human beings

Another kind of advice divides people into categories such as “high-value” and “low-value.”

Different creators use these phrases differently. Some discuss ordinary self-improvement. Others claim that people can be ranked according to appearance, income, age, career, social status, or conventional ideas about gender.

Sometimes this advice includes reasonable observations.

Your appearance affects first impressions. Financial circumstances can affect what kind of life you can build. Personal habits and communication skills matter.

But those facts don’t mean a person has a single, fixed romantic value.

Researchers Bachaud and Johns examined roughly 9,000 pages of material from male-oriented online communities. They found that legitimate research and uncertain evolutionary psychology hypotheses were sometimes selectively presented as permanent rules about how women and men behave (Bachaud & Johns, 2023).

Scientific language can make an argument sound more convincing than its evidence permits.

There’s a difference between discovering an average pattern in a population and predicting what a particular stranger will want.

Even a genuine average difference doesn’t mean every person in the group behaves the same way. And a disputed hypothesis shouldn’t become a rule for judging someone’s character.

Consider research on relationship quality.

A large analysis combined information from 43 longitudinal studies involving 11,196 couples. People’s perceptions of their actual relationships, including appreciation, conflict, and perceived partner commitment, were stronger predictors of current relationship quality than broad individual characteristics. Predicting how satisfaction would change over time was considerably harder (Joel et al., 2020).

This research primarily concerned existing couples. It doesn’t predict whether a particular stranger will agree to a date, and it doesn’t establish that income, attraction, or personal characteristics are irrelevant.

But it provides little reason to trust a simple ranking as a forecast of how two people will experience a relationship.

You can meet someone attractive, successful, and socially confident who is completely incompatible with you.

You can also meet someone who doesn’t fit your imagined ideal and discover that you genuinely enjoy each other’s company.

As earlier chapters explained, attraction matters, and compatibility matters. Neither requires assigning people a fixed level of human worth.

You can improve your circumstances without believing affection is reserved for people who achieve a particular status.

You can have preferences without treating people who don’t meet them as inferior.

Your worth as a person and your compatibility with a particular partner are different things.

Bitter communities: blaming whole groups

Some online communities begin with legitimate disappointment.

People discuss repeated rejection, harassment, discrimination, unfair expectations, or mistreatment in relationships.

Those experiences deserve attention. Finding people who understand can be comforting.

The problem begins when individual experiences become supposed proof of what everyone in a particular gender is like.

A large study of male-oriented online communities found distinct but overlapping groups, with some newer communities showing higher measured misogyny and toxicity (Ribeiro et al., 2021).

A separate 2025 study examined women-oriented communities, including Female Dating Strategy and TruFemcels. It documented pessimistic and sometimes transactional discussions of heterosexual dating while identifying important differences between the communities. The researcher emphasized the context of women discussing genuine mistreatment and cautioned against treating these communities as simply equivalent to male-oriented ones (Francis, 2025).

Neither study describes what men or women generally believe. Nor do the findings establish equal prevalence or equivalent kinds of harm across the communities.

The useful distinction is between recognizing harmful behavior and assigning motives to strangers based on identity.

Imagine Priya, 43, who’s returning to dating after an unhappy marriage.

She finds an online group where people discuss relationships in which their needs were ignored. Some members offer helpful advice about recognizing disrespect and protecting personal boundaries.

Other discussions insist that all potential male partners are selfish or dishonest.

Priya can appreciate the first kind of discussion without accepting the second.

She doesn’t have to dismiss her past experiences. She doesn’t need to ignore warning signs or trust someone she feels uncomfortable around.

She can remain cautious while paying attention to how each person actually behaves.

A difficult experience can teach you what you won’t accept again. It cannot establish what every stranger intends.

Perspective: Men

Some male-oriented dating content treats romantic attention, sexual experience, income, or dominance as proof of masculine worth. If you’ve been rejected repeatedly or have little dating experience, those messages may deepen an existing sense of shame.

The study of former incel-community members illustrates how some men sought belonging and explanations for their difficulties (Maryn et al., 2024). It doesn’t mean lonely men inevitably adopt hostile beliefs.

You don’t need romantic or sexual experience to establish your worth. You can learn dating skills, improve your circumstances, and take responsibility for your choices without accepting that rejection makes you inferior or that anyone owes you a relationship.

Perspective: Women

If you’ve experienced harassment, unwanted attention, or controlling behavior, advice about screening potential partners may be especially important.

Protecting yourself isn’t manipulation. You can decline invitations, take your time, insist on respectful treatment, and leave interactions that feel unsafe.

Be cautious when advice shifts from recognizing harmful behavior to assuming everyone in a particular gender has the same motives. You don’t have to give up your standards or your safety to reject that assumption.

Perspective: Non-binary

Much adversarial dating advice assumes everyone belongs to one of two genders and that relationships are competitions between fixed gender roles.

If you’re nonbinary, genderqueer, or otherwise outside that model, you may find that its supposedly universal rules don’t describe your life at all.

You don’t have to force yourself into someone else’s categories to learn about dating. Look for guidance that respects your identity, orientation, and preferences while treating everyone involved as capable of making their own choices.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from “How perspectives work.”

People stand in different places and bring different experiences to dating. Those experiences matter.

But nobody’s position provides complete knowledge of everyone else.

The answer isn’t to declare one perspective the truth about an entire gender. It’s to describe what you see and find out what the other person sees.

But what if dating games actually work?

It’s tempting to dismiss manipulative dating advice by saying that it never works.

The research doesn’t support such a simple conclusion.

A 2023 review covering 18 studies found mixed, context-dependent results concerning playing hard to get. Some forms of perceived selectivity or uncertainty appeared to increase immediate romantic desire under particular circumstances. In other situations, uncertainty or difficulty could have negative effects (Houle et al., 2023).

Three short-encounter experiments also found that perceiving a potential opposite-sex partner as selective or difficult to attract could increase immediate desire (Birnbaum et al., 2020).

Those findings matter because they prevent an overly simple argument.

But the experiments don’t establish that deception, pressure, or deliberately creating distress helps build a healthy relationship.

They concern immediate attraction and interest in meeting, not lasting trust, compatibility, consent, or long-term satisfaction.

Separately, a 2026 meta-analysis, described in an author-posted summary, found a modest negative association between interpersonal manipulation and relationship quality across 16 studies. The evidence was primarily correlational, so it doesn’t prove that manipulation caused the poorer relationship outcomes (Turner, 2026).

These findings aren’t contradictory. They concern different behaviors and outcomes.

Someone might feel immediate attraction without that interaction becoming a healthy relationship.

There’s also a difference between being genuinely selective and deliberately trying to control another person’s reaction.

Consider Alicia, 39, who likes another woman she’s recently met.

Alicia works irregular hours and helps care for her father. She’s not always available to meet, and she sometimes needs quiet evenings.

That doesn’t mean she’s playing a game.

She’s living according to her needs.

If the other woman wants more frequent contact, she can explain that preference. Alicia can describe what she can comfortably offer.

They may discover they’re compatible, or they may not.

Neither needs to pretend to be uninterested or manufacture uncertainty.

You can have standards, maintain independence, and take your time while remaining honest about your intentions.

Having boundaries is about making choices for yourself. Manipulation is about trying to control someone else’s choices.

A boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept. A request asks something of another person, who can say no.

Neither requires treating someone as an opponent.

Advice that makes ordinary behavior look suspicious

Another problem with adversarial dating advice is that it can turn everyday interactions into supposed evidence of hidden motives.

Someone takes several hours to answer a message, and a video claims to know what that means.

Someone offers to split the bill, and an online community assigns an intention to the offer.

Someone needs an evening alone, and a dating creator insists it reveals something about their romantic interest.

But the same behavior can have different explanations.

Consider Malik, 28, and Theo, 30.

Malik is neurotypical and generally answers messages quickly. He enjoys frequent conversation when he’s interested in someone.

Theo is autistic and sometimes needs time to organize his thoughts. After a demanding workday, he may have little energy left for messaging, even with someone he likes.

Malik encounters advice claiming that delayed replies reveal a lack of romantic interest.

He begins interpreting Theo’s communication through that rule.

Instead, Malik could ask:

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. I sometimes feel unsure when our messages are far apart. What kind of texting pace works for you?”

Theo might answer:

“I like talking with you too. Some evenings I need quiet time before I can reply. It doesn’t mean I’m uninterested.”

Now they have useful information.

Malik doesn’t have to pretend that frequent communication doesn’t matter to him. Theo doesn’t have to promise immediate replies.

They can decide whether their preferences fit together.

A 2026 qualitative survey of 335 adults with ADHD or self-identified ADHD documented experiences including rejection sensitivity, relational shame, and attention-related misunderstandings. The Irish sample was self-selected and focused largely on negative experiences; it didn’t examine whether people with ADHD are more likely to adopt harmful dating advice (O’Brien et al., 2026).

The practical lesson doesn’t require a diagnosis.

If uncertainty about someone’s intentions is painful, a confident explanation may feel reassuring.

But certainty isn’t the same as accuracy.

Sometimes you can ask for clarification. Sometimes you need to wait. Sometimes a difference in communication preferences means a relationship isn’t right for you.

You can make that decision without inventing a hidden motive.

If you want clear rules instead of guessing

If social situations are difficult to interpret, someone offering exact dating rules may seem particularly helpful. Wanting explicit instructions is reasonable.

A Canadian study comparing 95 autistic and 117 non-autistic adults found differences in measured sexual knowledge and sources of information, supporting the importance of accessible, explicit education. It did not establish that autistic people are more likely to believe manipulative dating advice (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

Prefer rules about your own actions: ask clearly, respect a refusal, explain your preferences, and never assume consent. Be cautious of rules that claim to reveal another person’s private thoughts based on one behavior. You can learn reliable ways to behave without pretending other people are completely predictable.

The same principle applies when you’re dating someone whose brain works differently from yours.

A slower reply, unusual expression, direct question, or need for quiet time doesn’t automatically reveal someone’s intentions.

Take the person’s explanation seriously. Describe your own needs. Find out whether those needs are compatible.

Neither partner should have to hide natural traits through masking to satisfy a dating creator’s idea of how interest is supposed to look.

When advice becomes a safety problem

Some dating advice is merely inaccurate.

Other advice encourages behavior that can harm people.

A questionable recommendation about clothing might waste your money.

Advice that encourages you to disregard another person’s discomfort, refusal, or consent creates a much more serious problem.

RAINN explains that sexual consent must be freely given, specific, and ongoing. Silence, freezing, pressure, and manipulation aren’t substitutes for voluntary agreement. Consent to one activity doesn’t automatically mean consent to another (RAINN, 2026).

These principles matter regardless of what a dating instructor claims is normal or expected.

If someone doesn’t want to date you, their answer isn’t a challenge.

If someone agrees to a date, that doesn’t mean they’ve agreed to physical contact or sexual activity.

If someone changes their mind, their earlier agreement doesn’t cancel their present decision.

When interest or consent is unclear, ask or wait. Don’t treat ambiguity as permission.

The same respect applies when someone ends contact.

If you’ve been told not to contact someone, or they’ve blocked you, stop. Their decision isn’t an invitation to demonstrate persistence.

And if you’re experiencing pressure, you don’t owe anyone repeated explanations until they accept your answer.

You can leave an interaction, contact someone you trust, and seek appropriate support.

The CDC identifies multiple factors associated with intimate-partner violence, including social attitudes that support aggression, along with protective factors such as supportive relationships. This doesn’t mean watching hostile content predicts that someone will commit violence (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

It does provide reason to take messages that normalize aggression or disrespect seriously.

If you recognize yourself doing something harmful

Perhaps you’ve followed advice that encouraged pressure.

Perhaps you’ve continued contacting someone after they tried to end an interaction. Or you’ve treated another person’s discomfort as something they should have to justify.

Recognizing the problem matters, but stopping matters more.

Stop the behavior. Respect the person’s answer. Do not contact someone who has blocked you or told you to stop.

Don’t turn an apology, explanation, or wish for forgiveness into another demand on them.

Take responsibility without blaming the person who rejected you, the advice you followed, or difficulties you have interpreting social situations.

If you’re struggling to stop pressuring, monitoring, or pursuing someone, seek professional support focused on accountability and changing harmful behavior.

Someone you’ve harmed doesn’t owe you access, reassurance, or another opportunity.

You can still make different choices in the future.

Later chapters explain boundaries, consent, harassment, stalking, and recognizing abuse in greater detail.

Protecting yourself without judging everyone

If you’ve experienced mistreatment, caution is reasonable.

You don’t have to trust someone simply because they seem friendly. You don’t have to continue dating someone who makes you uncomfortable.

And you don’t owe a stranger unlimited opportunities to prove their intentions.

But there’s a difference between a rule about what you’ll accept and a rule about what an entire group supposedly intends.

Consider Morgan, 46, returning to dating after an unhappy marriage.

Morgan has decided not to continue seeing anyone who repeatedly dismisses requests for personal space.

That’s a boundary.

A dating video might instead insist that everyone in a particular category is incapable of respecting personal space.

The first approach helps Morgan recognize behavior that matters.

The second makes assumptions about strangers before they’ve done anything.

You can pay attention to how someone treats you, maintain your boundaries, and decide whether you want to continue.

You don’t have to establish someone’s hidden motives before leaving a relationship that doesn’t feel right.

And you don’t have to treat everyone as dangerous to take your safety seriously.

When someone is selling you certainty

Dating insecurity can also become a sales opportunity.

Not every paid course, coach, or relationship book is dishonest. Teaching useful skills takes time, and charging for education isn’t automatically exploitative.

But paying money doesn’t make an explanation true.

Be cautious when someone presents your loneliness as proof that you need a secret system only they can provide.

Ask what you’re actually being offered.

Is it a practical skill, such as expressing interest clearly or recognizing incompatibility?

Or is it a promise that someone else’s attraction, choices, or commitment can be controlled?

Does the educator acknowledge uncertainty and individual differences?

Can they explain the limits of their evidence?

Can you disagree without being shamed or told you’re destined to remain alone?

A responsible educator should be able to acknowledge that even respectful, socially skilled people experience rejection.

Nobody can guarantee mutual attraction.

Financial exploitation involving supposed romantic connections is a separate concern. The Federal Trade Commission warns about romance scams and provides information about protecting yourself and reporting suspected fraud (Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

An expensive dating course isn’t automatically fraudulent, and a disappointing date isn’t necessarily a scam.

Chapter 47 covers online dating scams in detail.

For now, remember that promises of romantic success deserve scrutiny, particularly when someone is charging money for certainty.

Five questions to ask before following dating advice

You don’t need to become a relationship researcher to evaluate every video or book.

These five questions are practical judgment tools, not a scientifically validated test. They can help you decide whether advice is worth your attention.

1. Does it respect both people’s choices?

Useful advice helps you express interest and respond respectfully to another person’s answer.

Harmful advice treats that answer as something you should be able to control.

If someone’s freedom to refuse is presented as a problem, reject the advice.

2. Does it describe individuals or make claims about entire groups?

Notice the difference between recognizing someone’s particular behavior and assuming everyone with a certain identity behaves the same way.

A person’s gender, orientation, or neurotype doesn’t tell you everything about their character or intentions.

3. Does it distinguish attraction from relationship quality?

Getting attention, receiving a date invitation, and building a satisfying relationship are different outcomes.

Ask whether the advice helps you create the kind of connection you actually want, not merely whether it promises an immediate result.

4. Does it allow for uncertainty?

Different people want different things.

Useful advice should acknowledge context, exceptions, and the limits of its evidence.

Be cautious when someone claims to interpret every behavior or predict every person’s choices.

5. Does it teach something you can control?

You can practice communicating, asking questions, presenting yourself honestly, recognizing boundaries, and responding respectfully to disappointment.

You cannot control another person’s attraction or willingness to enter a relationship.

Good advice helps you take responsibility for your choices without pretending you’re responsible for everyone else’s.

Try replacing one bad rule

Think of a dating rule you’ve encountered that makes a definite claim about another person’s intentions.

For example:

“If someone is genuinely interested, they’ll always reply immediately.”

Ask what that rule assumes.

It assumes everyone has similar schedules, energy levels, communication preferences, and opportunities to use their phone.

It leaves no room for someone who needs time to process a message, has demanding responsibilities, or simply communicates differently.

Now rewrite the rule as something you can actually use:

“If regular communication matters to me, I can explain that preference and ask what works for the other person. Then I can decide whether our expectations are compatible.”

The original rule claims to know another person’s intentions.

The revised rule gives you a respectful way to learn something important.

Try the same exercise with another claim.

Suppose someone tells you that your income or appearance determines whether you’re worthy of commitment.

You might replace that with:

“I can improve the parts of my life I want to change while looking for someone whose goals, preferences, and way of treating people fit with mine.”

Neither rewritten rule guarantees a relationship.

But both give you something useful to do without pretending you can read minds or rank human worth.

Where to find better help

The alternative to harmful dating advice isn’t having no guidance.

It’s learning skills that help you participate in dating honestly, safely, and thoughtfully.

Structured relationship education has some research support, although the results are modest.

A meta-analysis of 32 controlled US evaluations of publicly funded couple-relationship education programs found small average improvements in relationship skills and relationship quality, but no statistically significant average improvement in relationship stability. The programs mainly served established couples, often with lower incomes, rather than adults beginning to date (Hawkins et al., 2022).

Those findings don’t establish that any particular dating course works.

They do provide reason to consider practical relationship education instead of trusting promises of guaranteed romantic success.

Look for resources focused on communication, consent, recognizing incompatibility, and managing disagreements respectfully.

Some useful starting points are RAINN for consent and sexual safety, the CDC for information about relationship violence and prevention, and the FTC for consumer protection and romance-scam guidance (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; Federal Trade Commission, 2025; RAINN, 2026).

These organizations don’t teach every aspect of dating. They provide useful foundations in areas where misleading advice can cause harm.

A qualified counselor or therapist may also help if shame, past experiences, or repeated relationship difficulties are making dating hard.

If attention differences, sensory needs, or direct communication are relevant to you, look for someone who understands those experiences and respects your preferences.

A credential doesn’t guarantee a good fit, and therapy isn’t required before you can date. Cost, accessibility, and personal comfort matter.

Friends can provide useful feedback too.

Choose people who respect you and the people you’re interested in.

A helpful friend might say:

“It sounds like you really liked that person, but they didn’t want another date. That’s disappointing. It doesn’t mean either of you did something wrong.”

Or they might tell you something more difficult:

“You’ve been assuming people are rejecting you for the same reason, but have you actually listened to what they want?”

Neither answer promises an easy solution.

Both invite you to think instead of blame.

Change what you spend your attention on

If a dating-related account regularly leaves you angry, ashamed, or convinced that entire groups are against you, consider unfollowing it.

You don’t have to prove every claim is false before deciding the material isn’t helping.

Replace it with sources that teach practical skills and acknowledge individual differences.

You can also take a break from dating advice altogether.

Reading about relationships can be useful. But another fifty explanations won’t replace ordinary conversations, respectful invitations, and getting to know people.

Sometimes the next useful step isn’t finding a better theory.

It’s talking with an actual person.

You don’t need to outsmart someone to date them

Imagine two people who meet through a local hobby group.

Quinn is 38 and autistic. Casey is 41 and also autistic. Both want companionship, but neither has much recent dating experience.

Quinn prefers planning ahead. Casey enjoys spending time with people but finds long social outings tiring.

They’ve talked several times and seem to enjoy each other’s company.

Quinn decides to express interest.

“I’ve really liked talking with you. Would you want to go on a date sometime?”

Casey answers:

“Yes. Could we go somewhere quiet? Busy restaurants can be a lot for me.”

“Sure. Would the little café near the library work?”

“That sounds good.”

Neither person needed a ranking system or a rule about what their gender supposedly wants.

They didn’t need to pretend to be uninterested or manufacture uncertainty.

They expressed a preference, listened to an answer, and made a plan that suited both.

That doesn’t guarantee a relationship. They might discover they’re incompatible. They might prefer friendship. They might grow closer.

But they’re learning about each other instead of trying to win.

That’s what useful dating advice should help you do.

It should leave you more capable of recognizing your needs, understanding someone else’s, and deciding whether the connection works for both of you.

It shouldn’t require you to surrender your judgment to someone claiming to understand every stranger before meeting them.

The next time someone offers a rule that supposedly explains everyone you might date, ask yourself:

Does this help me understand another person, or does it teach me to stop seeing them as an individual?

If it’s the second, you can leave that advice behind.

You don’t need a secret system to become worthy of a relationship. You need opportunities to meet people, skills for getting to know them, and the willingness to accept that not every connection will be mutual.

And when someone captures your attention, remember that feeling intensely interested doesn’t tell you whether the relationship would be right for either of you.

That’s where the next chapter begins.
