How to use this book

Before you start · about 6 minutes

You don’t have to read this book from beginning to end. You don’t have to complete every exercise, memorize what to say, or agree with every example. You can open it to the page you need, take something useful, and put it down.

This book is for adults learning to date, whether you’re starting for the first time or trying to understand why dating hasn’t gone the way you hoped. It’s also for anyone who wants to understand a partner whose mind, communication style, or experiences differ from their own.

You don’t need a diagnosis, a relationship history, or any particular level of confidence to begin.

Start wherever you need to

The book has nine parts, arranged to build on one another:

  1. Know yourself: Understand your needs, feelings, energy, and what you want from a relationship.
  2. Dating basics nobody taught you: Learn what dating is for, how to recognize compatibility, and how to set realistic expectations.
  3. Communication: Practice expressing yourself, understanding others, and repairing misunderstandings.
  4. Boundaries and consent: Learn how to recognize, communicate, and respect limits, including your own.
  5. Signals and interest: Understand attraction, flirting, friendship, and rejection without guessing more than you need to.
  6. Meeting people: Find opportunities to connect and learn how to approach dating apps and new acquaintances safely.
  7. Dates: Prepare for dates, navigate them, and decide what happens afterward.
  8. Relationships: Explore closeness, everyday life together, conflict, intimacy, and breakups.
  9. Support: Find additional help when a book isn’t enough.

Reading in order can help if everything feels unfamiliar. But these parts aren’t levels you must pass before moving forward.

If you’re going on your first date tomorrow, start with Chapter 49, Planning a date that works for you, and Chapter 50, On the date. If you’re unsure whether a friend is interested in you, try Chapter 41, Friend or more? When you’re not sure. If conversations keep going wrong, Chapter 24, Repairing misunderstandings, might be more useful today than starting at page one.

Use the table of contents to find a topic. In the website edition, you can also search for what you’re looking for.

You may find yourself returning to an earlier chapter after you’ve had a new experience. That’s fine. Something that seemed abstract before a date may make much more sense afterward.

The examples aren’t instructions to memorize

Throughout the book, you’ll find short examples of conversations and possible things to say. These are sometimes called scripts.

A script gives you a starting point when finding words is difficult. It isn’t a magic sentence that guarantees a particular response.

For example, if you want to ask someone on a date, the book might offer a simple, direct way to express your interest. You can change the words, shorten them, use your usual vocabulary, or say something entirely different.

What matters is the meaning: being clear, being respectful, and leaving the other person free to choose.

You don’t need to sound like the examples. In fact, if a sentence feels unnatural coming from you, change it.

You’ll also encounter questions, checklists, and exercises. Some invite you to notice a pattern in your own behavior. Others help you prepare for a conversation or decide what matters to you.

All of these activities are optional. You can think about a question without writing anything down. You can skip an exercise and still use the advice around it.

This is a reference book, not an exam.

Some advice comes in boxes

Most of the book speaks to everyone. Sometimes, though, an experience depends on how other people treat you, how you process information, or what you’ve lived through.

You’ll see three kinds of boxes:

  • Perspective boxes discuss experiences that may differ for men, women, and non-binary people. They describe relevant patterns, not rules about how everyone in a group behaves.
  • Experience boxes offer adjustments for situations such as losing track of time, finding noise exhausting, needing longer to process a feeling, or reacting strongly to reminders of past experiences. Some relate to autism, ADHD, or trauma, but you don’t need any of those labels to find the advice useful.
  • “If you’re dating someone who…” boxes address the other person in a relationship. They explain how to understand a different experience and what respectful support might look like.

Read the boxes that seem relevant. Skip the ones that don’t. Or read all of them to better understand people who experience the world differently.

The next section, How perspectives work, explains the idea behind these different viewpoints.

No single box is intended to describe every person in a group. You get to decide which advice fits your circumstances.

You can skip difficult material

Dating involves pleasant experiences, but it can also involve rejection, uncomfortable situations, and serious harm. This book discusses those subjects directly because understanding them matters.

Three chapters begin with specific content notes:

  • Chapter 32, Persistence, harassment and stalking: Discusses unwanted contact, harassment, stalking, and personal safety.
  • Chapter 35, Sexual assault and rape: Discusses sexual violence, unwanted sexual contact, and options for getting help afterward. It does not describe assaults graphically.
  • Chapter 36, Recognizing manipulation and abuse: Discusses emotional abuse, controlling relationships, sexual violence, and situations where a partner may feel unsafe.

A content note tells you what a chapter discusses before you decide whether to read it.

You can skip a chapter, read only the practical help sections, or return when you feel ready. You can also read difficult material with someone you trust or with professional support.

Other chapters address subjects such as sexual health, scams, rejection, and breakups. You can choose when and whether to explore those topics, too.

You don’t need to finish a difficult chapter to deserve support.

If you need help now, Chapter 59, Therapy and support, explains ways to find it. Chapter 60, Crisis resources, provides US support options. In the United States, you can call or text 988 for suicide or mental health crisis support. If you’re in immediate danger, call 911.

What this book won’t do

This book won’t teach you to manipulate someone into liking you, pressure someone into a relationship, or treat dating as a contest to win.

It won’t diagnose you or your partner, and it won’t tell you what people of any gender supposedly all want.

It will sometimes ask you to reconsider your expectations, habits, or choices. The purpose is to help you recognize what you can change, while respecting what other people are free to choose for themselves.

Choose the reading format that works for you

You can read the book as a PDF, download an EPUB for an ebook reader, or use the free website:

https://drewlitogot.github.io/perspectives/

The website includes search and a reading-settings panel. You can adjust text size, choose a font, and switch between light and dark appearances.

You can also show or hide the different kinds of boxes, depending on what you want to focus on. Some chapters include optional interactive activities.

If you prefer reading without an internet connection, the downloadable PDF or EPUB may suit you better. If changing the appearance of the text helps you concentrate, the website offers more flexibility.

There’s no preferred format. Use whichever makes reading easiest for you.

Take what you need, then try it

You don’t have to become an expert in relationships before you can have one. Reading about dating and actually dating are different experiences. The book can help you prepare, but it can’t make another person’s decisions for them.

Sometimes the useful next step is small: asking a clearer question, recognizing that you’re uncomfortable, or deciding what you want before agreeing to another date.

Sometimes the useful step is simply learning something you didn’t know.

You can read ten chapters in a weekend or one paragraph when you need it. You can revisit the same explanation several times. You can disagree with an example while keeping the underlying principle.

The point isn’t to finish the book.

It’s to understand yourself and other people a little better, and use that understanding when it matters.
