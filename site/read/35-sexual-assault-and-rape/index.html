Content note: This chapter discusses sexual assault, rape, unwanted sexual contact, and what someone can do afterward. It does not describe assaults graphically. If you’ve experienced sexual violence, you can skip any section or return when you feel ready. You don’t need to finish reading to deserve help.

If you need support now, the National Sexual Assault Hotline is available 24 hours a day at 800-656-HOPE (4673). You can also text HOPE to 64673 or use online chat through RAINN. The service can help you understand your options and find local support. If you’re in immediate danger, call 911 or move somewhere safer if you can. (RAINN, 2026b)

When sex happens without consent

Someone can agree to go on a date, visit an apartment, kiss, or have sex one evening and still say no to sex later. A relationship doesn’t change that. Neither does marriage.

Sexual assault happens when someone engages in sexual contact without the other person’s consent. Rape is a term used for certain forms of nonconsensual sexual penetration. The precise legal definitions differ across the United States.

These words can feel severe, especially if you’re trying to understand something involving a person you cared about. You might wonder whether an experience counts if nobody was physically injured, nobody threatened you with a weapon, or you didn’t say no aloud.

The important starting point is simpler than the legal terminology: sexual activity requires freely given consent. A person who doesn’t want sexual contact doesn’t have to fight, explain, or prove that their refusal is reasonable.

The previous chapter covered how consent works before and during sex. This chapter addresses what it means when consent is absent, what help is available, and what responsibility looks like when someone crosses that line.

What the words mean

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) uses several categories to measure sexual violence in the United States. These are descriptions of experiences used in a national survey, not legal charges. (Leemis et al., 2025)

  • Rape: In the CDC survey, completed or attempted unwanted sexual penetration involving physical force or threats, or penetration when someone is unable to consent because they are too intoxicated, drugged, or unconscious.
  • Being made to penetrate: Being forced or otherwise made to penetrate another person without consent. The CDC measures this separately from rape and asked men about this experience in its 2023/2024 survey.
  • Sexual coercion: In the CDC survey, unwanted sexual penetration following nonphysical pressure, such as repeated demands, threats involving a relationship, or misuse of authority.
  • Unwanted sexual contact: Sexual touching without penetration that the person did not consent to.

Sexual assault is a broader everyday term for nonconsensual sexual activity. Its exact meaning as a criminal offense depends on the jurisdiction.

These categories can overlap. Someone might experience more than one kind of sexual violence, and an experience may not fit neatly into a single label.

What about US law?

There is no single definition that determines every sexual assault case in every state.

In 2013, the FBI adopted a broader definition of rape for national crime-reporting statistics. It includes victims and perpetrators of any gender, recognizes situations involving incapacity, and does not require physical resistance.

But that FBI definition was designed for collecting crime statistics. It did not rewrite state criminal laws. (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2012)

States differ in how they define particular offenses, consent, incapacity, and relevant legal procedures. Some conduct may be charged under a different name from the CDC survey category that describes it. (RAINN, 2026c)

If you need to understand the law where something happened, RAINN’s State Law Database is a starting point. A qualified local attorney or victim advocate can help explain how the law applies to a particular situation.

You do not need to decide whether an experience meets a criminal statute before asking for medical care or emotional support.

It can happen to anyone, including with someone you love

Many people picture sexual assault as an attack by a stranger.

That picture leaves out a large part of what survivors actually report.

In the CDC’s 2016/2017 survey, among women who had experienced rape, 56.1% reported at least one acquaintance as a perpetrator and 39.3% reported at least one intimate partner. Among men who had experienced rape, 57.3% reported an acquaintance and 12.5% an intimate partner. These categories overlap because someone may have experienced assaults by more than one person. (Basile et al., 2022)

That means sexual violence can involve a date, a friend, a spouse, a former partner, or somebody who previously treated you kindly.

Imagine that Morgan, 38, has been dating Elise for six months. They’ve had consensual sex before. One night Morgan doesn’t want to. Elise continues pressuring Morgan, and sexual activity happens without freely given agreement.

Their relationship doesn’t make Morgan’s consent automatic. Their previous sexual experiences don’t make it automatic either.

Or imagine Daniel, 24, who is assaulted by someone he has been dating. He later wonders whether he can call it assault because his body responded physically during the experience.

An involuntary physical response isn’t permission. What matters is whether the sexual activity was freely agreed to.

No one owes sexual access to another person. Not because they accepted dinner, promised to stay over, started something sexual, or have been together for twenty years.

How common is it?

In the CDC’s 2023/2024 national survey, approximately 21% of women and 3.2% of men reported experiencing completed or attempted rape during their lifetimes. Another 3.8% of men reported having been made to penetrate someone.

When the CDC included its broader categories of contact sexual violence, the lifetime figures were 45.1% of women and 16.9% of men. These categories overlap and cannot be added together. (Leemis et al., 2025)

The survey had 15,609 respondents but a low response rate, excluded people experiencing homelessness and people living in institutions, and reported estimates for women and men rather than separately for every gender identity. Changes in survey methods also mean its figures should not be directly compared with earlier CDC surveys to claim that sexual violence has increased or decreased.

An older analysis using 2010–2012 US data found higher reported rates of rape among bisexual women and gay and bisexual men than among heterosexual people of the same surveyed gender. Its sexual-minority samples were smaller, and it included too few transgender respondents to produce reliable transgender estimates. It also did not provide separate non-binary estimates. (Chen et al., 2020)

These statistics describe groups, not individuals. They do not tell you whether a particular person has experienced violence, or whether someone is likely to harm another person.

Perspective: Men

Some men find it difficult to recognize their experience as sexual assault, particularly when they were made to penetrate someone else. The CDC counts being made to penetrate separately, and men have reported experiences of it involving both acquaintances and partners. (Basile et al., 2022; Leemis et al., 2025)

Being physically capable of sexual activity is not the same as consenting to it. Neither is being stronger, older, or previously sexually experienced. If you were assaulted, you deserve support regardless of the other person’s gender. 1in6 offers online, counselor-facilitated peer-support groups for adult men. These groups are not crisis services or therapy. (1in6 (Zero Abuse Project), 2026)

Perspective: Women

The 2016/2017 CDC survey found that intimate partners were identified as perpetrators by 58.3% of women who had experienced the survey’s category of sexual coercion. This was a measure among survivors, not a percentage of all relationships. (Basile et al., 2022)

If pressure came from someone you love, you might be sorting through affection, loyalty, fear, anger, and confusion at the same time. You don’t have to decide whether to end the relationship, report what happened, or forgive the person before seeking help. Caring about someone does not cancel your right to refuse them.

Perspective: Non-binary

Being transgender or non-binary does not make sexual assault any less serious. A service provider should not need to make assumptions about your gender, body, or relationships to understand that you need care.

You can ask how a service handles names, pronouns, privacy, and examinations before deciding what to share. FORGE provides resources for transgender and non-binary survivors, although it is not a crisis hotline. The national RAINN hotline is another place to ask about local services. (FORGE, 2026; RAINN, 2026b)

Freezing is not consent

Imagine something frightening happens during sexual contact.

You want it to stop. You may even know exactly what you want to say.

But your body doesn’t move. Your voice disappears. You might stare at one spot, go completely still, or feel unable to respond.

Afterward, you ask yourself why you didn’t push the person away or shout.

One possible explanation is tonic immobility, an involuntary defensive response to extreme fear in which a person becomes unable to move or speak.

In a study of 298 women attending a Stockholm emergency clinic for rape victims, 70% reported significant tonic immobility during the assault. The study also found an association between this response and greater psychological distress later.

That percentage describes this particular group of women seeking clinical care in Sweden. It is not an estimate for all survivors or all genders. (Möller et al., 2017)

Some people freeze. Others resist, escape, speak, cry, try to calm the person down, or respond in ways they don’t understand until later.

None of those reactions determines whether they consented.

Not fighting back is not the same as agreeing.

This distinction matters whether you’re trying to understand something that happened to you or considering how your own behavior affects somebody else.

If a partner goes still, stops responding, or seems unable to communicate during sexual activity, stop. Do not interpret their stillness as permission.

And if you were the person who froze, an involuntary response is not a failure to protect yourself.

If you’re only realizing what happened now

Sometimes the meaning of an experience changes after you’ve had time to think.

Consider Jules, 31, who remembers an encounter from several years earlier. Jules initially described it as an awkward sexual experience. More recently, after learning about consent, Jules has started wondering whether it was assault.

Jules isn’t required to settle on a label immediately.

A meta-analysis of 28 studies found that many women whose experiences met researchers’ definitions of rape did not themselves label those experiences as rape. The estimated percentage varied greatly across studies, so it cannot tell us how any particular survivor will understand an event. (Wilson & Miller, 2016)

Recognizing an experience later doesn’t automatically tell you every detail about what happened. But delayed recognition is not, by itself, a reason to dismiss your concerns.

You might have thought that being in a relationship meant you had to agree. You might have assumed that accepting an invitation meant accepting sexual contact. You might have needed time to understand what consent actually required.

Or you might remember what happened clearly but still feel uncertain about the word for it.

You can describe an experience in your own words. An advocate or therapist can listen without requiring you to call it rape or assault before you’re ready.

If your discomfort arrives hours or days later

Some people can explain their feelings immediately. Others need time to notice what their bodies or emotions are telling them.

A study involving autistic and non-autistic US college students found that students in both groups described difficulty identifying their own sexual desire. Autistic participants also reported dissatisfaction with consent education and, in some accounts, realizing only later that they had experienced sexual assault. This was a student study, not a finding about every autistic person. (McKenney et al., 2024)

If you understand what happened only after you've had time to process it

You may have gone along with an interaction while concentrating on what the other person wanted, particularly if you were masking or trying to follow social expectations. You may have experienced overload or shutdown, making speech difficult. You might not have understood your own reaction until much later.

Try writing down what you remember in ordinary language, without forcing yourself to decide on a label. You can use questions such as: “What did I want?” “What did I communicate?” and “Did I feel free to stop?” This is a personal reflection tool, not a legal test or a requirement to recover a perfect memory.

A small Australian study of 14 practitioners supporting autistic women survivors highlighted the importance of safety, explanations, and help understanding experiences. It reflected practitioners’ views, not measured treatment outcomes. (Fox et al., 2025)

It was not your responsibility to prevent someone else’s assault

People sometimes blame themselves for sexual violence.

They question their decision to go somewhere, trust somebody, drink alcohol, or continue dating the person.

But choosing to spend time with someone is not consenting to every possible sexual activity.

Clothing isn’t consent. Flirting isn’t consent. A previous yes isn’t a permanent yes.

If you were assaulted, the responsibility belongs to the person who acted without your consent.

This remains true if you froze, didn’t recognize the situation immediately, or had difficulty communicating.

It also remains true if you trusted the person afterward, continued seeing them, or didn’t report what happened.

You can look back at an experience and decide that you want different boundaries in the future. That is your choice. It doesn’t mean the assault was your fault.

What to do afterward

There is no single required response to sexual assault.

You might need emergency medical attention. You might want to speak with someone privately. You might want a forensic examination. You might want to report the assault to police, or you might not.

You can make these decisions separately, and you do not need to make all of them on the same day.

1. Get somewhere safer, if you can

If the person who harmed you is still nearby or you’re worried about further harm, consider moving to a place where you feel safer.

That could be a trusted person’s home, a staffed public location, or somewhere else the person cannot easily reach you.

If you’re in immediate danger or have a serious injury, emergency services may be needed.

You do not have to confront the person, explain why you’re leaving, or collect proof before getting help.

If leaving immediately would put you in greater danger, an advocate can help you think through safer options.

2. Consider medical care

Even if you have no visible injuries, a medical professional can discuss care after sexual assault, including injuries, sexually transmitted infections, pregnancy concerns, and emotional wellbeing.

Some preventive treatments and medications are time-sensitive. Contacting a healthcare professional promptly gives you more options. A clinician can explain what is appropriate for your circumstances. (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2024)

You can seek ordinary medical care without agreeing to a forensic examination.

You can also bring a support person, ask for an advocate, or request an explanation of what will happen before making decisions about an examination.

If you need a moment to process a question, you can say:

“Please explain what you’re asking before I decide.”

Or:

“I need a few minutes before I answer.”

3. Ask about a sexual assault forensic examination

A sexual assault forensic examination (SAFE) is a medical examination that can include injury assessment and collection of evidence after sexual assault.

It may be performed by a specially trained nurse, often called a Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner (SANE), or another qualified professional.

The examiner should explain what the examination involves and ask for permission. You can decline individual parts, ask for a pause, or stop the examination. (RAINN, 2026a; U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2024)

You do not have to be certain about reporting to police before asking about an examination.

Under federal funding conditions, states must ensure that eligible medical forensic examination costs are covered without requiring the patient to participate in the criminal justice process. Other medical treatment, medications, and follow-up care may have separate costs. Ask the provider or an advocate what is covered. (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2024)

What if you’ve already showered or several days have passed?

You can still ask about an examination.

The 2024 US Department of Justice protocol recommends collecting evidence as soon as possible, often within five days or longer depending on the circumstances. RAINN notes that DNA collection is often emphasized within 72 hours, but other evidence may remain available after that.

Neither time frame should be treated as an absolute deadline for getting care. (RAINN, 2026a; U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2024)

If you’ve showered, changed clothes, eaten, used the bathroom, or waited, tell the examiner if you feel comfortable doing so.

Those actions do not make care pointless, and they do not make you responsible for what happened.

If you have clothing, messages, or other items you may want to preserve, an advocate or examiner can explain your options. You do not have to turn recovery into an evidence-collection project.

An examination cannot guarantee a particular legal outcome. Deciding not to have one also doesn’t invalidate your experience.

4. Decide whether you want to report

A police report and a medical examination are separate decisions.

In many locations, survivors can receive forensic examinations without making an immediate police report. Some jurisdictions offer anonymous or non-reporting evidence collection, which may preserve options for later. The rules for storage, identification, confidentiality, and reporting vary. (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2024)

Reporting is a personal decision. It can involve practical, emotional, and legal questions.

In the Bureau of Justice Statistics’ 2024 survey, most rape and sexual assault victimizations were not reported to police. The measured reporting percentage changed substantially from 2023, so a single year’s estimate shouldn’t be treated as a fixed rule about survivor behavior. (Tapp & Coen, 2025)

Some people report immediately. Others wait. Some decide not to report.

An advocate can explain local procedures without deciding for you. They can also help you ask about confidentiality, possible mandatory-reporting obligations, and what may happen if you do make a report.

If you are considering legal action, a local attorney or victim advocate can explain available options and time limits.

5. Find someone who can support you

You might want a friend, sibling, partner, counselor, or advocate to know what happened.

You don’t have to share every detail.

You could say:

“Something sexual happened that I didn’t agree to. I don’t want to explain everything right now, but I could use some support.”

Or:

“I’m not sure what to call what happened. Can you listen without trying to decide for me?”

If speaking is difficult, sending a message or showing someone something you’ve written is another option.

The National Sexual Assault Hotline, 800-656-HOPE (4673), is available 24/7. You can also text HOPE to 64673 or visit rainn.org for online support and referrals. (RAINN, 2026b)

You do not need to wait until you feel certain, calm, or ready to report to contact an advocate.

The book’s later chapters on therapy and crisis resources provide more help with finding ongoing support.

If someone tells you they were assaulted

Perhaps someone you’re dating tells you that a previous partner assaulted them. Or a close friend says something happened last weekend and they’re frightened.

You might feel angry, protective, confused, or desperate to fix the situation.

Your response matters.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 51 studies on disclosure of interpersonal violence found that negative reactions were associated with worse mental-health outcomes. These studies were largely correlational, so they cannot establish that a particular response caused someone’s later distress. But they give good reason to take blaming and controlling reactions seriously. (Dworkin et al., 2019)

Start by listening.

You could say:

“Thank you for telling me.”

“I’m sorry that happened.”

“Would you like me to listen, help you look at options, or just sit with you?”

Avoid turning the conversation into an investigation. You don’t need to ask why someone went to a particular place, what they were wearing, why they didn’t leave, or why they haven’t reported.

You also don’t need to decide whether their experience meets a criminal definition.

If you believe they might be in immediate danger, tell them what concerns you and offer practical help. Unless emergency intervention is necessary, avoid taking control of decisions they can make themselves.

Ask before sharing their experience with others. If confidentiality has limits, such as in some professional settings, explain those limits rather than promising secrecy you cannot keep.

If you’re their partner now

A person who has experienced sexual assault may want closeness, distance, reassurance, ordinary conversation, or none of those things at a particular moment.

Don’t assume that physical affection is automatically comforting.

Ask:

“Would you like a hug, or would you prefer some space?”

If they decline physical contact, accept that answer without treating it as a rejection of you.

You can offer to accompany them to an appointment, help them find information, or simply spend time together doing something unrelated.

You are not required to become their therapist, and they are not required to make you their main support person.

The later chapter on trauma in relationships covers how past experiences can affect intimacy and everyday connection without turning either partner into a caretaker.

Getting help when communication or sensory needs are different

Medical examinations, police interviews, and support calls can involve unfamiliar environments, many questions, and decisions under pressure.

If bright lights, noise, touch, or rapid questioning make communication difficult, you can ask for adjustments.

The US Department of Justice forensic examination protocol recognizes the need for accommodations for patients with disabilities, including communication and sensory needs. (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2024)

Possible requests include:

  • A quieter space or adjusted lighting where available.
  • Written information or one question at a time.
  • Extra time to answer without interruption.
  • An explanation before someone touches you.
  • A break, an advocate, or a support person when permitted.

You can ask for these adjustments without disclosing a diagnosis.

If you’re autistic, have ADHD, have experienced trauma, or simply process information differently under stress, the important question is what helps you communicate and make your own decisions.

You might also need different support at different stages. Someone who speaks easily during an ordinary conversation might experience shutdown during an examination. Another person might communicate comfortably with a nurse but find a telephone call difficult.

These needs should not be treated as evidence that you’re unreliable or incapable of making decisions.

The sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of this book applies here too. People may have very different ways of understanding and describing an event. The response should be to ask what someone means and what they need, rather than assume that an unfamiliar communication style makes their experience less credible.

If you think you may have sexually harmed someone

This section is for the reader who recognizes something troubling in their own behavior.

Maybe you continued sexual activity when the other person seemed uncertain. Maybe you pressured someone after they said they didn’t want sex. Maybe someone has told you that an encounter was not consensual, and you remember it differently.

You may feel frightened, ashamed, defensive, or confused about what happened.

Those feelings do not determine what the other person experienced.

If you think you crossed someone’s sexual boundary, stop the behavior.

Do not continue sexual contact while hoping the uncertainty will resolve itself. Do not seek another sexual encounter to prove that everything is fine.

If the person has asked you not to contact them, respect that. Do not contact them through friends, other accounts, or another method. Respect any protective order and other legal requirements.

Do not pressure them for reassurance, forgiveness, an explanation, or a conversation about whose memory is correct.

An apology isn’t a substitute for giving someone the distance they’ve requested. Nor does feeling misunderstood entitle you to access to them.

Intent does not erase responsibility

People sometimes genuinely misunderstand social signals.

That is one reason this book teaches direct communication, consent, and boundaries so explicitly.

But an inability to interpret someone’s expression does not grant permission to continue sexual activity without clear, freely given consent.

Autism, ADHD, trauma, inexperience, intoxication, or uncertainty about what someone meant cannot make consent unnecessary.

There is also an important distinction between not knowing how to express romantic interest and refusing to respect another person’s limits.

If you’re uncertain whether the other person wants sexual activity, the safe response is to stop and clarify. If they cannot or do not freely agree, the activity does not continue.

Take action beyond feeling sorry

You can take responsibility without forcing the other person to participate in your process.

Speak privately with a qualified mental-health professional who has experience addressing harmful sexual behavior. Be specific and accurate about what you did, rather than describing only your intentions or feelings.

Ask for help understanding your actions, taking responsibility, and preventing further harm.

If you are concerned about criminal or civil legal consequences, seek advice from a qualified attorney. A dating handbook cannot determine whether a particular event meets a legal definition.

You may not get forgiveness. You may not get another opportunity to explain yourself. You are still responsible for changing your behavior.

There is no need to make the person you harmed responsible for teaching you how to respect consent.

The research available for this chapter did not establish a validated US adult-dating-specific program for people concerned that they have sexually harmed another adult. The recommendation to seek qualified professional help is a safety and accountability step, not a claim that a particular intervention has been proven effective.

There is no required way to respond

If you experienced sexual assault, you might feel frightened or furious. You might feel distant from what happened, question your own interpretation, or have periods when you don’t think about it at all.

You do not need to respond in a particular way to establish that your consent mattered.

You also don’t have to make every decision now.

A small first step could be saving the National Sexual Assault Hotline number, speaking with a trusted person, asking a clinician about an examination, or writing down what you would like to understand.

If someone has trusted you with their experience, your first step could be listening without trying to take charge.

And if you recognize that you crossed another person’s sexual boundary, your first step is to stop, respect their distance, and seek appropriate help.

The distinction at the center of all three situations is the same: each person has the right to decide what happens to their own body.

The next chapters look at broader patterns of manipulation and abuse, then at concrete behaviors that help you recognize when a relationship is safe or unsafe. Those patterns matter because consent is not just a question asked once. Respect must continue throughout a relationship.
