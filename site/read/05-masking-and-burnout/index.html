Chapter 5: Masking and burnout

The date went well. At least, that’s what the other person thinks.

You laughed at the jokes. You kept the conversation moving. You looked interested even when the music grew louder, and you said yes when your date suggested another hour together. They went home thinking they had met someone relaxed, energetic, and easy to please.

You went home, turned off the lights, and spent the rest of the evening trying to recover.

Now they’ve invited you out again. You like them. You’re also wondering whether you can get through another evening the same way.

Maybe your version involves forcing eye contact, smiling when you’re confused, or hiding a need for quiet.

This is masking: hiding or suppressing your natural traits to fit in. It may help you enter a social situation, but it can also hide the very information two people need to decide whether they enjoy being together.

The goal isn’t to become a person who never adjusts to anyone else. It’s to tell the difference between consideration and a performance you cannot comfortably sustain.

When fitting in takes work

Almost everyone changes their behavior according to the setting. You probably don’t speak to a job interviewer exactly as you speak to a longtime friend. You may rehearse a greeting before a date, choose your words carefully when discussing something sensitive, or avoid interrupting someone you like.

Those aren’t automatically problems. Consideration, learned skills, and courtesy can make connection easier.

Masking becomes a different question when you’re hiding something important about how you function or what you need, particularly because you’re afraid of rejection.

Compare two situations.

In the first, you want to tell someone you disagree with them. You pause and choose words that are clear without being insulting. The meaning stays the same. You’re being thoughtful.

In the second, you disagree but say you agree, because you believe any difference of opinion will make the other person dislike you. You do this repeatedly, until your date has a picture of your interests and values that doesn’t match your own.

Or perhaps you can manage eye contact for a few seconds, but keeping it up throughout a conversation makes listening harder. You do it anyway, because you’ve been told that looking away is rude. Your date sees attention; you experience strain.

In a qualitative study, 92 autistic adults described hiding or changing their behavior to fit expectations, avoid negative treatment, and connect with other people. Some described practical advantages. Others described exhaustion and a sense that people did not really know them (Hull et al., 2017).

The reasons for masking are often understandable. Maybe you were teased for talking about a favorite subject. Maybe people repeatedly misread your facial expressions. Perhaps asking for clarification once led someone to call you difficult. You may have learned that appearing comfortable is safer than explaining discomfort.

Those lessons may have protected you elsewhere. Dating gives you a chance to examine whether they still help.

When the date likes your performance

Ana, 38, meets Malik, 43, for coffee. They’re both interested. They talk for two hours, and Malik suggests a live-music venue for their next meeting.

Ana finds loud spaces difficult. She struggles to follow speech when music is playing, and after a busy evening she needs quiet. But she enjoyed meeting Malik, who seems genuinely excited about the idea. She says yes.

At the venue, Ana concentrates on looking comfortable. She smiles when she misses parts of the conversation. She stays after she wants to leave. Malik sees someone who likes the same activities he does.

At home, Ana feels frustrated. She has spent another evening managing her reactions rather than enjoying herself. Malik has no idea.

Neither has behaved maliciously. But they are now making plans with different information.

The next time Malik suggests music, Ana could say:

“I like spending time with you. Loud places wear me out, though. Could we try somewhere quieter?”

She hasn’t explained a diagnosis, childhood experience, or private history. She has simply said something true.

Malik might be happy to change the plan. He might say concerts are important to him and suggest going with friends instead. Or they might discover that their preferences are difficult to reconcile. All three outcomes provide more useful information than another evening of pretending.

Being liked is not the same as being compatible. Someone may sincerely like the energetic, agreeable version of you they saw on an unusually good day. They may like your quieter, more direct, or less socially energetic side as well. But they cannot respond to needs they don’t know about.

An eight-study systematic review found that autistic participants sometimes used masking to make friends or enter social circles. They also described feeling tired, less known, or less close to people when they had to keep masking. Some reported that particular social strategies were worthwhile. The review found much less evidence about romance than friendship, so it cannot tell us how often masking helps or harms dating relationships (Ridgway et al., 2025).

The practical issue is still worth noticing: a successful introduction should not require an impossible promise to perform the same way forever.

The cost you don’t see until afterward

A first date already involves unfamiliar information: a new person, a new setting, new questions, and uncertainty about how things are going. Masking can add another layer of work.

Instead of simply listening, you may also monitor your posture, face, tone, gestures, timing, and answers. You may keep checking whether you’re doing the conversation correctly. You might ignore the discomfort in your body because you’re concentrating on the other person’s reaction.

Sometimes you notice the cost immediately. Sometimes it arrives when you get home. As Chapter 4 explained, you may need time to identify what you felt during an interaction.

That doesn’t mean a tiring date was necessarily a bad date. You can enjoy someone and still need recovery time. Nor does it mean every social skill is harmful. The more useful questions are whether the effort was voluntary, whether it worked for you, and whether you could keep doing it without hiding important needs.

Several studies link greater self-reported masking with anxiety, stress, depression, or other difficulties. A 2026 scoping review of 48 empirical studies found that these associations appeared frequently, although they varied by outcome and how masking was measured (Hodge & Meltzoff, 2026). A survey of 262 autistic people also associated frequent masking, and switching between masking and not masking across situations, with poorer mental health (Cage & Troxell-Whitman, 2019).

But an association is not proof of a cause.

A preregistered study followed 332 autistic adults in the Netherlands over roughly two years. It did not find strong evidence that people who masked more went on to experience worsening mental health. Higher initial masking was followed by a small reduction in reported difficulties, which the authors cautioned might reflect statistical effects rather than a benefit of masking (Putten et al., 2025).

These studies do not settle why the relationship differs between people and settings.

You don’t have to turn your personal experience into a scientific verdict. If an activity consistently leaves you depleted, that is information about your needs, whatever the broader research eventually finds.

Different people, different masks

Masking isn’t exclusive to autism. You can hide your natural reactions because of repeated criticism, social pressure, fear, or experiences you cannot easily label. The same visible behavior can have more than one explanation.

Adults with ADHD also describe concealing or adjusting aspects of themselves in social settings. A Dutch study found that adults with ADHD reported more camouflaging on some measures than a comparison group, though less overall than autistic adults. The measure was designed around autistic camouflaging, so the comparison may miss forms of masking particularly relevant to ADHD (Putten et al., 2024). A separate Norwegian study of young adults with ADHD, autism, or both found context-dependent reports of masking aspects of personality (Wurth et al., 2025).

If sitting motionless makes it harder to listen, you might conceal your need to move. If you need an extra moment to answer, you might rush your response. If you have repeatedly been criticized for enthusiasm, you might play down how much you enjoy a subject.

Someone with a history of rejection or trauma might also hide disagreement to avoid conflict. That can resemble the fawning response discussed in Chapter 2. The sources for this chapter do not establish that trauma-related fawning and autistic masking are the same process. You don’t need to diagnose yourself from a behavior to recognize that it is costing you something.

Gender is part of the picture, not a prediction

One survey compared 306 autistic and 472 non-autistic adults. Autistic women reported more camouflaging on average than autistic men, especially for some aspects of masking and trying to blend in. The same study did not find a comparable male–female difference in its non-autistic sample. Its nonbinary group was too small to support reliable conclusions (Hull et al., 2020).

Those results describe group averages in a self-selected sample. They cannot tell you how much any particular person masks.

Perspective: Women

If people often describe you as socially capable, they may not see how much work goes into that appearance. In one study, autistic women more often described masking for conventional reasons, such as managing formal settings (Cage & Troxell-Whitman, 2019). That’s a pattern in a sample, not a rule about women.

On a date, appearing fine doesn’t cancel your right to say that something isn’t working. You can name a limit before someone else notices the strain.

Perspective: Men

Men in masking studies also described hiding traits and managing others’ expectations (Hull et al., 2020). If you have learned to conceal uncertainty or discomfort, asking for a quieter setting or more time to answer may feel unusually exposed.

You do not have to prove competence by enduring an activity you dislike. A clear preference gives your date a more accurate picture of you.

Perspective: Non-binary

Research has not included enough nonbinary participants to establish dependable patterns by gender (Hull et al., 2020). Your experience doesn’t have to match findings organized around men and women to count.

You can decide separately what to share about your gender, your communication needs, and any diagnosis or personal history. Being open in one area doesn’t obligate you to disclose everything else.

How to recognize a mask that has become automatic

One difficult part of masking is that you may no longer notice the moment you begin.

A date asks, “What would you like to do?”

You answer, “Anything is fine.”

Sometimes that’s true. You genuinely don’t mind.

But sometimes you know exactly what you would prefer, and the answer disappears as soon as you imagine someone being disappointed.

Try asking yourself what happens when there is no audience to please. Do you choose quieter restaurants? Do you speak more directly? Do you move your hands while thinking? Do you enjoy a subject more openly? Do you leave events earlier?

None of those private behaviors is automatically your one “real” personality. People naturally behave differently across settings. The question is whether your public behavior gives you room to have needs, preferences, and limits.

A 2026 US study of autistic college students included 14 interviews and a separate survey of 214 participants. Students described masking as sometimes so habitual that reducing it was difficult. Some reported relief when they unmasked, along with anxiety about others’ reactions. They also reported different levels of openness across situations (Durben, 2026). This was a study of students, not a trial of dating advice.

A useful question is: Am I making this adjustment because I choose to, or because I believe I cannot safely do otherwise?

Sometimes the answer will be both. That’s important information, too.

You can share a need without sharing a label

Unmasking means allowing more of your usual behavior or reducing efforts to hide your traits. Diagnostic disclosure means telling someone about a diagnosis. They aren’t interchangeable.

You might stop forcing eye contact without telling your date you’re autistic. You might tell someone you have ADHD but still conceal how hard certain situations are. You might have no diagnosis and still know that a loud bar is a poor choice for you.

For example, you could say:

“I listen better when I’m not looking directly at someone the whole time.”

Or:

“I sometimes need a little extra time to answer.”

Or:

“I’d like to leave after dinner rather than go somewhere else.”

These statements are truthful and practical. None requires an explanation of your medical history.

In interviews with 12 UK adults diagnosed with autism in adulthood, participants described disclosure as a personal decision. Some received understanding and practical support. Others encountered doubt or dismissive reactions. They sometimes chose to tell particular people only what was relevant (Au-Yeung et al., 2025). A review of 27 studies likewise found that autistic people used concealment, selective disclosure, and advocacy in response to stigma, with mixed or limited evidence about which strategies worked best (Han et al., 2022).

There is no research-supported rule that you must disclose autism or ADHD on the first date, third date, or any particular date. An unfamiliar person hasn’t earned unlimited access to private information merely by being interested in you.

Privacy, however, isn’t the same as creating a false impression about a shared plan.

You can keep the reason private while saying you don’t want to go to a concert. You can choose not to discuss a diagnosis while explaining that you’ll need to leave at a certain time. You can decline to answer a personal question without inventing a story.

If a date responds to a small, reasonable limit with ridicule, pressure, or anger, you do not have to provide more personal details in an attempt to make them respect it. Your boundary still stands.

Choosing when to unmask

There is no perfect schedule. Treat unmasking as a series of choices rather than one dramatic announcement.

Four questions can help.

What am I hiding? Try to identify a specific behavior. Are you pretending to enjoy a place? Rushing every response? Forcing eye contact? Agreeing with opinions you don’t share? The more precise the answer, the easier it is to decide what should change.

What does hiding it cost? A brief social adjustment that helps you connect may be worthwhile. An evening spent suppressing pain, distress, or an important preference is a different matter. Consider not only what happened on the date but how you felt afterward.

What would I prefer to do or say? Choose something manageable. You might look away while thinking, request a quieter table, say “I don’t know,” or express an actual preference. You don’t need to deliver a complete account of your past to make one truthful adjustment.

How has this person responded to limits so far? Pay attention to how they handle an ordinary disagreement or request. Can they hear “not tonight” without treating it as a challenge? Do they ask questions respectfully? Can they explain their own needs without dismissing yours? A warm response isn’t a guarantee of future safety, but repeated disrespect is a reason for caution.

This is practical guidance, not a clinically tested unmasking method. In the 2026 college-student study, unmasking varied by setting and trusted relationships; its findings do not prove that unmasking on dates improves mental health or romance (Durben, 2026).

If disclosure could put your housing, employment, family relationships, or physical safety at risk, you can prioritize safety and privacy. You are not failing at authenticity by choosing carefully.

And if you try being more open and decide you’re not ready to share something else, you can stop. One conversation does not obligate you to disclose everything later.

A small experiment for the next conversation

Choose one behavior that you suspect is masking. Don’t choose the hardest or most personal one. Choose something you could safely change in an ordinary interaction.

Write four short answers:

  1. What I usually do: “I say I’m fine with any plan.”
  2. What I actually prefer: “I’d rather meet somewhere quiet.”
  3. What I could say: “Could we choose a quieter place? I find it easier to talk there.”
  4. What I’ll notice afterward: “Did the person respect the preference? Did I enjoy the conversation more? How much recovery did I need?”

You can do this with a friend before trying it on a date. If a situation doesn’t feel safe, don’t use it as an experiment.

The point isn’t to pass a test of bravery. It’s to find out whether a little more honesty about your needs makes the interaction more workable.

Notice your own reaction as carefully as the other person’s. Perhaps the date goes smoothly, yet you still feel guilty for asking. That guilt doesn’t prove you asked for too much. Perhaps your date is disappointed but accepts the change. Disappointment doesn’t automatically mean rejection. Perhaps they say no to the request because they have their own limits. Then both of you have information and can decide what to do next.

Asking doesn’t guarantee agreement. It makes honest conversation possible.

When a partner meets a different version of you

Suppose you’ve been dating for a month. Your partner knows you as cheerful, talkative, and willing to try every activity. Then you begin admitting that crowded places wear you out. You sometimes answer slowly. You don’t always want to make eye contact.

They may be surprised. They may need time to understand. They might worry that you’ve become less interested.

You can explain the difference without apologizing for having needs:

“Early on, I was working hard to seem comfortable. I’m enjoying getting to know you, and I want to be clearer about what actually works for me.”

Your partner can take that seriously without pretending every request is easy for them.

Perhaps they love live music and you don’t. You could attend a quieter event together, go to concerts separately, or decide that the difference matters too much. None of those outcomes requires either person to be wrong.

What would be unfair is demanding that someone permanently conceal discomfort to preserve the version of themselves that first attracted you.

Unmasking also doesn’t excuse hurtful conduct. Being direct doesn’t require insulting a partner. Needing quiet doesn’t grant permission to ignore another person’s stated boundary. Having a difficult experience doesn’t remove your responsibility for consent or respectful behavior.

Both people deserve room to be themselves, and both remain responsible for how they treat each other.

If the person you're dating seems quieter as trust grows

Someone who seemed socially effortless at first may have been working hard to meet unfamiliar expectations. They might later look away more, speak less, or tell you about limits they previously hid. This could be growing comfort, but it could also mean fatigue, distraction, or changing interest. You cannot know without asking.

Try a neutral question: “I’ve noticed you’re quieter lately. Is something different?” Listen to the answer rather than insisting the earlier behavior was the more accurate version. You can also explain your own needs and talk about activities that work for both of you. Supporting a partner doesn’t require becoming their therapist.

This applies when two neurodivergent people date, including two autistic people. Shared labels don’t guarantee identical preferences.

Ren likes to think aloud. Jamie needs silence before answering a question. During their first dates, Jamie rushes responses because Ren seems to expect quick conversation. Ren interprets Jamie’s short answers as lack of interest.

Eventually Jamie says, “I need a moment to think. I’m listening even when I’m quiet.”

Ren replies, “Thanks for telling me. I thought you wanted to stop talking.”

Neither person has to become the other. They needed to describe what each was experiencing instead of guessing. It’s the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book: two people can stand in the same situation and see different things.

When exhaustion becomes something larger

There is a difference between needing a quiet night after a date and finding that your usual abilities have become much harder to use for an extended period.

Researchers and autistic people use the term autistic burnout for a pattern of prolonged exhaustion, reduced ability to manage everyday tasks, and lower tolerance for demands or sensory input. In US community-based qualitative research, autistic adults connected these experiences with accumulated demands, pressure to conform, and barriers to support (Raymaker et al., 2020).

An Australian study involving 23 autistic adults with lived experience of burnout described exhaustion, withdrawal, thinking difficulties, and reduced everyday functioning (Higgins et al., 2021). A 2025 systematic review of 48 studies, encompassing approximately 4,000 participants, found recurring reports of severe exhaustion and increased difficulty functioning, with masking and unsupportive environments among the reported contributors (Ali et al., 2025).

These accounts matter, but the research is still developing. Autistic burnout is not a universally established medical diagnosis, and these studies do not give a reliable representative estimate of how many people experience it.

Consider the difference between two examples.

After one busy date, you spend the next afternoon alone and feel ready for your normal routine afterward. That may be ordinary recovery from social effort.

In another situation, you have struggled for weeks. Ordinary sounds are harder to tolerate. Everyday tasks you previously handled feel increasingly difficult. Conversation takes much more effort. An evening off doesn’t restore your usual functioning.

The second example is closer to what participants in burnout research described, but it is not enough to diagnose the cause.

Depression, physical illness, sleep problems, and other difficulties may overlap with aspects of burnout. Researchers are still working out how best to distinguish these experiences (Ali et al., 2025; Higgins et al., 2021). If your functioning has deteriorated persistently, or you’re experiencing significant mood changes, seek evaluation from an appropriately qualified healthcare professional. Don’t stop prescribed treatment based on a label suggested in a book.

Some research has also found an association between masking, unmet support needs, and suicidality in self-selected autistic adult samples. That does not establish that masking causes suicidal thoughts or that unmasking prevents them (Cassidy et al., 2018). If you’re in immediate emotional crisis in the United States, call or text 988. Chapters 59 and 60 cover crisis and support resources in more detail.

Participants in burnout studies described rest, fewer demands, understanding, and supportive surroundings as helpful (Ali et al., 2025; Raymaker et al., 2020). These are reported experiences, not scientifically established cures.

For dating, the narrower lesson is useful: if you’re struggling to function, don’t treat another exhausting date as a challenge to overcome through a better performance. You can choose a shorter outing, a quieter place, or a break from dating. Chapter 6 will look more closely at energy and the everyday tasks involved.

The person across the table needs the real information

Dating doesn’t require displaying every part of yourself immediately. Strangers haven’t earned your whole history. Privacy and caution can be sensible. So can ordinary politeness and making an effort for someone you like.

But a relationship becomes harder to understand when one person keeps saying yes to things they want to decline, or appearing comfortable when they are consistently struggling.

You may discover that your date happily accommodates a preference you were afraid to mention. You may discover a difference you can negotiate. You may discover that the two of you aren’t compatible. Each is more useful than maintaining a performance neither person realizes is happening.

You don’t have to remove every mask before dating. You can start with one honest preference, one clearer limit, or one moment when you allow yourself to answer at your own pace.

The point isn’t to make everyone like you. It’s to let both people discover what being together is actually like.
