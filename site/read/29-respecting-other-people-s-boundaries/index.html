Chapter 29: Respecting other people's boundaries

Nora is 41 and has been seeing Sam for a few weeks. They have dinner, walk around the neighborhood, and stop outside Sam’s apartment.

“Would you like to come upstairs?” Sam asks.

“Not tonight,” Nora says. “I’d rather go home.”

Sam is disappointed. The evening was lovely, and Sam had hoped to spend more time together.

But the next thing Sam says is simple.

“Okay. Good night.”

They say goodbye. Nora goes home.

Nothing dramatic happened. Nobody delivered a speech about personal freedom. Sam didn’t have to understand exactly why Nora wanted to leave.

Sam asked. Nora answered. Sam respected the answer.

That’s what respecting a boundary often looks like. It isn’t a grand gesture. It’s an ordinary moment when someone tells you what they’re comfortable with, and you let their answer guide what you do next.

The earlier chapters covered finding your boundaries and expressing them. This chapter looks at the other side: what happens when someone else’s boundary changes what you wanted, expected, or planned.

Sometimes that will be easy. Sometimes you’ll feel rejected, confused, embarrassed, or disappointed. Those feelings matter, but they don’t give you the right to cross someone else’s limit.

You can be disappointed by someone’s boundary and still respect it completely.

A no is an answer, not the beginning of an argument

Imagine three ordinary situations.

You want to call your partner after work. They tell you they’re too tired to talk tonight.

You suggest a weekend trip. The person you’re dating says they aren’t comfortable traveling together yet.

You ask someone to share a personal experience. They say they’d rather not discuss it.

In each situation, you wanted something reasonable. The other person declined something reasonable. Neither fact makes the other person wrong.

A boundary doesn’t have to mean someone dislikes you. It doesn’t have to mean they’ve stopped caring. It doesn’t even have to mean the activity itself is unpleasant.

They might enjoy talking to you but need a quiet evening. They might want a relationship but not be ready to travel together. They might trust you while keeping one part of their life private.

You might never learn the reason.

The important distinction is between understanding someone’s choice and accepting someone’s choice.

Understanding can be helpful. Acceptance is necessary.

If someone says they don’t want you to do something involving them, stop. You don’t have to agree that their reason is good enough.

A respectful answer can be remarkably short:

“Okay.”

“Thanks for telling me.”

“Understood. I won’t bring that up again.”

These aren’t magic words. You can say something else, or say nothing when silence is appropriate. What matters is what happens afterward.

If your partner says they need an evening alone, you give them the evening. If someone asks you not to share their photograph, you don’t share it. If a person tells you not to contact them, you stop contacting them.

An acknowledgment that isn’t followed by a change in behavior doesn’t respect the boundary.

You aren’t owed an explanation

Suppose someone says they don’t like being hugged.

You might wonder why. Maybe hugs are comforting to you, and the idea of refusing one seems strange.

Curiosity is understandable. But the person’s comfort isn’t a problem you need to solve.

You don’t need to investigate their past, identify a diagnosis, or decide whether their discomfort is justified.

You can simply stop offering hugs.

There may be circumstances where a practical question is useful. If someone says they prefer not to receive telephone calls, you might ask whether they would rather communicate by text.

That’s a question about how to respect their preference, not whether the preference is legitimate.

But even a practical question should wait if the person has asked to stop discussing the subject.

A boundary doesn’t become invalid because you don’t understand it.

“Not yet” means not now

Some answers leave open the possibility that things could change.

“Not yet.”

“Maybe another time.”

“I’m not ready.”

“I’m not sure.”

These phrases can be difficult to hear, particularly if you prefer clear categories. You might want to know whether the answer is yes, no, or something in between.

The safest interpretation is straightforward: the proposed activity isn’t agreed to now.

“Not yet” isn’t a promise about next week. “Maybe” isn’t permission to make plans. “I’m not sure” isn’t an invitation to decide for the other person.

Consider Mateo, 33, who has been dating Chris for two months.

Mateo suggests spending the weekend together. Chris replies, “I like you, but I’m not ready for that.”

Mateo can accept that answer without deciding what it means for the entire relationship.

“Okay. There’s no need to decide about that now.”

If Chris becomes comfortable with the idea later, Chris can bring it up.

Mateo doesn’t need to keep asking.

This doesn’t mean uncertainty always conceals rejection. Sometimes people genuinely need time to think, or their preferences change.

The point is that a possible future yes isn’t a present yes.

Perspective: Women

A refusal is not always given plainly, and often that is a safety calculation rather than politeness. In two experiments, making safety concerns salient increased stated preference for indirect endings such as ghosting, though the pilot and main study disagreed about how this varied by gender (Freedman et al., 2022). That is a finding about choices under perceived risk, not a rule about women.

If you soften your no, you are not being dishonest or unfair. It can help to know which refusals you want to give plainly, and in what settings, so the decision is yours in advance rather than made under pressure.

Why someone might not say no directly

You may have learned that people should always speak plainly when they don’t want something.

Clear communication is valuable. But people don’t always reject invitations directly.

In two experiments with single adults, researchers found that participants were less willing to reject an unsuitable potential romantic partner when they believed the choice was real rather than hypothetical. Concern about hurting the other person’s feelings partly explained the difference (Joel et al., 2014).

That doesn’t mean every hesitant answer conceals a no. It means saying no can be socially difficult, even when someone doesn’t want to proceed.

An earlier conversation-analysis paper also challenged the idea that refusal requires the literal word “no.” The authors described how people can communicate refusal through delays, qualifications, and other indirect responses. That work concerned sexual refusal and shouldn’t be treated as a universal guide to interpreting everyone’s speech (Kitzinger & Frith, 1999).

For everyday dating, the lesson is to take reluctance seriously.

Someone who changes the subject when you propose an activity might not want to do it. Someone who becomes quiet when you suggest something more intimate might not be comfortable.

You don’t need to identify the hidden meaning with certainty before acting respectfully.

Instead of proceeding, stop and leave the choice open.

For example:

“That’s fine. We don’t have to make that plan.”

Or, where clarification would genuinely help and the person seems comfortable continuing the conversation:

“Would you rather leave it for now?”

If they don’t answer, leave it.

The aim isn’t to obtain a particular answer. It’s to avoid doing something the other person hasn’t chosen.

Perspective: Men

You may occasionally be treated as a possible risk by someone who has no evidence about you either way. That is a response to a situation and a history of situations, not a verdict on your character, and reacting as though it were an accusation tends to confirm the worry.

What helps is making refusal cheap: offer the exit yourself, accept the first answer, and leave space rather than closing it. “No problem at all” and a changed subject does more than any reassurance that you’re one of the good ones.

Silence is not permission

Messages introduce another kind of uncertainty.

You send someone a message and receive no reply.

Maybe they’re working. Maybe they’ve forgotten. Maybe they feel overloaded and don’t know what to say. Maybe they no longer want contact.

You cannot reliably identify the reason from an unanswered message alone.

In experiments examining ghosting, where someone ends a connection by not responding, researchers found that people who ghosted sometimes expressed more concern for the other person’s welfare than the recipients expected (Park & Klein, 2024).

A separate US study found that heightened safety concerns increased participants’ stated preference for indirect romantic rejection. The researchers’ findings about gender-related differences were inconsistent across their studies (Freedman et al., 2022).

These findings don’t make ghosting harmless or prove that every unanswered message is an attempt to reject you.

They establish something more modest: silence can have different explanations, and you shouldn’t assume you know which one applies.

You also shouldn’t treat silence as agreement.

If someone hasn’t replied to an invitation, don’t assume the date is arranged. If someone hasn’t answered a question about sharing their private information, don’t share it.

An unanswered message also doesn’t establish that you have permission to keep pursuing contact.

This distinction can be especially useful if you find uncertainty difficult.

You may want a definite answer so you can stop thinking about the situation. But another person’s silence doesn’t create an obligation for them to resolve your uncertainty.

Sometimes the only available answer is that you don’t know.

You can make your own decision anyway.

For example, you can decide not to make the reservation, stop waiting for a reply, or turn your attention to other people and activities.

That’s different from deciding what the other person secretly feels.

When someone needs time to respond

Silence during an in-person conversation can mean something different from silence after days of unanswered messages.

Some people need time to work out what they feel or how to say it. Others may experience shutdown after overload, making speech difficult.

You may experience these things yourself, or date someone who does.

If your partner becomes quiet during an important conversation, don’t automatically assume agreement or rejection.

Pause.

If they’re able and willing to communicate, you can ask whether they’d prefer to continue later.

If they need time, give them time.

A clear question can help someone understand what you’re asking. It cannot turn an unavailable answer into permission to proceed.

This works in both directions. Someone who communicates quickly should respect a partner who needs time. Someone who needs time should respect a partner’s clearly expressed limits.

The double empathy problem matters here: two people may experience the same exchange differently. Explaining your view and listening to theirs can help, but neither person’s communication style overrides the other’s boundaries.

Respecting a boundary when you feel rejected

For some people, hearing “no” is simply disappointing.

For others, it can bring a rush of painful thoughts.

They don’t like me.

I’ve embarrassed myself.

I’m never going to find anyone.

Why would they do this if they cared about me?

You might feel angry. You might become quiet. You might want to explain why your request was reasonable or why the other person’s answer hurt.

Those feelings don’t automatically make you a bad partner.

What you choose to do with them matters.

Imagine that Jasmine and Elena have been dating for six months. Jasmine likes a good-night phone call. Elena likes to spend the last hour of the day alone.

Elena says, “I don’t want to talk on the phone every night.”

Jasmine feels hurt. To her, those calls are a sign of closeness.

She could interpret Elena’s preference as proof that she isn’t loved. She could also recognize that Elena is describing her own need for quiet.

Jasmine doesn’t have to pretend she’s happy about the change.

“I’ll miss our calls. But I understand you need that time.”

Later, when they both want to discuss their routines, Jasmine can explain that regular conversation is important to her. Perhaps they can find an arrangement that works for both.

Or perhaps their needs don’t fit together.

Respecting Elena’s boundary doesn’t require Jasmine to abandon her own.

It requires Jasmine not to pressure Elena into giving up something she has clearly said she needs.

Your needs matter too

A boundary is a limit on what someone will do or accept. It isn’t a command giving one person authority over the other’s life.

Suppose your partner doesn’t want to attend social gatherings.

You can respect that without agreeing to stop seeing your friends.

Suppose the person you’re dating doesn’t want a relationship that involves living together.

You can respect that while deciding that living together is important to you.

You don’t have to stay in a relationship that cannot meet your needs.

What you cannot fairly do is treat the other person’s boundary as an obstacle you must wear down until they agree with you.

Consider:

“I respect that you don’t want to live together. I do want that eventually, so I don’t think we’re looking for the same kind of relationship.”

That’s an honest recognition of incompatibility.

It doesn’t accuse the other person of doing something wrong. It doesn’t demand they change. It leaves both people free to choose.

Accepting someone’s no doesn’t mean you must say yes to everything else.

If you're dating someone who goes quiet instead of saying no

Silence can mean “I’m thinking,” “I’m overwhelmed,” or “I don’t want this and can’t find the words”. It never means yes, and you can’t tell which it is from the outside.

Stop and ask something answerable: “Shall I carry on, or stop for now?” A question with two clear options is far easier to answer than an open one when someone is already struggling to speak.

Treat stopping as the default when you’re unsure. Nothing is lost by pausing something that was welcome; a great deal is lost by continuing something that wasn’t.

When nobody taught you the rules

A surprising amount of dating advice assumes you’ve already learned how to handle rejection, interpret hesitation, and notice when someone wants more space.

Some people learned these things from friends or early dating experiences. Others had few opportunities to practice.

A small 2007 study compared parent reports about 25 autistic adolescents and adults with reports about 38 non-autistic people. Parents of autistic participants described less learning about romance from peers and more difficulty with certain courtship conventions (Stokes et al., 2007).

The study was small, relied on parents rather than participants’ own accounts, and reflected the assumptions of its time. It cannot establish how autistic adults generally respond to rejection, or suggest that autistic people are inherently more likely to stalk someone.

What it does help illustrate is why relationship rules shouldn’t be left entirely to guesswork.

You may have missed opportunities to learn those rules. You may interpret words literally, overlook an indirect message, or have difficulty remembering an agreement when you’re distracted.

None of that means you cannot learn to respect boundaries.

Start with the rule that matters most: when you aren’t sure whether an activity is welcome, don’t proceed without clarity.

There are also practical ways to make respecting limits easier.

If you forget verbal agreements, write down the ones that matter, provided doing so doesn’t violate someone’s privacy. If your partner needs quiet after work, add that preference to the way you organize your evenings.

If you aren’t sure whether a limit applies in a particular situation, ask when the person is comfortable discussing it.

You can say, “You mentioned that you don’t like surprise visitors. Should I always arrange visits in advance?”

Then follow the answer.

A preference doesn’t become less important because it seems unusual to you. And a difference in how your brains work isn’t a reason to expect only one person to adapt.

If you need something your partner cannot comfortably give, you can acknowledge that incompatibility rather than trying to eliminate their boundary.

When you’ve already crossed one

Nobody handles every interaction perfectly.

You might forget that your partner asked you not to share certain information. You might interrupt someone’s quiet time. You might misunderstand a request and do something they didn’t want.

Sometimes you realize what happened immediately.

Other times, the person has to tell you.

What matters next is not proving that you meant well. It’s stopping the behavior and taking responsibility for the effect it had.

Start by stopping

Suppose Priya shares a photograph of her date, Danielle, on social media.

Danielle sees it and says, “I didn’t want that picture posted.”

Priya hadn’t realized Danielle wanted to keep their dating life private.

The immediate response is to take the picture down.

Not to explain how flattering the photograph is. Not to argue that everybody shares pictures of dates. Not to insist that Danielle is overreacting.

Remove the photograph first.

Then, if Danielle is willing to talk, Priya can acknowledge what happened.

“You’re right. I posted that without checking with you. I’ve removed it, and I’ll ask before posting any pictures of you again.”

The apology names the action and a change for the future.

Priya doesn’t ask Danielle to pretend it never happened.

An apology is not a defense

A common temptation after making a mistake is to explain your intentions.

You may want the other person to understand that you weren’t trying to hurt them.

That can be relevant later. But if you make the entire conversation about proving your innocence, the person whose boundary was crossed may end up having to comfort you.

An apology should recognize what happened before trying to explain it away.

Research on apologies offers some useful, though limited, guidance.

In two experiments, researchers asked participants to evaluate apologies after hypothetical workplace trust violations. Apologies that included taking responsibility and offering repair were generally rated more effective than those emphasizing a request for forgiveness (Lewicki et al., 2016).

These were judgments about imagined workplace situations, not evidence that a particular apology will restore trust after a romantic boundary is crossed.

Still, the distinction is useful.

Compare:

“Sorry, but I didn’t know you’d be upset.”

With:

“I shared something you told me privately. I should have checked with you. I won’t share your personal information again.”

The second response identifies the action and the responsibility for preventing it from happening again.

It doesn’t make forgiveness a condition of the apology.

Don’t make the other person manage your guilt

A research review of apologies identifies several possible obstacles to apologizing well, including concern for one’s own self-image and doubts about whether apologizing will help (Schumann, 2018).

You might recognize the urge to defend yourself.

I’m normally respectful. This was just a mistake. I need them to know I’m not that kind of person.

But the other person doesn’t have to reassure you that you’re good.

You can feel ashamed, regretful, or frightened about losing the relationship without making those feelings their responsibility.

If you need support processing what happened, seek it somewhere appropriate without revealing private information that would create another boundary violation.

Your responsibility is to change your behavior. Their responsibility is not to make you feel better about having crossed their limit.

Repair must be something the other person wants

After a boundary violation, you may want to fix everything immediately.

But repair isn’t simply whatever makes you feel that you’ve made up for the mistake.

Sometimes the other person wants an explanation. Sometimes they want the practical problem corrected. Sometimes they want time alone.

Sometimes they no longer want the relationship.

If someone is willing to discuss repair, listen to what they want rather than assuming.

If they ask for space, respect it.

If they ask for no contact, don’t use an apology as a reason to contact them again.

You can take responsibility without receiving forgiveness. You can change your behavior without being offered another chance.

An apology cannot create an obligation to reconnect.

A mistake and a repeated violation are not the same

Imagine someone tells you that they don’t want an unexpected visitor.

You misunderstood their earlier preference and showed up once. When they explain, you apologize and stop making surprise visits.

That is a mistake followed by a correction.

Now imagine the person has repeatedly told you not to come over unexpectedly, and you keep doing it.

The issue is no longer that you don’t know the boundary.

You know it. Your behavior hasn’t changed.

The same distinction applies when someone asks you not to contact them, not to share their information, or not to touch them in a particular way.

Repeated violations aren’t erased by repeated apologies.

And some violations are serious even the first time. Threats, coercion, sexual violations, and other harmful acts shouldn’t be described as ordinary communication mistakes. Their consequences can extend beyond the relationship.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly ignoring someone else’s limits, stop the behavior. Don’t make the other person responsible for teaching you self-control or supervising your progress.

You can seek individual help from a qualified mental health professional to address what you’re doing and how to prevent it from happening again. That help isn’t a substitute for respecting the person’s wishes now.

When persistence becomes harmful

Romantic films sometimes portray persistent pursuit as evidence of extraordinary love. Someone refuses a date, and the determined admirer keeps trying until the story rewards their persistence.

One experiment found that a romantic portrayal of persistent pursuit increased endorsement of stalking-supportive beliefs among certain viewers who found it realistic or became absorbed in it. A frightening portrayal reduced support for those beliefs. The study measured attitudes after watching film scenes, not whether viewers later stalked anyone (Lippman, 2018).

Real people don’t owe anyone a romantic ending.

A person who says no isn’t an obstacle in a story about your determination.

Their choice is the ending of that particular possibility.

The consequences of disregarding boundaries can also be serious. The CDC describes stalking as a pattern of unwanted behavior that causes fear or concern for safety. Its national survey estimates indicate that approximately one in five women and one in ten men have experienced stalking during their lifetimes (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025).

Those figures concern stalking victimization, not how frequently someone continues pursuing a date after rejection. They also don’t provide a complete picture for nonbinary people.

The following chapter on persistence, harassment, and stalking will examine that distinction more fully. US legal definitions and protective options vary by jurisdiction.

For now, the practical rule is enough: once someone has made it clear that your contact or attention is unwanted, stop.

Don’t turn the refusal into a personal challenge.

What respecting a boundary looks like from the other side

Think back to the earlier chapters, where the challenge was identifying and expressing your own limits.

Imagine how different that experience becomes depending on the response you receive.

You tell someone you need to go home early because you’re exhausted.

One person accepts it. You leave without having to defend yourself.

Another person becomes upset, demands to know why you can’t stay, and treats your departure as a personal rejection.

Both people may feel disappointed. Only one is making that disappointment your problem.

The response changes whether you feel comfortable expressing your needs again.

This matters especially when you or your partner finds it difficult to identify feelings immediately, needs extra processing time, or has learned to avoid conflict.

You may not know how to describe discomfort until later.

A partner who accepts your limits without demanding an elaborate justification gives you room to express them when you can.

And you can offer that same consideration.

Respecting another person’s boundary doesn’t require you to read their mind. It requires you to take their words seriously, notice when something doesn’t seem welcome, and stop rather than assume.

US consent and relationship-safety guidance from RAINN and the National Domestic Violence Hotline emphasizes that silence, reluctance, and pressure cannot establish freely given sexual agreement (RAINN, 2026; The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

The next chapter will explain sexual and other consent fundamentals in detail. But the everyday relationship principle is already useful: another person’s participation should be something they freely choose, not something they give because refusing feels too difficult.

Three situations worth practicing

Consider what you would do in each of these situations before reading the suggested responses.

1. Someone declines an activity you were excited about.

You’ve planned a busy Saturday with several stops. Your partner says they can’t manage that much activity and would rather stay home.

You feel frustrated because you’ve already made plans.

A respectful response is to accept that they aren’t joining you. You can decide whether to go on your own, change your own plans, or discuss a different shared activity if they’re interested.

What you shouldn’t do is make their participation a test of how much they care about you.

2. Someone gives an uncertain answer.

You suggest a second date. They say, “I’m not sure.”

You want a definite yes or no.

You can acknowledge the uncertainty and leave the invitation alone. You don’t have to make plans or wait indefinitely.

If they decide they want another date, they can tell you.

3. Someone tells you that you crossed a boundary.

Your partner says you repeated something personal that they had told you privately.

You remember the conversation differently and hadn’t realized the information was private.

Your first job is to stop sharing the information. Then acknowledge what happened, listen if your partner wants to discuss it, and take reasonable steps to prevent it from happening again.

You can discuss the misunderstanding if the person wants to. But your explanation doesn’t undo what happened, and they don’t owe you immediate forgiveness.

These situations are different, but they have something in common.

In each, the other person’s boundary changes what you can reasonably expect to happen next.

That’s the moment when respect becomes a behavior rather than just an intention.

When the answer stays no

Sometimes you’ll respect every boundary and the relationship still won’t work.

You may want more affection than someone feels comfortable offering. You may need frequent communication while the other person needs long periods without contact. You may want a commitment they don’t want to make.

Nobody has to be the villain in that situation.

You can recognize that your needs are incompatible, even if you care about each other.

You can grieve a relationship that doesn’t happen. You can be sad about an invitation that was declined. You can wish the answer had been different.

But you cannot make mutual interest, affection, or commitment happen through persistence.

Respecting boundaries means allowing another person to make choices that you wouldn’t make yourself.

It also means keeping the freedom to make your own choices.

When both people can decline something without being punished, pressured, or forced to justify themselves, they have more room to discover what they genuinely want together.

And when a yes comes, it can mean much more because no was a real option.

That’s where the next chapter begins: understanding consent as a choice that belongs to the person making it, not a result someone else is entitled to obtain.
