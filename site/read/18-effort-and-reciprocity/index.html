Rina has been on three dates with Alex. All three were enjoyable. Alex laughs at her jokes, remembers details about her work, and seems genuinely happy when they meet.

But Rina has noticed something.

She suggested the first date. She chose the restaurant for the second. She asked about the third. After each date, she was the one who reached out.

Alex always responds warmly. Alex just never seems to start anything.

Now Rina is wondering whether to suggest a fourth date. She likes Alex, but she is beginning to feel as though the connection exists only because she keeps arranging it.

Does Alex like her? Is Alex shy? Does Alex assume she enjoys making plans? Is something else going on?

Rina doesn’t know yet. She has enough information to notice a pattern, but not enough to know what it means.

That distinction matters.

A relationship needs participation from both people. But participation doesn’t always look the same, and one person doing more in a particular moment doesn’t automatically mean something is wrong.

The important questions are whether both people want the connection, whether their actions reflect that interest, and whether they can find a way of participating that works for them both.

What effort actually means

Effort is what you voluntarily do to help a connection grow and remain healthy.

Reciprocity means that both people participate in sustaining that connection. Each person’s contribution matters, even when the contributions are different.

That sounds straightforward until you try to recognize effort in everyday life.

Imagine two people who have been dating for a few months.

One buys an expensive birthday present but frequently cancels plans without warning. The other cannot afford an expensive present but remembers an important appointment, checks whether their partner got home safely, and makes time to visit when they promised.

Which person is making more effort?

There isn’t enough information to decide. A single gift, cancellation, or thoughtful act doesn’t tell the whole story.

But the question reveals something useful: effort isn’t simply how much money, time, or energy someone spends. It includes how they treat the other person’s time, needs, and choices.

Researchers describe relationship maintenance as the things people do to sustain their relationships. A meta-analysis of 35 studies found that behaviors involving positivity, openness, reassurance, social connections, and sharing tasks were associated with relationship satisfaction and commitment. Interestingly, the associations were often stronger for people’s perceptions of their partner’s maintenance behaviors than for reports of their own (Ogolsky & Bowers, 2013).

In everyday terms, it matters not only that you make an effort, but also whether your partner experiences that effort as helpful and caring.

In early dating, effort might look like:

  • Showing interest in spending time together and sometimes helping make that happen.
  • Keeping a plan, or communicating when something prevents you from keeping it.
  • Remembering something important to the other person, or using a reminder if remembering is difficult.
  • Paying attention to what the other person enjoys and finds uncomfortable.
  • Making room for the other person’s preferences rather than assuming yours should determine everything.
  • Taking responsibility when you forget something, misunderstand a request, or inconvenience someone.

These aren’t requirements for passing a dating test. They’re examples of ways people can participate.

Someone might be excellent at remembering details but find planning difficult. Another might organize dates easily but need more time to express feelings.

The question isn’t whether they contribute identically. It’s whether each person’s contribution helps make the relationship workable for both.

Consistency matters more than impressive moments

When you’re strongly attracted to someone, memorable moments can take up a great deal of attention.

Maybe they planned a wonderful evening. Maybe they said something that made you feel especially wanted. Maybe the conversation lasted until two in the morning.

Those experiences can be meaningful. They are also only pieces of the relationship.

A large analysis combined data from 43 longitudinal studies involving 11,196 couples. Among the strongest relationship-specific predictors of how satisfied people felt were their perceptions of their partner’s commitment and their feelings of appreciation. The analysis also found that these measures were much less useful for predicting whether satisfaction would increase or decrease over time (Joel et al., 2020).

The study examined established couples, not a method for identifying a good partner after two dates. Still, it offers a useful distinction for beginners.

Being impressed by someone is different from experiencing them as committed and considerate over time.

Consider two possible patterns.

Pattern A: Someone plans an extraordinary date and talks enthusiastically about the future. But afterward, they repeatedly cancel, disappear from agreed plans, or leave you responsible for arranging every meeting.

Pattern B: Someone plans ordinary outings, sometimes gets nervous, and occasionally needs to reschedule. But they keep you informed, follow through on revised plans, and take an interest in what works for you.

Pattern B may feel less exciting at first. That doesn’t guarantee the people are compatible. It does provide more opportunities to experience reliability.

Consistency doesn’t mean constant availability.

Someone can care about you without answering immediately, spending every weekend together, or wanting the same amount of contact you want. People have jobs, responsibilities, friendships, health needs, and time they want to spend alone.

Instead, consistency means that the person’s behavior is reasonably dependable within the expectations you’ve actually discussed.

If someone says they will call on Sunday, do they usually call? If something changes, do they let you know when possible? If an arrangement isn’t working, are they willing to discuss an alternative?

The goal is not perfect performance. It is a connection in which you don’t have to repeatedly guess whether the other person intends to participate.

Feeling cared for and actually showing care

There is another complication: someone can make an effort that their partner doesn’t recognize.

Imagine two people looking at the same sculpture from different positions. One notices the shape of its front. The other sees details on its back. Neither is inventing what they see.

Effort can work that way.

For one person, making plans ahead of time communicates care. For another, allowing plans to remain flexible is a way of respecting someone’s freedom.

One person may express affection through conversation. Another may notice practical problems and quietly try to solve them.

Neither approach is automatically right. Both people still need to know whether their intended care is reaching the other person.

Researchers often use the term partner responsiveness to describe the experience of feeling understood, valued, and cared for by a partner.

In a 42-day diary study of 96 married couples, daily intimacy was associated with sharing personal information and perceiving one’s partner as responsive. The researchers found that perceived responsiveness helped account for the relationship between sharing and intimacy (Laurenceau et al., 2005).

That doesn’t mean everyone needs to share personal information at the same pace. It means that a partner’s response to what matters to you is an important part of how closeness can be experienced.

A study of 95 autistic and 65 non-autistic adults who had been in relationships involving an autistic person also found that perceived partner responsiveness was associated with relationship satisfaction in both groups. It was the only significant predictor in the researchers’ final model and uniquely explained approximately 23% of the variation in satisfaction (Yew et al., 2023).

This was a relatively small, cross-sectional survey, not a study watching couples interact. Participants were predominantly cisgender women, and the non-autistic group was especially limited in its representation. The findings cannot tell you exactly how a particular autistic or non-autistic partner should communicate.

They do suggest an important point: feeling understood and cared for matters on both sides of a relationship.

You can try making your care more visible without pretending to be someone else.

If you are thinking about a person you are dating, saying that you enjoyed your time together may help them understand your interest.

If you know a particular setting is uncomfortable for them, asking about a different location communicates that their experience matters.

If you need time alone after work, explaining that preference can help distinguish a need for rest from a lack of interest.

These are possibilities, not guaranteed solutions. The other person still gets to decide whether the arrangement meets their needs.

You don’t need a perfect romantic language

You may have encountered the idea that everyone has one of five primary “love languages,” and that successful couples need to identify and match them.

A 2024 scholarly review found little convincing evidence for the framework’s central claims: that everyone has one primary love language, that there are exactly five kinds of loving behavior, or that matching preferred languages reliably improves relationship satisfaction (Impett et al., 2024).

That doesn’t make personal preferences unimportant.

You can enjoy receiving thoughtful gifts and also want meaningful conversation. You can value affection while needing physical space on certain days.

You don’t have to choose one category, and you don’t have to fit yourself into someone else’s.

A simpler approach is to ask what makes each person feel considered, then pay attention to how those preferences work in practice.

For example:

“I really enjoy it when we make plans ahead of time. What works better for you?”

The question leaves room for two answers.

A conversation about preferences isn’t supposed to reveal which person is correct. It helps you discover whether your needs can coexist.

Fair doesn’t always mean equal

A common misunderstanding about reciprocity is that every contribution must be matched.

If you pay for dinner, the other person must pay next time. If you initiate two conversations, they must initiate two. If you spend four hours together on Saturday, they owe you four hours on Sunday.

Relationships aren’t accounting systems.

A fair arrangement doesn’t necessarily mean identical contributions. It means an arrangement both people can freely accept, where neither person’s needs are continually disregarded.

Research on perceived fairness helps explain why the distinction matters.

In a longitudinal study of dating couples, Sprecher found that people who felt they were receiving disproportionately less than they contributed tended to report lower satisfaction and commitment. Some findings also linked this experience to greater likelihood of breakup. However, the study found limited evidence that earlier perceptions of fairness consistently predicted later changes in satisfaction or commitment (Sprecher, 2001).

That leaves an important uncertainty. Does an unfair arrangement make someone dissatisfied, or does dissatisfaction make the arrangement feel less fair? Both may matter, and this study doesn’t fully settle the question.

Another study followed marital quality using information from 704 people whose marriages continued across six waves between 1980 and 2000. Feeling that one contributed more was associated with lower marital quality, although the author also discussed earlier research that found weaker or conflicting relationships between fairness and satisfaction (DeMaris, 2010).

That study involved a predominantly White sample of people in long-lasting marriages. Its measures also couldn’t capture every form of contribution. It should not be treated as a formula for how people dating in 2026 must divide their responsibilities.

What these studies do support is taking a persistent sense of unfairness seriously without assuming that fairness requires a perfect numerical balance.

Different abilities, different contributions

Mateo and Jalen, both in their thirties, have been dating for several months.

Mateo enjoys researching places to go. Jalen works irregular hours and finds advance planning harder, but enjoys spending time with Mateo.

They eventually settle into an arrangement.

Mateo usually suggests possible activities. Jalen checks his schedule, confirms which dates he can attend, and helps choose between the options. When Jalen needs to cancel, he lets Mateo know and suggests another time.

Mateo also sometimes asks Jalen to take the lead. When that happens, Jalen makes a plan that works for him, even if it is simpler than something Mateo might organize.

Their contributions aren’t identical. Neither person is doing nothing.

Now change the example.

Mateo always plans. Jalen regularly cancels without communicating. When Mateo asks for help arranging something, Jalen agrees but never follows through or proposes an alternative.

Mateo begins to feel responsible for every opportunity they have to see each other.

The difference isn’t that Jalen is bad at planning. It’s that the difficulty remains Mateo’s responsibility alone, despite attempts to find another arrangement.

You can respect someone’s limitations without accepting an arrangement that consistently leaves your needs unmet.

The same principle works in reverse. Having more energy, money, time, or organizational ability doesn’t automatically make you responsible for carrying the relationship.

And if you need more support than your partner does, you aren’t required to prove your worth by contributing in exactly the same ways.

The aim is a sustainable arrangement that both people understand and agree to.

When effort starts feeling one-sided

Return to Rina and Alex.

Rina has been initiating every date. That is an observable pattern. But it doesn’t tell her why Alex isn’t initiating.

Alex might assume Rina prefers making plans. Alex might be uncertain about her interest. Alex might genuinely enjoy seeing her but have little desire to build a relationship. There may be another explanation entirely.

Rina can recognize the imbalance without deciding in advance which explanation is true.

A useful question is not simply, “Who has done more?”

It is:

“If something important to this connection needs attention, do both people take some responsibility for it?”

Consider these signs of a potentially one-sided pattern:

  • You repeatedly initiate opportunities to spend time together, and the other person rarely shows interest in arranging time themselves.
  • Plans depend on your reminders, with little effort from the other person to find a workable system.
  • Your preferences are regularly set aside, while the other person’s preferences determine what happens.
  • When you raise a reasonable concern, the other person shows little willingness to discuss it or consider alternatives.
  • You feel responsible for maintaining the connection, even after making your needs clear.

One incident isn’t necessarily a pattern.

A person who forgets a date because of an unusually difficult week hasn’t automatically demonstrated that they don’t care. A person who needs to cancel because they’re ill isn’t failing a relationship test.

Look at what happens over time, including what happens after a difficulty becomes visible.

Does the other person acknowledge the difficulty? Do they participate in finding another way forward? Does anything change that makes the arrangement workable?

Those questions are more useful than assigning points for every message and invitation.

There is no research-backed rule saying that someone must initiate half the conversations, respond within a certain number of hours, or arrange a specific number of dates. There is also no universal number of weeks you must observe before deciding that an arrangement doesn’t work for you.

You are allowed to notice what you need, ask about it respectfully, and make decisions based on the answer and subsequent behavior.

The difference between a difficult week and an ongoing pattern

Imagine you are dating someone who has a demanding project at work.

For two weeks, their schedule is unpredictable. They explain that they have limited time and ask whether the two of you could plan something afterward. They remain interested in seeing you and follow through when they’re able.

You might decide that this temporary arrangement works for you.

Now imagine someone who repeatedly says they are too busy, makes no suggestions, and has no interest in discussing a workable arrangement.

They may have genuine demands on their time. Those demands don’t obligate them to date you, and they don’t obligate you to remain available indefinitely.

You can care about the person and still recognize that the connection isn’t meeting your needs.

A mismatch doesn’t require a villain.

Sometimes both people are decent, interested to different degrees, and unable to build the same kind of relationship.

When showing up is harder than wanting to

For some people, interest and follow-through don’t line up easily.

You might sincerely want to see someone but forget to reply to a message. You might enjoy spending time together but find a busy restaurant exhausting. You might be enthusiastic about making plans and then discover you don’t have the energy to carry them out.

These experiences can occur for different reasons. They don’t establish that someone has a particular diagnosis.

A scholarly review of ADHD and romantic relationships described greater relationship difficulties on average in the research it examined, while also identifying important gaps in understanding the mechanisms involved and how best to help couples (Wymbs et al., 2021).

That review doesn’t establish why any particular person forgot a plan or missed a message. It also doesn’t justify treating someone with ADHD as automatically unreliable.

The useful distinction is between having difficulty and taking no responsibility for how that difficulty affects the relationship.

If you struggle with follow-through, you can look for practical arrangements that work with your abilities.

For example, if you frequently forget appointments, consider adding agreed plans to your calendar as soon as they’re made.

If you sometimes need an entire evening to recover after work, suggest meeting at a time when you are more likely to have energy.

If spontaneous plans are difficult, say that you prefer advance notice.

If you need a quieter setting, suggest one instead of agreeing to a place you expect to find uncomfortable.

None of these arrangements guarantees a successful relationship. They are practical options that you and the other person can discuss.

You also don’t need to provide a diagnosis or disclose your personal history to explain a simple preference.

“I do better when plans are made a few days ahead” is enough information to begin a conversation.

If you’re dating someone who follows through differently

Suppose your partner remembers anniversaries but often forgets ordinary arrangements. Or they express affection easily in writing but need extra time to respond in person.

Before deciding that the difference means they don’t care, consider asking what makes the task difficult and what alternative might work.

But don’t assume that understanding the difficulty requires you to solve every problem for them.

You can offer support while still having needs of your own.

For example:

“I know planning ahead is difficult for you. I enjoy spending time together, but I don’t want to arrange every meeting. Could we find a way to share that?”

Your partner might have a workable suggestion. You might create one together. You might discover that the arrangement each person wants is different.

That isn’t automatically a failure. It is useful information about compatibility.

The study involving autistic and non-autistic adults suggests that feeling understood and responded to matters on both sides (Yew et al., 2023). It doesn’t establish that one partner must make all the adjustments.

Neither person should be treated as the problem simply because their way of participating differs.

If your effort doesn't arrive evenly

You may put in enormous effort in bursts and very little in between, and experience that as consistent caring because the intent never switched off. From outside it reads as unpredictable.

Smaller and regular beats large and occasional. One message at a reliable time is worth more to most people than an elaborate gesture after three quiet weeks.

If you know the gaps happen, say so rather than letting them be interpreted: “I go quiet sometimes and it isn’t about you. Tell me if it’s too much and I’ll set a reminder.”

Are you giving too much, or expecting too much?

One-sided effort isn’t always caused by the other person.

Sometimes you may be contributing far more than you were asked to contribute.

For example, you meet someone you like. You begin rearranging your schedule for them, buying gifts, offering assistance, and planning activities before either of you knows whether the relationship will become serious.

The other person enjoys your company but hasn’t promised the same level of commitment.

You may begin to feel disappointed that they aren’t giving as much as you are.

That feeling deserves attention. But the other person hasn’t necessarily acted unfairly by failing to match contributions they never requested or agreed to.

Voluntary effort doesn’t create a debt.

Buying dinner doesn’t entitle you to another date. Listening to someone’s problems doesn’t entitle you to romantic affection. Providing favors doesn’t entitle you to physical intimacy, personal disclosures, or a committed relationship.

You can offer care freely. You can also decide what you’re willing to offer.

If you are frequently giving more than feels comfortable, consider whether you’re choosing those contributions because you want to, or because you hope they will make the other person choose you.

You don’t need to earn someone’s interest by making yourself indispensable.

Similarly, if you notice yourself expecting another person to make every plan, organize every conversation, or adapt to every preference, ask whether you’ve been leaving enough room for their needs.

Are you participating in the connection you say you want?

Appreciation is not a payment

Appreciation can help make effort visible.

In a series of studies using different methods, Gordon and colleagues found that people who felt more appreciated tended to appreciate their partners more. Greater appreciation was also associated with responsiveness, commitment, and relationship continuation (Gordon et al., 2012).

These findings don’t prove that expressing gratitude will cause a reluctant partner to become more invested.

They support a more modest point: appreciation and responsive behavior are meaningfully connected in relationships.

If someone makes time to see you when their week is busy, it’s reasonable to acknowledge that.

“Thanks for making time today. I enjoyed seeing you.”

If someone remembers that crowded places are difficult for you and suggests somewhere quieter, you can tell them it helped.

“I appreciate you thinking about that.”

Appreciation is recognition, not a transaction.

You shouldn’t have to praise someone repeatedly to secure ordinary consideration. And being thanked doesn’t mean you now owe another favor.

The purpose is to let someone know when something they’ve freely chosen has mattered to you.

If you're dating someone whose effort comes in bursts

Intensity followed by silence is confusing, and it’s reasonable to find it hard. It usually isn’t a strategy, and it usually isn’t a verdict on you.

Ask for the floor rather than the ceiling: a short regular check-in is easier to sustain than a promise to be more attentive in general, and far easier to notice when it slips.

If you need more steadiness than someone can give, that’s a real mismatch and not a failure of patience on your part. Say it directly while it’s still a request rather than a grievance.

How to talk about an imbalance

Rina decides she wants to see Alex again, but she doesn’t want to keep guessing whether Alex is interested in making plans.

She doesn’t need to stop responding as a test or wait for Alex to notice her frustration.

Instead, she brings up the specific pattern.

“I’ve really enjoyed our dates. I’ve noticed I’ve been the one suggesting when to meet. I’d enjoy it if you chose something for us to do next time. Would that work for you?”

Alex has an opportunity to respond.

Perhaps Alex says, “Yes. I didn’t realize I’d left all the planning to you. Would you be free Saturday?”

Perhaps Alex explains that choosing locations is difficult and suggests taking responsibility for scheduling instead.

Perhaps Alex says they aren’t sure they want to continue dating.

Rina cannot determine the outcome by finding perfect wording. What she can do is communicate what she wants and respect Alex’s answer.

For a conversation about effort, four parts are often enough:

  1. Describe something observable. “I’ve arranged our last few dates.”
  2. Explain your experience. “I’d like to feel that we’re both interested in making time together.”
  3. Make a specific, reasonable request. “Would you be willing to suggest our next plan?”
  4. Listen to the answer. Consider both what the person says and what they freely choose to do afterward.

A request is not an instruction the other person must obey.

They can decline. They can propose a different arrangement. You can decide whether that arrangement suits you.

If they agree but repeatedly don’t follow through, you don’t need to turn the same conversation into a campaign for their attention.

And if they clearly decline further dating, respect that decision. More effort is not a way around a no.

The aim is to find out whether both people want and can support the connection, not to convince an unwilling person to participate.

You cannot build a relationship by yourself

There is an important difference between working on a relationship and working to keep someone in it.

A meta-analysis examining commitment found that satisfaction, perceived alternatives, and investments were all related to how committed people felt. Commitment, in turn, was associated with a lower likelihood of breakup (Le & Agnew, 2003).

An investment is something that would be costly to lose if the relationship ended. It might include shared time, plans, resources, or parts of a life built together.

But staying together isn’t automatically evidence that a relationship is healthy.

Someone may remain because they value the relationship. Someone may also find leaving difficult because of what they’ve already invested.

Likewise, spending more time and effort on an unsatisfying connection doesn’t guarantee it will become satisfying.

You can make yourself available. You can communicate clearly. You can acknowledge mistakes, adjust habits, and offer genuine care.

You cannot provide another person’s willingness.

That can be difficult to accept, especially if you have spent a long time hoping for a relationship or if a particular person feels unusually important to you.

You may want to believe that another thoughtful gesture, another conversation, or another opportunity will finally produce the interest you’ve been hoping for.

But a relationship isn’t a reward for persistence.

It is something two people continue to choose.

There are also situations where the problem is more serious than unequal effort. If someone frightens you, pressures you into unwanted intimacy, controls your access to other people, or punishes you for asserting your needs, don’t treat that as an ordinary disagreement about how to share responsibilities. Later chapters address manipulation, abuse, boundaries, and safety in detail.

Trying harder is not the answer to being mistreated.

A small check on your own relationship effort

You don’t need a spreadsheet of every message, favor, and expense. That could distract you from the experience you’re trying to understand.

Instead, think about your most recent interactions with someone you’re dating, or someone you’re considering dating.

Ask yourself four questions.

1. Who makes opportunities to connect?

Does each person sometimes show interest in spending time together, in ways that work for them?

If one person does most of the initiating, is that an arrangement both people understand and want?

2. What happens when something goes wrong?

Does a missed plan lead to acknowledgment and an attempt to find another arrangement?

Or does the same responsibility repeatedly return to one person?

3. Can both people express a need?

When you explain a preference, does the other person consider it?

Do you consider theirs, including when it requires a change from what you expected?

4. Does the arrangement feel sustainable?

Could you continue participating in this way without repeatedly ignoring your needs, exceeding your available energy, or hoping the other person becomes someone different?

These questions are a reflection exercise, not a scientific test. They won’t tell you whether someone secretly loves you or predict whether you’ll stay together.

They can help you identify one observable pattern to discuss, or one change you might make yourself.

If the answers reveal something uncomfortable, you don’t have to decide everything immediately. You can choose one reasonable conversation, observe what happens, and make a decision based on what you learn.

If you’ve already made your needs clear and the other person doesn’t want the same relationship, you are allowed to stop investing.

Both people get to choose

A healthy connection doesn’t require both people to have identical personalities, schedules, budgets, energy levels, or ways of showing affection.

It does require enough mutual willingness to make the connection possible.

Sometimes effort means initiating a date. Sometimes it means keeping a promise, finding an accessible alternative, admitting a mistake, or taking someone’s preference seriously.

Sometimes effort means recognizing that you don’t have the capacity or desire to offer what another person wants, and being clear rather than allowing them to keep making plans on an assumption you don’t share.

And sometimes the most respectful decision is to accept that a connection isn’t mutual.

You can be kind without taking responsibility for another person’s choices. You can recognize someone’s limitations without abandoning your own needs. You can appreciate the care someone offers without owing them a relationship.

After the excitement of a crush and the uncertainty of getting to know someone, this is one of the simplest questions worth asking:

Are both people freely participating in something that works for them?

You won’t always know the answer by watching. Sometimes you’ll need to explain what you’re experiencing and ask how the other person sees it.

Finding someone starts with talking to them. Next: communication.
