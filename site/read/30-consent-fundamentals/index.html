Morgan and Eli have been on three dates. They like each other. They’ve kissed before, and both enjoyed it.

Tonight they’re sitting together on the couch. Eli moves closer. Morgan smiles, then looks away. Eli isn’t sure what the smile means.

Maybe Morgan wants another kiss. Maybe Morgan is nervous. Maybe Morgan likes being close but doesn’t want to kiss tonight.

Eli could try to interpret the smile, remember what happened on their previous dates, and make a guess. Or Eli could ask:

“Would you like to kiss?”

That question does something guessing cannot. It gives Morgan a chance to choose.

If Morgan says yes, they can kiss. If Morgan says no, they don’t. If Morgan isn’t sure, they can stay where they are.

Nothing has gone wrong. Nobody has failed a romantic test. Two people are finding out what they both want.

This is consent.

What consent actually means

Consent is a freely chosen agreement to a particular activity, with a particular person, at a particular time.

For an agreement to count, the person needs to understand what they’re agreeing to, have the ability and freedom to choose, and communicate their agreement clearly enough that the other person isn’t relying on a guess.

Consent is also ongoing. Someone can change their mind, including after an activity has started.

These principles reflect guidance from RAINN, the US sexual violence prevention organization. Researchers also distinguish between wanting something internally, communicating that willingness, and another person interpreting the communication. Those three things don’t always match (Muehlenhard et al., 2016; RAINN, 2026a).

Think about the difference between consent and a boundary, which earlier chapters introduced.

A boundary concerns what you will do or accept. Consent concerns whether you agree to a particular interaction.

You might have a boundary against kissing on a first date. You might be comfortable kissing but not with other sexual activities. You might ordinarily enjoy hugging and still not want a hug today.

Your preferences can be consistent or change with the situation. Either way, the other person needs your agreement for the activity you’re considering together.

The same is true in reverse. Being attracted to someone doesn’t give you permission to act on that attraction. Both people must have a choice.

Although consent matters in everyday interactions, such as hugs or sharing private information, it is especially important in sexual situations. The legal consequences and definitions of sexual consent are different from the ordinary courtesy of asking before borrowing someone’s jacket.

This chapter explains the underlying skill. Later chapters apply it to physical touch, sexual situations, and safety.

The five parts of a genuine yes

There isn’t a required sentence that makes consent valid in every situation. It helps, however, to understand five qualities of meaningful agreement.

1. Freely given

A person needs to be able to say no without facing pressure, punishment, or threats.

Imagine that Avery agrees to a romantic activity because they genuinely want it. That’s different from agreeing because they feel unable to refuse safely.

The spoken word might be the same. The freedom behind it is not.

Consent isn’t something a person earns by spending money, being kind, providing a ride, or investing time in a relationship. Someone can enjoy dinner, accept a gift, and appreciate the evening without agreeing to anything physical afterward.

Likewise, being disappointed by a refusal is a feeling you can have. Making someone else responsible for relieving that disappointment is not an entitlement.

A freely chosen yes must remain an option alongside a genuinely acceptable no (RAINN, 2026a).

2. Specific

Agreement to one activity isn’t automatic agreement to another.

Saying yes to holding hands doesn’t mean agreeing to a kiss. Agreeing to a kiss doesn’t mean agreeing to sex. Agreeing to one sexual activity doesn’t mean agreeing to every other activity.

Research with 658 adults in the United States and United Kingdom found that willingness and consent communication varied across different sexual behaviors and contexts. This was an online convenience sample, not a representative survey of all adults, but it reinforces the importance of distinguishing one activity from another (Willis & Smith, 2022).

The same distinction matters outside sex.

Someone might happily share a photograph with you but not want it posted online. Someone might tell you a private story but not want you to repeat it to friends.

Permission to do one thing isn’t permission to do everything related to it.

3. Informed

A person needs to understand what they’re agreeing to.

Agreement shouldn’t depend on a mistaken understanding of what’s being proposed or on important information being deliberately withheld.

For example, consenting to a photograph doesn’t automatically include permission to publish it. Agreeing to spend an evening together doesn’t mean agreeing to spend the night.

If you’re proposing something and the other person doesn’t understand what you mean, clarify before proceeding.

You don’t need complicated language. In fact, simpler language often makes the choice easier.

4. Ongoing

Consent matters throughout an interaction, not just at the beginning.

Two people may agree to kiss. A few moments later, one might want to stop. The earlier agreement doesn’t override the new decision.

You don’t need a formal question every few seconds. But you do need to remain attentive to whether both people still want what’s happening.

If the situation changes, someone’s response changes, or you’re uncertain, stop and check.

Ongoing consent isn’t a test of whether you can perfectly interpret facial expressions. It’s a responsibility to notice uncertainty and make room for a real answer.

5. Withdrawable

Anyone can change their mind at any time.

They don’t owe a reason.

They may feel tired, uncomfortable, distracted, uncertain, or simply no longer interested.

They might have genuinely wanted the activity five minutes earlier. That doesn’t mean they’re obligated to finish it.

If someone withdraws consent, the activity stops.

The same principle protects you. You can begin something willingly and decide you no longer want it.

A previous yes cannot be collected and saved for later use (RAINN, 2026a).

What does not count as consent

One of the most important dating skills is learning not to turn missing information into a yes.

Consider these common situations:

What happened What it actually tells you
Someone smiled or flirted They may be interested, but haven’t necessarily agreed to physical activity
Someone agreed to a date They agreed to the date, not to kissing or sex
Someone invited you home They agreed to spend time in that setting, not to sexual activity
Someone didn’t say no You still don’t know whether they agree
Someone didn’t physically resist Lack of resistance doesn’t establish willingness
Someone agreed before That agreement concerned an earlier occasion
You’re in a relationship Relationship status doesn’t grant permanent permission
Someone seems physically aroused A bodily response isn’t a decision
Someone is asleep or too impaired to choose They cannot meaningfully agree in that state

RAINN specifically warns against treating flirting, dating history, previous sexual activity, silence, or spending time together privately as consent (RAINN, 2026a).

These distinctions matter because attraction, affection, and willingness aren’t identical.

You can find someone attractive without wanting to be touched. You can love a partner without wanting sex. You can feel physically close to someone while wanting to keep certain limits.

There is no contradiction in any of those situations.

If you are wondering whether a particular gesture counts as agreement, that uncertainty is a reason to ask or wait.

Why not resisting isn’t a yes

Some people become very quiet when uncomfortable. Others freeze.

During sexual violence, a person may experience tonic immobility, an involuntary defensive response involving physical immobility. A review by de Heer and Jones discusses this response and the incomplete way it has been reflected in some US legal and policy approaches (Heer & Jones, 2024).

You cannot assume that someone who remains still is enjoying or agreeing to what’s happening.

Nor can you assume that someone who freezes will be able to explain what is happening in the moment.

This distinction is particularly important if a person stops responding, appears frightened, or seems unable to participate in a choice.

The appropriate response is to stop, not to continue until they actively object.

The later chapter on sexual assault explains freezing and its consequences in more detail.

When someone says yes but doesn’t feel free to say no

A spoken yes is important, but it isn’t enough by itself.

Imagine someone agreeing to physical intimacy because they’re afraid of what will happen if they refuse. From the outside, the agreement might sound clear. Internally, the person may be trying to stay safe.

Or someone might feel that a partner’s disappointment, anger, or expectations leave them no acceptable alternative.

That isn’t the same as freely choosing the activity.

If you recognize yourself in this situation, you don’t need to prove that your discomfort was serious enough to matter. Your wishes matter whether you said no clearly, went quiet, or agreed because you didn’t feel safe refusing.

If you recognize yourself expecting an activity from someone who appears uncomfortable, stop. Don’t treat their eventual agreement as evidence that your earlier expectations were reasonable.

There is also a difference between choosing an activity you aren’t especially excited about and feeling unable to refuse it.

For example, someone might freely agree to a quiet evening at a restaurant they wouldn’t have chosen themselves. People make ordinary compromises. Consent doesn’t require someone to be visibly thrilled about every choice.

The important questions are whether the choice is informed, freely made, and something they are willing to do.

These distinctions become especially important in sexual situations, where pressure and fear can undermine meaningful agreement (RAINN, 2026a).

“But we’ve done this before”

Familiarity can make consent feel less necessary to discuss.

You’ve kissed a partner a hundred times. You know their habits. You can often recognize when they’re happy, tired, or uncomfortable.

That familiarity is valuable. It can also create assumptions.

In one study, 414 Canadian undergraduates read scenarios involving fictional couples. As the couples’ relationship histories became more intimate, participants tended to judge ambiguous situations as more consensual. This study measured observers’ perceptions, not whether the fictional people had actually agreed (Humphreys, 2007).

A separate study followed 84 US participants over 30 days and examined how previous sexual experiences with a partner related to consent communication. Reliance on consent cues generally decreased as shared sexual history accumulated, although the relationship reversed at very high levels of prior activity. The findings were more complicated than a simple claim that long-term partners stop communicating (Willis & Jozkowski, 2019).

Neither finding makes earlier consent valid for a new occasion.

Consider Naomi and her wife, Elena. They’ve been together for nine years. Naomi usually likes being kissed when Elena gets home.

One evening, Naomi has a headache and doesn’t want to be touched.

Elena’s knowledge of their relationship is useful background. It isn’t a substitute for Naomi’s current wishes.

The practical difference is straightforward.

A familiar partner may not need to explain every preference from the beginning each time. But familiarity should make it easier to notice and respect a changed answer, not harder.

In a study involving 37 committed couples, researchers found an association between more accurate perceptions of partners’ consent cues and stronger feelings of internal willingness. The study was small, involved mostly young, White, heterosexual, monogamous couples, and cannot establish cause and effect (Willis et al., 2021).

The lesson isn’t that every established couple needs to speak in exactly the same way.

It’s that understanding a partner remains an active process, even after years together.

Marriage, commitment, and shared history aren’t permanent consent.

What about alcohol and drugs?

Alcohol and drugs can complicate both communication and the ability to make choices.

But there are two distinctions worth understanding.

Having consumed alcohol is not automatically the same thing as being incapable of consent. There isn’t one number of drinks that establishes capacity or incapacity for every person and every situation.

Someone who is too impaired to understand or freely choose cannot provide meaningful consent. Being able to speak or move doesn’t automatically establish that they have the capacity to consent.

A 2022 diary study of 86 young adults found an association between binge drinking and relying more on contextual assumptions rather than active consent communication. The study was small and mostly involved women and people in monogamous relationships. It cannot prove that drinking caused any particular decision (Marcantonio et al., 2022).

The CDC’s 2023–2024 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey includes rape involving people who were too drunk, high, drugged, or passed out to consent or prevent penetration. This includes situations involving voluntarily consumed substances as well as substances given without the person’s knowledge (Leemis et al., 2025).

That is a public-health survey definition, not a universal statement of every state’s criminal law.

US states differ in their legal definitions of consent, intoxication-related incapacity, and other circumstances. RAINN maintains a state-by-state legal comparison, but current laws must be checked for a particular jurisdiction (RAINN, 2026b).

For everyday decisions, use a protective rule:

If someone is asleep, unconscious, or too impaired to make an informed, freely chosen decision, don’t initiate or continue sexual activity. If you’re unsure whether the person has the capacity to consent, wait until they are clearly able to choose.

You don’t need to calculate a legal threshold before deciding to treat someone carefully.

Capacity is not determined by a diagnosis

The ability to consent is not determined simply by whether someone is autistic, has ADHD, has an intellectual disability, or has another diagnosis.

Legal rules may consider a person’s actual ability to understand and choose in particular circumstances, along with age, authority relationships, and other factors. These rules vary by state (RAINN, 2026b).

Don’t assume that a disabled adult cannot make choices about intimacy. Equally, don’t ignore circumstances that prevent someone from understanding or freely choosing.

If a situation raises genuine questions about legal capacity, seek qualified local advice rather than guessing.

Asking doesn’t have to ruin the moment

People sometimes worry that asking for consent will make a romantic moment awkward.

What if the question sounds too formal? What if the other person thinks you’re inexperienced? What if asking interrupts the mood?

Those worries are understandable. They aren’t reliable reasons to guess.

McKenney and colleagues studied explicit verbal sexual consent among neurodiverse undergraduate students at universities in the northeastern United States.

Among 30 interviewees, 24 preferred explicit verbal consent in some or all situations. That included seven of the eight autistic interviewees. The researchers also surveyed another 230 students, finding broadly similar valuation of explicit verbal consent across autistic and non-autistic groups. Some autistic participants reported more difficulty initiating the conversation (McKenney et al., 2026).

These were mostly young college students. Their preferences don’t establish what every adult wants.

Still, the findings challenge the assumption that asking is inherently unwelcome.

Another survey involving 388 adults from several sexual communities found differences in how normal or disruptive explicit consent discussions seemed. Participants identifying with BDSM communities tended to regard such discussions as more ordinary and less disruptive than a comparison group, although the differences were not consistent across every measure. The participants were self-selected, so the study cannot establish population-wide attitudes (Harris et al., 2024).

Consent conversations can feel different depending on what people are used to.

You don’t need to imitate anyone else’s approach. You can learn a simple way that feels natural to you and is understandable to your partner.

Try a short, specific question

A good question makes the proposed activity clear and gives the other person room to choose.

For example:

“Would you like to kiss?”

“Is holding hands okay?”

“Would you like to keep going?”

“Do you want to stop?”

These are examples of communication, not tested formulas that guarantee consent.

They also aren’t lines that make an interaction acceptable regardless of the circumstances. A question cannot undo pressure, and an answer isn’t meaningful if the person cannot freely choose.

The goal is understanding, not obtaining a particular answer.

Make room for every answer

Suppose Priya asks Sam whether they would like to kiss.

Sam says, “Not yet.”

Priya replies, “Okay.”

That’s a complete exchange.

Priya doesn’t need to ask why. Sam doesn’t need to produce an explanation. Their evening can continue in whatever way they both want.

Or suppose Sam says, “Maybe. I’m not sure.”

That isn’t a yes. Priya can leave the kiss for another time.

A useful response is:

“That’s okay. There’s no hurry.”

Then leave the decision alone. Don’t turn uncertainty into an invitation to keep asking.

Don’t ask only once and stop paying attention

A clear yes at the beginning is useful information. It doesn’t remove the need to notice what happens afterward.

If someone suddenly stops participating, looks distressed, says they are uncomfortable, or asks to stop, take that seriously.

Stop the activity and give them space to respond.

Sometimes a simple check-in is appropriate. Other times the right response is simply to stop and leave the activity there.

You aren’t required to decode every small movement. But you are responsible for not treating uncertainty as permission.

Perspective: Men

Consent is often read from behavior rather than words, and the reading is unreliable. In a study of 185 heterosexual college students, men and women reported differing verbal and nonverbal cues for indicating consent while agreeing on what consent itself meant (Jozkowski et al., 2014). Shared definitions did not produce shared signals, which is where misreadings start.

So treat an absence of objection as no information at all. Asking out loud costs a sentence and removes the part you were going to have to guess.

If your communication styles are different

A person might prefer direct questions. Another might usually communicate through gestures. Someone may need extra time to answer, especially when tired or experiencing overload.

These differences can occur whether either person has a diagnosis.

Research on autistic and non-autistic young adults offers a useful caution: don’t assume one communication style automatically means better or worse understanding.

Brown-Lavoie and colleagues studied 95 autistic adults and 117 non-autistic adults. In that online sample, autistic participants scored lower on the researchers’ sexual-knowledge measure and reported fewer social sources of sexual information. The study also found a statistical relationship between knowledge and victimization, but it could not establish that one caused the other (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

Another study, involving 27 autistic and 122 non-autistic young adults, did not find significant group differences in sexual knowledge or receipt of sex education. Instead, differences in the ability to understand how language works in social situations were related to knowledge accuracy across both groups (Kohn et al., 2023).

These studies genuinely differ. Neither supports assuming that every autistic person knows less about consent or needs someone else to make decisions for them.

The practical approach is to learn how the individual communicates.

A person who doesn’t use much speech may still communicate a clear choice through an established gesture, sign, or written response. Someone who speaks easily may nevertheless feel unable to say no in a particular situation.

Words can be especially helpful when gestures are ambiguous, but speech isn’t the only possible means of communicating agreement.

When you need more time to answer

Imagine that two autistic adults, Daniel and Luis, are dating.

Daniel is comfortable answering questions immediately. Luis often needs a moment to understand how he feels, especially after a noisy evening.

Daniel asks whether Luis wants a kiss.

Luis replies, “Give me a minute.”

Daniel waits without moving closer.

After a moment, Luis says, “Yes, that sounds nice.”

On a different night, Luis might say no.

Neither response needs to be treated as strange.

If you need time, you can say so. If you’re dating someone who needs time, leave that time available without demanding an immediate answer.

If a person experiences shutdown and speech becomes difficult, don’t assume their silence means agreement. Pause the activity. A response can come later when they are able to communicate and choose freely.

The same principle applies if someone loses track of the conversation, becomes distracted, or needs a question repeated.

Use clarity rather than assumptions.

This is an application of consent principles to different communication needs, not a claim that autism or ADHD determines how someone understands consent.

Perspective: Women

If you’ve been taught to signal rather than state, saying what you want plainly can feel like a bigger step than it is. The same study found the gap was in the cues people reported using, not in how they understood consent (Jozkowski et al., 2014).

Being direct is not a loss of warmth, and it isn’t a demand. “I want this” and “I don’t want that yet” are both ordinary sentences, and they spare you relying on a signal the other person may never receive.

You get to ask, too

Consent isn’t a system in which one person initiates everything while the other person only accepts or rejects it.

Both people have desires. Both have boundaries. Both can propose an activity, ask questions, and change their minds.

If you want to hold someone’s hand, you can ask.

If you want a kiss, you can say so.

If you don’t want physical affection tonight, you can say that.

You can also ask about the other person’s preferences before a romantic moment happens.

For example:

“Do you generally like being asked before someone kisses you?”

“Would you rather talk about physical boundaries ahead of time?”

Those conversations can reduce uncertainty. But a general preference shared beforehand doesn’t replace consent in the moment.

You might learn that a partner likes affection most of the time, prefers spoken questions in unfamiliar situations, or needs more space when tired.

That’s useful information, not a permanent set of permissions.

The earlier chapters on communication and boundaries provide the groundwork for having these conversations without trying to read someone’s mind.

If you're dating someone who needs the question asked plainly

Mood, pace and body language may not carry the message you think they carry. Someone who reads language literally can miss an invitation entirely, or take a lack of objection as agreement when they are the one unsure.

Ask in words, about a specific thing, now: “Would you like me to kiss you?” Specific beats atmospheric, and it is much easier to answer honestly.

Being asked directly is a relief for a lot of people rather than a mood-breaker. It removes a guess they were going to have to make, and it shows them how to ask you.

If you change your mind

Sometimes you only discover what you want after an activity has started.

Maybe you expected to enjoy kissing and suddenly don’t.

Maybe you’re distracted, uncomfortable, or simply no longer interested.

You don’t need to establish that something is objectively wrong.

You can say:

“Actually, I want to stop.”

“I thought I’d be comfortable with this, but I’m not.”

“Can we take a break?”

These statements don’t contradict your earlier agreement. They update it.

If you’re on the receiving end, stopping is the appropriate response.

You can feel surprised or disappointed. You can need a moment to adjust. But the other person doesn’t have to continue the activity to protect your feelings.

If you’re the person changing your mind, you don’t owe continued participation because someone else is excited, because you’ve done it before, or because you were the one who suggested it.

The right to stop belongs equally to both people.

If you realize you crossed a line

Sometimes a person looks back on an interaction and realizes they made an assumption they shouldn’t have made.

Perhaps they treated silence as agreement. Perhaps they continued after someone became uncomfortable. Perhaps they thought earlier intimacy meant permission this time.

The correct response isn’t to search for a detail that makes the assumption acceptable.

Stop the behavior and take responsibility.

If the interaction is still happening, stop immediately. Respect the other person’s wishes about distance, contact, and whether they want to discuss what happened.

Don’t demand reassurance that everything was fine. Don’t turn your guilt into another responsibility for the person who was affected.

If you recognize a pattern of ignoring uncertainty or someone else’s wishes, seek qualified professional help to change that behavior. Where serious harm may have occurred, appropriate professional or legal guidance may also be necessary.

Being inexperienced may explain how a misunderstanding happened. It doesn’t cancel the other person’s right to choose.

And learning about consent isn’t a reason to interrogate someone who may have been harmed about whether they communicated clearly enough. The responsibility for ensuring meaningful agreement is never transferred to someone because they froze, hesitated, or struggled to speak.

A small practice exercise

You don’t have to wait for a sexual situation to practice asking clearly.

Start with ordinary choices where both answers are genuinely acceptable.

Imagine you’re spending time with someone you like. You would enjoy holding their hand, but you don’t know whether they want that.

First, identify exactly what you’re proposing.

Then choose a question:

“Would you like to hold hands?”

Now imagine three different answers.

“Yes.” You can hold hands, provided both of you remain comfortable.

“No, thanks.” You accept the answer and continue without holding hands.

“Maybe later.” You leave it alone for now. You don’t assume that later will automatically become yes.

Notice what the exercise doesn’t require.

You don’t need to predict the answer. You don’t need to persuade anyone. You don’t need to turn the interaction into a conversation about whether they like you.

The question is about holding hands.

That is all it needs to be about.

Try the exercise again with something nonphysical, such as asking whether someone wants to share a photograph publicly or spend more time together.

The underlying skill remains the same: propose something clearly, leave genuine room to choose, and respect the choice.

Consent and the law aren’t exactly the same question

This chapter gives a practical standard for treating people safely and respectfully. It isn’t a substitute for legal guidance.

In the United States, consent laws vary by state. Jurisdictions differ in how they define consent, age-related capacity, intoxication, unconsciousness, and other circumstances. Some statutes explicitly address relationship history and the absence of physical resistance. Others use different wording or structures (RAINN, 2026b).

A behavior can also violate someone’s boundaries or be harmful without necessarily satisfying every element of a particular criminal offense.

You don’t need to establish that something is illegal before deciding not to do it.

If you need information about a specific state’s law, RAINN’s State Law Database is a useful starting point. A qualified attorney or sexual assault advocate can help with questions about an actual incident.

Later chapters explain sexual consent in more detail, sexual assault, and where to find support.

The question that matters

The hardest part of consent often isn’t learning the definition. It’s giving up the idea that you should already know what someone wants.

You may be very good at understanding a familiar partner. You may have trouble reading anyone’s signals. You may sometimes notice your own preferences only after they’ve changed.

None of those experiences eliminates the need for consent, and none makes respectful dating impossible.

You don’t have to see the situation from exactly the same position as your partner. You do have to remember that your view isn’t the only one.

A clear question can reveal something that a hundred guesses cannot.

Consent isn’t getting someone to agree. It’s finding out whether you both want the same thing, with the freedom for either person to say no.

And when the answer is uncertain, the next step is simple: ask or wait.
