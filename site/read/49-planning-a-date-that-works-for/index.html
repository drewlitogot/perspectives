Samira has been messaging Eli for two weeks. They enjoy talking, and Eli has agreed to meet.

Now Samira has a problem: what are they supposed to do on a date?

She considers a nice restaurant. That seems romantic. But she dislikes crowded rooms, the menu is expensive, and she worries about sitting across from someone for two hours with nothing to talk about.

Then she considers a movie. At least there would be no pressure to keep talking. But how would they get to know each other?

She spends an evening searching for the perfect first-date idea.

Meanwhile, Eli would be perfectly happy getting coffee and walking around a bookstore.

Neither person needs an extraordinary experience. They need a place where they can meet, feel reasonably comfortable, and find out whether they enjoy each other’s company.

A good date plan makes it easier for two people to be themselves around each other.

That sounds simple, but it changes how you choose the place, the activity, the time, and even how much money to spend.

What a first date is actually for

A first date is not a job interview, an audition for a relationship, or a performance in which you must demonstrate how interesting, attractive, successful, or romantic you are.

It is a chance for two people to learn a little more about each other.

You might discover that you enjoy the conversation. You might discover that you have very different interests. You might feel attracted to someone, or realize that the connection you felt while messaging doesn’t carry over into person.

You do not need to decide whether someone could become your lifelong partner.

You are looking for enough information to decide whether you would like to know them better.

This is especially worth remembering if you have little dating experience. You may have learned what dates are supposed to look like from movies, television, or other people’s stories. Those examples often emphasize elaborate settings, surprises, and dramatic romantic moments.

Real dating can be much more ordinary.

In interviews with 28 autistic young adults in the United States, participants described difficulties with implicit social expectations and a need for more concrete dating education. They also described strengths they brought to relationships, including acceptance and empathy. The study was small and focused on adults aged 19–28, but it helps illustrate why dating conventions that others seem to take for granted may need to be explained rather than assumed (Kohn et al., 2025).

Here is one of those conventions:

The person who suggests a date does not have to design the entire experience alone.

Making the invitation is one step. Agreeing on a plan is something both people can participate in.

You can suggest somewhere. The other person can suggest somewhere else. You can ask about comfort, cost, and timing. Neither person has to pretend that every suggestion sounds wonderful.

A first date is already an opportunity to practice something useful in a relationship: making a small decision together.

Choose a place where you can actually get to know each other

Imagine two first dates.

On one, you’re at an expensive restaurant. The food is excellent, but the room is so loud that you can barely hear the other person. You keep asking them to repeat themselves. By dessert, you’re exhausted.

On the other, you’re at a modest café with a comfortable table. You talk about your favorite places in town, laugh about a shared experience, and discover that you disagree about almost everything involving pineapple on pizza.

The second date may tell you far more about whether you enjoy spending time together.

That doesn’t mean cafés are scientifically proven to be the best date locations. There is no good evidence in this chapter’s research comparing cafés, restaurants, museums, walks, or other activities to establish a winning first-date format.

Instead, use a practical question:

Will this setting help both of you enjoy spending time together?

Think about whether you will be able to hear each other, talk without constantly competing with the surroundings, and participate in the activity comfortably.

You might choose:

  • A café or casual restaurant where conversation is possible.
  • A museum, gallery, or bookstore where you can look at things together.
  • A short walk in a populated park or public shopping area.
  • A daytime community event or low-pressure activity that interests you both.
  • A familiar public place where you already know the noise level, seating, and general atmosphere.

These are options, not a ranking. An excellent place for one person may be unpleasant for someone else.

Conversation or activity?

A sit-down date gives you a chance to concentrate on talking. That can be appealing if conversation comes easily or you would rather not manage several things at once.

But sitting across from a stranger with nothing else happening can feel intense.

An activity gives you something to look at or discuss. In a museum, for example, the next question might naturally be about a painting rather than your entire romantic history.

You can also combine the two: visit an exhibit, then sit somewhere nearby if both people want to keep talking.

The important thing is not to use an activity to avoid learning about the other person entirely.

A movie can be an enjoyable date, but if you spend nearly all your time watching a screen, you may have relatively little opportunity to talk. You can choose that deliberately, or arrange some conversation time before or afterward.

There is no requirement to talk continuously. Comfortable pauses are allowed.

Your senses get a vote

A restaurant might look wonderful in photographs and still be a terrible place for you to spend an evening.

Perhaps the music is too loud. The lights shine directly into your eyes. There are several conversations happening at once. Someone nearby is wearing strong perfume. Your clothes feel uncomfortable, and the noise makes it difficult to concentrate on what your date is saying.

You may end up using most of your attention just to tolerate the environment.

In a UK study of 49 autistic adults, participants described many kinds of sensory experiences. Among the 40 who answered specific sensory questions, loud noises, multiple simultaneous conversations, bright lights, music, and strong scents were common sources of difficulty. Participants also described strategies that helped, including greater control, knowing what to expect, and being able to leave an uncomfortable environment (MacLennan et al., 2022).

Another study involving 24 autistic adults in UK focus groups found that public spaces could be difficult for overlapping reasons, including sensory conditions, the amount of space available, unpredictability, and the reactions of other people. Participants emphasized the importance of adjustments and opportunities for recovery (MacLennan et al., 2023).

These were small, self-selected studies, not tests of dating venues. They do not mean every autistic person dislikes restaurants, music, crowds, or surprises.

In fact, the first study found something interesting: many participants who identified music as a source of sensory difficulty also reported seeking music in other circumstances (MacLennan et al., 2022).

Someone might love loud music at a concert but struggle with music playing over a conversation. Someone else might feel more comfortable with background activity than in a silent room.

The issue is not simply whether a place is quiet.

It is whether the environment works for the people who will be there.

You might prefer more activity, not less

If sitting still makes it harder to concentrate, a walk or activity-based date may feel more comfortable than a long restaurant meal.

A Dutch study of 116 adults diagnosed with ADHD found differences in sensory responses, including both greater and reduced sensitivity, particularly involving sound and activity level (Bijlenga et al., 2017).

That finding doesn’t mean everyone with ADHD needs to move, or that movement is always helpful on dates. It means sensory preferences can vary in more than one direction.

You might love a busy market, provided you can step away when needed. You might prefer walking while talking. You might enjoy something visually interesting but dislike crowded restaurants.

There is no need to turn an individual preference into a diagnosis.

You can simply say, “I find it easier to talk when we’re walking. Would that work for you?”

Ask about preferences instead of guessing

Suppose Maya enjoys art museums because she likes having something interesting to examine. Her date, Rowan, finds museums tiring because standing for long periods is difficult.

Neither person’s preference is wrong.

Maya could suggest a smaller gallery with seating. Rowan could suggest a café with artwork on the walls. They might choose something entirely different.

This is the double empathy problem in miniature: two people can experience the same setting differently, and neither knows exactly what it feels like for the other.

You cannot solve that by guessing harder.

You can ask.

“Would you rather sit somewhere and talk, or do something while we talk?”

“Do you like busy places, or somewhere quieter?”

“Would you be comfortable walking, or would you prefer somewhere with seating?”

These questions are not medical evaluations. They are ordinary ways to make a plan together.

You do not owe anyone an explanation of your neurotype, disability, or personal history just to request a suitable date.

The same applies to the other person.

If someone says that they prefer a quieter venue, you can take that preference seriously without asking them to justify it.

If not knowing the plan is harder than the plan itself

An unspecified evening can cost more than a long one. Not knowing where, how loud, how long or how you’ll get home takes up the capacity you wanted for the person.

Asking for specifics isn’t high-maintenance, it’s planning: “Where were you thinking, and roughly how long?” Most people have an answer and are glad to be asked.

Knowing the shape in advance frees you to improvise inside it. Certainty about the frame is often what makes spontaneity possible.

The best date plan has two people in it

Suppose Theo prefers knowing exactly what will happen. He likes to see the menu before visiting a restaurant, know how long an activity will take, and understand where he should park.

His date, Andre, enjoys spontaneous plans. He doesn’t mind deciding where to eat after arriving downtown.

Neither preference is inherently better.

But “Let’s meet downtown and figure it out” may be much more comfortable for Andre than for Theo.

They can solve that difference before the date.

Theo might say:

“I’d be more comfortable if we picked a place ahead of time. Would you be okay with that?”

Andre might answer:

“Sure. Let’s choose the restaurant now, and if we’re both having a good time afterward, we can decide whether to do something else.”

They now have a predictable starting plan without scheduling every minute.

Research involving autistic and non-autistic adults in Australia identified themes of communication, sensory needs, and personal control in intimate relationships. That study was not about organizing first dates, so applying those findings to scheduling is a practical inference rather than a tested date-planning method (Sala et al., 2024).

A separate US study compared short conversations between unfamiliar autistic and non-autistic adults. The results suggested that social experiences depend partly on who is interacting with whom, rather than being determined entirely by one person’s characteristics. These were five-minute non-romantic conversations, so they cannot tell us how to plan an ideal romantic date (Morrison et al., 2020).

Still, the principle is useful: a date belongs to both participants.

If you prefer precise plans, it is reasonable to explain that.

If you prefer flexibility, you can explain that too.

If you’re dating someone who wants more details than you do, giving them those details isn’t surrendering your independence. If you’re dating someone who likes spontaneity, you don’t have to abandon your own limits to accommodate them.

You are trying to find an arrangement that gives both people enough comfort and freedom.

Don’t design a date around a stereotype

A person who tells you they are autistic might enjoy concerts. Someone with ADHD might prefer a quiet café. A person with no diagnosis might dislike unfamiliar restaurants. Someone who has lived through difficult relationships might simply want a clear plan and independent transportation.

You cannot reliably choose a date from someone’s label.

Nor should you assume that someone of a particular gender wants a particular kind of romance.

Ask what the person enjoys, then believe the answer.

Making the invitation specific

“Do you want to hang out sometime?” can be a starting point, but it leaves several important things unclear.

Does “hang out” mean a romantic date or a friendly meeting? When is “sometime”? What will you do?

If you intend a romantic date, being clear avoids unnecessary guessing.

For example:

“Would you like to go on a date with me? There’s a café downtown I think you’d enjoy. Are you free Saturday afternoon?”

That gives the other person a clear decision to make.

If they say yes, you can work out the details together.

If they decline, respect that answer. A declined invitation is not a puzzle to solve by proposing increasingly different activities.

If they like the idea of going on a date but cannot do Saturday afternoon, they can suggest another time.

When someone genuinely wants to participate, you can both contribute to making the plan possible.

Give enough information to make a real choice

Consider this message:

“How about the art museum Saturday at 2 p.m.? Admission is $12 each. We could spend about an hour looking around. Would that work for you, or would you prefer something else?”

It gives the other person useful information without requiring them to guess what you expect.

They know the proposed activity, time, approximate cost, and length.

They also know that suggesting an alternative is welcome.

Contrast that with:

“I have something amazing planned. Just dress nicely and trust me.”

A surprise might be enjoyable to someone who has explicitly said they like surprises. But for someone you barely know, withholding ordinary planning information can make it difficult to decide whether the activity is comfortable, affordable, accessible, or safe.

You do not have to reveal every small detail.

You should give someone enough information to choose freely.

An invitation isn’t a commitment to everything afterward

Agreeing to coffee does not mean agreeing to dinner afterward.

Agreeing to dinner does not mean agreeing to go to someone’s home.

Agreeing to a date does not mean agreeing to kiss, hug, have sex, or begin a relationship.

Plans can be changed only when both people want the change.

That applies equally if you are the person who planned the date and if you are the person who accepted the invitation.

Choose a manageable amount of time

An elaborate first date can create a practical problem: you commit to several hours with someone you barely know.

Imagine planning dinner, a show, and dessert afterward. Even if you enjoy the person, you might become tired before the evening ends.

If you discover early that you don’t enjoy the interaction, you may feel uncomfortable with how much of the evening has already been arranged.

A simpler plan gives both people room to decide what they want.

For example, you might suggest meeting for coffee for about an hour.

That isn’t a scientifically established ideal duration. The available research does not identify a best number of minutes for a first date.

It is simply a manageable option.

If you are enjoying the meeting, you can ask whether the other person would like to continue. If either of you wants to finish at the agreed time, that is fine too.

A date does not become better merely because it lasts longer.

Plan for your actual energy, not your imaginary best day

Think about the version of yourself who will arrive at the date.

If you’re scheduling it after an eight-hour workday, will you still have enough energy for an unfamiliar restaurant?

If Saturday mornings are difficult, is a 9 a.m. breakfast date really your best choice?

If you need to prepare mentally for unfamiliar situations, have you allowed time to do that?

In interviews with 16 autistic adults in the United Kingdom, participants described spending time alone to respond to and recover from social and sensory demands before becoming ready to reconnect. The researchers identified several different roles that time alone can play (Neville et al., 2026).

This doesn’t establish that everyone needs recovery time, or tell you exactly how much.

It does support taking that need seriously when it applies to you.

You might decide not to schedule a date immediately after a demanding work shift. You might leave the following morning free. You might prefer an afternoon meeting because evenings are difficult.

The useful question is not, “How much dating can I force myself to manage?”

It is, “When will I have enough energy to be reasonably present?”

Being comfortable does not mean you won’t be nervous. Some nerves are perfectly compatible with an enjoyable experience.

But there is no prize for making an already challenging situation unnecessarily exhausting.

If someone needs quiet time afterward

Some people enjoy social interaction and still need substantial time alone to recover.

If your date mentions this before meeting, you do not need to interpret it as a prediction about whether they will like you.

You might simply agree on a time that works and avoid adding unplanned activities.

The other person’s needs matter, and so do yours. If you prefer longer outings while they prefer shorter ones, the goal is not to convince either person that their preference is unreasonable.

It is to see whether you can find an arrangement you both enjoy.

Deal with practical details before they become problems

Not every part of planning is romantic. Some of it is simply making sure you can get to the right building at the right time.

That matters.

A pleasant idea becomes much less pleasant when you discover that the museum closes an hour after you arrive, the restaurant requires reservations, or the nearest parking is several blocks away and one person cannot comfortably walk that distance.

Before confirming a date, check the details that matter for your plan.

For a restaurant, that might mean the menu, opening hours, prices, reservations, dietary needs, and whether the seating works for both people.

For an outdoor activity, think about weather, daylight, accessible paths, available seating, and an alternative if conditions change.

For a museum or event, check admission costs, entry times, accessibility, and whether tickets are required.

If you’re going somewhere unfamiliar, it is reasonable to check photographs or the venue’s information in advance.

You don’t need to research a café as thoroughly as someone purchasing a house.

You just need enough information to avoid preventable difficulties.

When organization takes extra effort

Perhaps you routinely underestimate travel time.

You remember an appointment exists but forget the day. You leave at the right time but realize you never checked the address. Or you have the address and forget that parking will take another ten minutes.

These difficulties can be frustrating when you are trying to make a good first impression.

The US National Institute of Mental Health identifies planning, organization, time management, appointments, and remembering tasks among areas that can be difficult for some adults with ADHD (National Institute of Mental Health, 2024).

That doesn’t mean lateness or forgotten plans are inevitable.

It means using external supports may be more practical than relying on memory alone.

You could put the date in your calendar as soon as it is confirmed, save the address, set a reminder to get ready, and check your travel time beforehand.

If you routinely need a few extra minutes to find your keys or get to the car, include that in your plan.

These are practical suggestions, not experimentally proven date-planning treatments.

You can also ask your date to confirm the details in writing. A quick message with the time and location can help both people, regardless of how their attention works.

Confirm without turning the plan into a project

A confirmation message can be very simple:

“Looking forward to Saturday. Just confirming we’re meeting at the museum entrance at 2 p.m.”

You don’t have to send a detailed itinerary or repeatedly check whether the other person still wants to come.

If something changes, let them know promptly.

If the restaurant closes unexpectedly, you can suggest another public location. If the weather changes, you can agree on a suitable indoor alternative.

If the new plan doesn’t work for someone, you can reschedule.

A plan is there to support the date, not control it.

Money doesn’t have to be a mystery

Who pays?

It is a surprisingly ordinary question that can carry a great deal of unspoken expectation.

Some people expect the person who invited the other to pay. Some expect to split the cost. Others enjoy treating someone, especially on an occasion they suggested.

You might have grown up around the idea that one gender is supposed to pay. Your date may have grown up with different expectations.

A large US online survey published in 2015 examined payment expectations among 17,607 unmarried heterosexual respondents. It found both continuing traditional gender patterns and substantial disagreement within gender groups. Some participants wanted a partner to insist on paying, while others disliked that insistence (Lever et al., 2015).

A later study of 552 heterosexual college students also found traditional payment patterns and expectations (Wu et al., 2023).

Both studies were observational, relied on self-report, and examined heterosexual participants within binary gender categories. The older survey was self-selected, and the newer study focused on college students. Neither tells you what every person expects today, particularly in same-gender or gender-diverse dating.

The practical answer is to avoid expecting someone to read your mind.

Choose something both people can afford

A date does not have to be expensive.

If you are worried about spending $100 on someone you have never met, you don’t need to arrange a $100 date.

If your date has a limited budget, choosing an expensive restaurant may create pressure even when you intend to pay.

You can suggest an affordable activity from the beginning.

“I’d enjoy getting coffee or checking out the free exhibit at the library. Which would you prefer?”

You can also be direct about costs.

“Tickets are $15 each. Would that work for you?”

There is no need to apologize for having a budget.

Talk about paying instead of staging a test

If you intend to treat someone, you can offer.

“I’d be happy to pay for the tickets if you’re comfortable with that.”

If you prefer to split the cost, you can say so.

“Would you be okay with each of us paying for our own meal?”

The other person can accept, decline, or suggest something different.

You do not need to use payment as a test of generosity, independence, masculinity, femininity, or romantic interest.

And there is one rule that does not change with the price of the date:

Paying for something never entitles anyone to affection, physical contact, sex, or another date.

If someone offers to buy you dinner, accepting that offer does not mean you owe them anything beyond ordinary courtesy.

If you are the person paying, your offer is a gift or an agreed expense, not a purchase of someone else’s attention or body.

If you recognize yourself thinking that the other person owes you something because you spent money, stop and reconsider that expectation before dating further.

Keep comfort and safety together

A comfortable environment and a safe meeting place are not competing goals.

You might want a quiet place, but quiet does not have to mean isolated.

A staffed café during a less busy hour may offer both conversation and other people nearby.

A museum can offer space without requiring you to meet in a private home. A populated park can offer a pleasant walk while remaining publicly accessible.

For first meetings with someone you do not know well, RAINN recommends public locations, control over your own transportation, letting a trusted person know your plans, and maintaining the ability to leave (RAINN, 2026).

These precautions cannot guarantee safety. If someone harms you, responsibility belongs to the person who caused the harm, not to you for failing to predict it.

Chapter 48 covers meeting-stranger safety more fully. You should use those precautions alongside your venue preferences, not instead of them.

If you want to meet somewhere public and the other person repeatedly pressures you toward an isolated location, take that seriously. You do not need to negotiate away your safety to prove that you are trusting or interested.

And if someone declines your proposed location, you can respect that decision without trying to pressure them into changing it.

A mutually enjoyable date requires a plan both people freely accept.

If you're dating someone who asks a lot of questions beforehand

Questions about the venue, timing and arrangements usually mean the date matters and they want to arrive able to enjoy it, not that they’re difficult to please.

Answer concretely and offer a choice: “This bar at seven, it’s quiet, about two hours — or the café instead if you’d rather.” That costs you nothing and removes most of the problem.

A date that’s been planned around both people’s needs is generally a better evening. You’re likely to meet a more relaxed version of them.

You don’t have to hide what you need

There can be a temptation to plan the date around the person you imagine you should be.

Perhaps you think a confident dater should love packed restaurants. Perhaps you think a romantic person should enjoy surprises. Perhaps you worry that asking about the menu, parking, or seating will make you seem difficult.

So you agree to things you already know will make the evening harder.

Then you spend the date managing discomfort.

The result is frustrating: you wanted to get to know someone, but the situation demanded so much effort that you could barely pay attention to them.

Being considerate does not require ignoring yourself.

You can say:

“I’d rather go somewhere without loud music so I can hear you.”

“I have a food restriction. Could we check the menu before choosing?”

“I do better when I know the plan in advance.”

“I’d like to keep the first meeting fairly short, if that’s okay with you.”

You don’t need to turn each preference into a confession or an apology.

You also don’t need to make every request a nonnegotiable boundary.

Sometimes you can compromise. Sometimes you can find another activity. Sometimes your needs and the other person’s preferences genuinely don’t fit.

That is useful information.

Someone who wants a lively, late-night restaurant date is not necessarily inconsiderate. Someone who wants a quiet afternoon is not necessarily boring.

They may simply enjoy different environments.

You can look for the overlap.

The same courtesy goes both ways

Imagine that Avery tells Jordan that crowded restaurants are exhausting.

Jordan responds by suggesting a small café instead.

That’s considerate.

Now imagine that Jordan mentions enjoying occasional spontaneous activities, while Avery prefers knowing plans beforehand.

Avery could say, “I’d enjoy something spontaneous once I know the general idea and how long it might take.”

That is considerate too.

Neither person is obligated to fulfill every preference. Both people are responsible for communicating honestly and considering what the other person says.

In dating, accommodation should not mean one person’s comfort always wins.

It means making reasonable efforts to find conditions where both people can participate.

What if the plan goes wrong?

Even careful plans sometimes fail.

You discover the café is unexpectedly crowded. The weather changes. Public transportation runs late. The exhibit you intended to see is closed.

None of these automatically ruins a date.

You can communicate what happened and decide together what to do.

“We got here and it’s much louder than expected. Would you prefer trying the place across the street?”

“It’s raining pretty hard. Would you like to switch to the indoor market, or reschedule?”

“The museum is closed today. I didn’t catch that when I checked. I’m sorry. Would you like to find somewhere else nearby?”

You do not have to pretend an inconvenient situation is delightful.

You also do not have to become angry with yourself for failing to predict every possibility.

Your response can be straightforward: acknowledge the problem, offer a reasonable alternative, and let the other person decide whether it works for them.

If you made a mistake, take responsibility for it. If the change was outside your control, you can still help work out the next step.

There is a difference between being flexible and disregarding someone’s limits.

If your date cannot comfortably manage the alternative, rescheduling may be the better choice.

A plan for two different people

Consider a more complete example.

Nico, 41, has never been on many dates. He has been messaging Marcus, 38, for several weeks.

Nico wants to meet, but he dislikes unpredictable environments. He enjoys quiet conversation and tends to become tired after a long day of social interaction.

Marcus likes active outings. He enjoys exploring new places and finds extended periods of sitting uncomfortable.

Nico initially considers suggesting a romantic dinner. He believes it is what adults are supposed to do on a first date.

But when they discuss it, Marcus says he would rather do something than sit in a restaurant all evening.

Nico suggests an afternoon visit to a small public art market. They can walk around, see the stalls, and sit for coffee if they want.

Marcus likes the idea.

They agree to meet at 1 p.m. on Sunday, spend roughly an hour looking around, and decide whether they want coffee afterward. Each will arrive independently.

Nico checks the location and discovers that the market is busiest around lunchtime. He mentions it.

Marcus suggests meeting a little later instead.

They settle on 2:30 p.m.

Nothing about this plan is remarkable.

But notice what happened.

Nico did not force himself into a date format he disliked. Marcus did not have to pretend that an extended restaurant meal sounded appealing. They made their needs clear and found an activity that might work for both.

Neither person had to disclose a diagnosis or explain every reason behind a preference.

And neither had to prove that their idea of a date was superior.

They simply planned something together.

That is a useful beginning.

Your five-part date plan

Before confirming your next date, make sure you and the other person have answers to five questions.

  1. What are you doing? Choose a public place and activity that both people genuinely want to try.
  2. When and for how long? Agree on the day, starting time, and a manageable approximate duration.
  3. What will it cost? Know about significant expenses, and clarify payment expectations if needed.
  4. Will it work for both people? Consider noise, seating, mobility, food, accessibility, travel, and energy.
  5. What are the practical details? Confirm the address, arrival arrangements, independent transportation, and a reasonable alternative if plans change.

You can keep the plan in a calendar note or message. It does not need to become a complicated document.

For example:

“Saturday, 2 p.m., downtown museum. Meet at the main entrance. About an hour. $12 admission each. Both traveling separately. Café nearby if we both want to stay longer.”

That’s enough to turn a vague idea into something manageable.

It is not necessary to check every item on this list with the same level of detail. A familiar café needs less preparation than an unfamiliar event with tickets and transportation arrangements.

The point is to remove avoidable uncertainty, not to eliminate every surprise from life.

The date that works for you

There is a difference between planning an impressive date and planning a date where two people can actually connect.

An impressive date might involve expensive food, elaborate arrangements, and an activity carefully chosen to look romantic.

A date that works for you might be a walk, a museum visit, a cup of coffee, or something entirely different.

What matters is whether both people can freely participate, feel reasonably comfortable, and have an opportunity to get to know each other.

You don’t have to earn someone’s interest with the price of the activity. You don’t have to disguise your sensory needs or prove that you can tolerate an uncomfortable environment. You don’t have to provide a flawless experience.

You do need to consider the other person, communicate your own preferences, and make arrangements you can reasonably follow through on.

Once the time and place are agreed upon, the planning part is finished.

The next task is not to deliver the perfect performance. It is to show up, meet the person in front of you, and discover what it is like to spend time together.

That is what the next chapter is about.
