Chapter 12: Looking after yourself: hygiene, clothes and presentation

Imagine getting ready for a first date. You’ve chosen somewhere to meet, figured out how to get there, and decided what you’re going to wear. Then you notice a stain on your shirt. You can’t remember when you last washed your hair. Your favorite shoes have developed a smell you hadn’t noticed before.

You might feel embarrassed. You might wonder whether everyone else learned some secret set of rules that you missed.

There is no secret. But there are some ordinary skills worth learning, and nobody should have to guess what they are.

Looking after your appearance isn’t about becoming conventionally attractive, buying expensive clothes, or turning yourself into someone else. It’s about taking reasonable care of your body and presenting yourself in a way that is clean, comfortable, and honest.

You cannot control whether another person finds you attractive. You can control whether you’ve brushed your teeth, whether your clothes are reasonably clean, and whether you’ve given yourself enough time to get ready.

That distinction matters.

Dating is about finding a mutual fit, not earning someone’s approval. Taking care of yourself can help remove avoidable distractions so that two people can concentrate on getting to know each other.

And if some of these ordinary tasks are surprisingly difficult, there are ways to approach them without treating difficulty as a personal failure.

What people notice, and what they don’t know

When you meet somebody, they encounter more than your face.

They might notice your clothing, hair, posture, voice, or smell. They may notice whether you look comfortable or whether your shoes seem appropriate for the place you’re visiting.

Smell, in particular, can influence how people perceive one another. A review of experimental studies found that odors, including perfume and body odor, can affect social judgments. But the effects depend on the circumstances, and many experiments don’t resemble an actual date (Spence, 2021).

There is no scientifically established fragrance that makes somebody want to date you. There is no magic outfit or grooming routine that guarantees attraction.

And first impressions aren’t always fair.

A systematic review of studies involving brief recordings and images found that observers, on average, formed less favorable initial impressions of autistic people than non-autistic people. Those differences were generally not found when observers evaluated written transcripts without the same visual or vocal information (Wanigasekera et al., 2025).

That finding says something important about how observers interpret social presentation. It does not establish that autistic people are less appealing as partners, and it certainly doesn’t establish that they have poorer hygiene.

Someone might misread a person’s natural expression, movement, or tone of voice. Washing more carefully won’t eliminate that misunderstanding. Nor should somebody have to suppress their natural mannerisms to deserve a relationship.

The useful goal is narrower: take care of the things you can reasonably maintain, while allowing yourself to remain recognizable as yourself.

Getting clean without making it complicated

Basic hygiene means regularly cleaning your body and paying attention to areas where sweat, dirt, and odor collect. The CDC includes washing the body and hair, cleaning hands, and caring for nails and the face among ordinary personal hygiene practices (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025).

You don’t need an elaborate collection of products.

Start with the basics.

Washing your body

Before a date, consider whether your body needs washing, especially areas that tend to collect sweat or odor, such as your armpits, feet, and groin.

Use a method that cleans your skin without causing pain or irritation. Dry yourself afterward, paying attention to areas that remain damp.

A shower works for many people, but it isn’t the only possible way to clean your body. A washcloth and a sink or basin can help when a full shower isn’t manageable.

Think of a shorter wash as one option for taking care of immediate needs, not as a universal replacement for bathing.

There isn’t one shower schedule that suits everybody. Your activity level, body, climate, skin, and access to facilities all affect what you need.

If your skin becomes dry or irritated, washing more aggressively may make things worse. Dermatologists recommend shorter warm showers, gentle cleansing, and fragrance-free products for dry or sensitive skin (American Academy of Dermatology Association, n.d.).

The objective is clean skin, not skin that has been scrubbed until it hurts.

Understanding body odor

Sweat and body odor are related, but they aren’t identical.

Body odor can develop when substances in sweat interact with bacteria on the skin. Washing and laundering clothes can help manage it. Deodorants are designed to address odor, while antiperspirants reduce sweating (Mayo Clinic Staff, 2025).

You can use either, both, or neither depending on your needs. The point is to find an approach that works for your body, not to collect products because advertisements say you should.

Clothing matters, too. A freshly washed body can quickly pick up odor from a shirt that has been worn repeatedly without laundering.

If you aren’t sure whether something smells, don’t assume that adding fragrance will solve it. Wash the clothing when possible, allow it to dry properly, and use a fresh item.

And remember that people have different sensitivities to smells. A fragrance you barely notice might be unpleasant to somebody sitting beside you.

When you’re uncertain, little or no added fragrance is a reasonable first-date choice.

A change in your normal body odor or sweating, especially if it is unexplained or persistent, is something you can discuss with a healthcare professional (Mayo Clinic Staff, 2025). It isn’t something you need to conceal with increasingly strong products.

Your teeth and breath deserve attention

Dental care is one of those adult responsibilities that can be easy to neglect until something goes wrong.

A basic routine doesn’t need to be complicated.

The CDC recommends brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between your teeth, and getting regular dental care. The American Dental Association recommends brushing for two minutes, twice daily, and using an appropriate method to clean between teeth (American Dental Association, n.d.; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

That means brushing your teeth in the morning and before bed is a useful everyday habit, not simply something to remember when you have a date.

Before meeting someone, brushing can also help you feel ready to spend time talking at close distance.

A breath mint might make your mouth feel fresher for a while, but it isn’t a replacement for cleaning your teeth or getting needed dental care.

If you have persistent unpleasant breath, tooth pain, or bleeding gums, a dentist can help you understand what needs attention. You don’t need to diagnose the cause yourself.

If brushing is uncomfortable

Perhaps toothpaste burns your mouth. The sound or vibration of an electric toothbrush feels intolerable. Flossing is frustrating because the movements are difficult to coordinate. Or you keep intending to brush but forget until you’re already in bed.

These are problems to solve, not evidence that you don’t care about yourself.

Different brushes, toothpaste products, and between-teeth cleaning tools can make the task more manageable. The ADA specifically supports selecting an appropriate interdental cleaning method for the individual (American Dental Association, n.d.).

Research into oral-care interventions for autistic people also suggests that visual preparation and structured routines can sometimes help, although the evidence remains preliminary and almost all the included studies focused on children rather than adults (Floríndez et al., 2025).

You might experiment with putting your toothbrush somewhere visible, choosing a tolerable product, or following the same short sequence each night.

If a particular sensation makes brushing painful, discuss alternatives with a dentist rather than simply forcing yourself through it.

The goal is a routine you can actually maintain.

Hair care doesn’t have one universal schedule

People have different hair textures, scalp conditions, styles, and preferences. What works for somebody with fine, oily hair may not work for somebody with thick, curly, coily, or dry hair.

Dermatologists recommend deciding how often to shampoo based on hair type and scalp oiliness. Some people benefit from frequent shampooing. Others, especially those with drier hair textures, may need it less often (American Academy of Dermatology Association, 2024).

So don’t assume that preparing for a date means washing your hair that day.

Think instead about whether your hair and scalp are cared for according to your needs.

You might wear your hair loose, braided, tied back, covered, cropped short, or styled in another way. A simple, familiar hairstyle that feels comfortable is perfectly acceptable.

You don’t need an expensive haircut. You don’t need hair that moves or looks like somebody else’s. You don’t need to change your natural texture.

If you’re uncertain about your hair-care routine, a knowledgeable barber, stylist, or dermatologist can help you find one suited to your hair and scalp.

The same principle applies to facial hair.

If you have a beard or mustache, consider whether you want to clean, comb, or trim it before meeting someone. Shaving is optional. Having facial hair is optional. The decision is about your preferred appearance and care, not about meeting a universal standard.

Perspective: Men

If you were taught very little about grooming, you may know how to shave but not how to care for a beard, manage your hair texture, or choose products that don’t irritate your skin. It’s reasonable to ask a barber or stylist to explain the basics. There is no requirement to look rugged, polished, clean-shaven, or conventionally masculine. Choose an appearance you can comfortably maintain.

Clothes: clean, comfortable, and appropriate for the occasion

Clothes don’t need to be expensive to work well on a date.

A clean T-shirt and jeans may be entirely suitable for coffee or a casual walk. A restaurant with a dress code may call for something different.

The first question isn’t, “Will this make me look impressive?”

It’s, “Does this work for where I’m going, and do I feel comfortable wearing it?”

What does it mean for clothes to fit?

People sometimes talk about wearing clothes that “fit” as though everyone should aim for the same body shape or style.

That’s not useful.

For everyday dating, fitting means that your clothing allows you to sit, stand, walk, and move comfortably. It doesn’t repeatedly slide out of place, restrict your breathing, or demand your attention throughout the date.

A loose shirt can fit. A close-fitting shirt can fit. A long skirt, suit, jumpsuit, or pair of jeans can all work.

Choose according to your comfort, the activity, and how you want to present yourself.

It’s also worth checking practical details.

A shirt might look fine from the front but have a food stain near the hem. A pair of shoes might be comfortable at home but painful after twenty minutes of walking. A jacket may be too warm for an indoor restaurant.

Try the outfit before the date if you’re uncertain. Sit down, walk around, and see whether anything bothers you.

These aren’t beauty rules. They’re ways to avoid spending an entire date adjusting your clothing.

Clean matters more than new

You don’t need a separate wardrobe for dating.

Repeating an outfit is fine. Wearing thrifted clothes is fine. Wearing something you’ve repaired is fine. What matters for basic presentation is that it’s reasonably clean, comfortable, and suited to the setting.

If you have limited money, begin with what you already own.

You may find that one or two dependable outfits are enough for most first dates. Choose items you can wash and wear again without an elaborate preparation process.

If laundry is difficult because of money, housing, disability, or access to facilities, focus on realistic options rather than an imaginary standard of perfection.

What someone can afford tells you very little about how considerate or interesting they might be as a partner.

Perspective: Women

If you’ve been told that getting ready for a date requires makeup, elaborate hairstyling, shaved body hair, or uncomfortable shoes, you can reject that requirement. Those are choices, not basic hygiene. Wearing cosmetics because you enjoy them is different from feeling obligated to change your face to be acceptable. Your date should meet someone you recognize in the mirror.

Perspective: Non-binary

If clothing stores divide everything into categories that don’t reflect how you see yourself, getting dressed may involve an extra decision about how openly you want to express your gender. You don’t owe a new date a particular presentation, and you don’t have to dress in a way that compromises your comfort or safety. Choose clothing that feels right for you in that setting.

When ordinary grooming feels difficult

For some people, getting ready is mostly a matter of time.

For others, it involves navigating sensations that are unpleasant, distracting, or even painful.

The shower might be too loud. The water might feel sharp against your skin. The bathroom light might be unbearable first thing in the morning.

A towel may feel rough. A clothing label may scrape against your neck for hours. The scent of laundry detergent might remain noticeable long after your clothes have dried.

These experiences aren’t imaginary.

In a small study of autistic adults, participants described varied reactions to textures, sounds, and smells. Their preferences often depended on predictability, context, and control (MacLennan et al., 2022).

Another study involving ten autistic university students described how tags, seams, fabrics, and restrictive clothing could affect comfort and daily activities (Kyriacou et al., 2023).

Sensory differences also appear in studies involving people with ADHD, although those findings vary widely and do not mean everyone with ADHD experiences the same difficulties (Jurek et al., 2025).

None of these studies identifies one perfect bathing routine, outfit, or product. They do, however, support taking sensory discomfort seriously.

Change the obstacle, not your entire personality

Suppose you dislike showering because the sudden temperature change is unpleasant.

The problem might not be washing. It might be the transition from dry skin to wet skin and back again.

You could try warming the bathroom beforehand, testing the water temperature before entering, or preparing a towel and clean clothes in advance.

If the problem is sound, consider whether a quieter showerhead or different washing method would help.

If strongly scented soap bothers you, try a fragrance-free option.

If clothing seams cause continual discomfort, choose a different construction or fabric. Removing a bothersome label may help, provided doing so won’t damage the garment.

These are practical experiments, not guaranteed treatments.

You can adjust one thing at a time and see what happens.

If washing or clothing causes sensory distress

You don’t have to pretend a sensation is comfortable just because somebody else barely notices it. Try identifying the exact part of the task that bothers you: temperature, pressure, sound, smell, texture, or the transition between activities.

Build your routine around what you can tolerate. If the difficulty is seriously interfering with health or daily living, an occupational therapist or another appropriate professional may be able to help you explore options. A partner can understand your needs without requiring you to prove how unpleasant a sensation feels.

When getting started is the hardest part

Sometimes you don’t particularly dislike brushing your teeth or taking a shower. You just struggle to begin.

You may lose track of time. You might become absorbed in something else. You know what needs to happen, but the sequence of getting supplies, changing clothes, washing, drying, and putting everything away feels like a lot.

Instead of relying on motivation at exactly the right moment, simplify the process.

Put the supplies where you’ll use them. Keep a clean towel ready. Decide on your clothes the previous evening. Give yourself a reminder before you need to leave.

If you’ve never managed a consistent routine, begin with one repeatable action rather than designing a complicated schedule that collapses after two days.

These are organizational suggestions, not research-proven dating interventions.

If you mean to get ready but time disappears

A date at six doesn’t mean you should start getting ready at 5:45. Work backward from the time you need to leave, and include time for washing, dressing, finding belongings, and unexpected interruptions.

A reminder labeled “start getting ready” may be more useful than one labeled “date tonight.” Put it at the point when you need to begin, not when you’re already supposed to be leaving.

A minimum-care option for difficult days

Some days you may have little energy, limited facilities, or an unusually difficult time with sensory input.

When a complete routine isn’t manageable, try a smaller one.

You could wash the areas of your body that need immediate attention, dry yourself, brush your teeth, and change into clean underwear, socks, and clothes.

Then make a plan for any care you still need to complete.

This isn’t a claim that a brief wash replaces every other hygiene need. It’s a way of making a difficult task more manageable without deciding that doing nothing is the only alternative.

If even a short routine regularly feels impossible, you deserve support in understanding why.

You don’t need to wait until dating becomes a problem to ask for that support.

Prepare for the person you’re actually going to meet

Imagine that Jordan, 38, is getting ready to meet Taylor for coffee.

Jordan has picked out a new shirt that looks great but feels scratchy around the collar. Wearing it makes Jordan tense and distracted.

There’s also a familiar shirt in the closet. It’s clean, comfortable, and looks perfectly appropriate for the café.

Jordan chooses the familiar shirt.

That’s a reasonable decision.

If your clothing causes continual discomfort, you may spend the whole date thinking about your body instead of listening to the person across from you.

A date isn’t an examination of how much discomfort you can tolerate to look impressive.

The same goes for makeup, accessories, footwear, and fragrance.

You can make an effort without creating a costume you cannot comfortably wear.

And you don’t have to conceal sensory preferences from somebody you’re getting to know.

For example:

“I’d prefer somewhere that isn’t too loud. It’s easier for me to have a conversation.”

Or:

“Strong fragrances bother me. Would you be comfortable skipping perfume or cologne when we meet?”

These are requests. The other person can explain their needs or preferences, too.

If something matters to both people, the next step is to see whether there’s a workable arrangement, not to declare one person’s senses correct and the other’s incorrect.

If your date wears the same clothes or avoids scented products

Repeating a favorite outfit doesn’t automatically mean someone isn’t making an effort. They may prefer its texture, weight, fit, or predictability. Similarly, avoiding fragrance can be a matter of comfort rather than indifference to presentation.

You can still have your own preferences about cleanliness and shared spaces. Ask respectfully about the specific issue rather than assuming what it means. The goal is to understand each other, not to become somebody else’s grooming supervisor.

Presenting yourself honestly in photos

How you present yourself online is part of the same conversation.

Dating profiles often encourage you to choose photographs that show you at your best. That’s reasonable. The difficulty begins when “at your best” becomes “no longer recognizable.”

Two early studies examined inaccuracies in online dating profiles and photographs.

One study of 80 dating-site users found generally small differences between some profile claims and independently measured characteristics, while participants regarded their photographs as less accurate than other parts of their profiles (Toma et al., 2008).

Another study examined photographs from 54 heterosexual online daters and found that users and independent observers did not always agree about how accurately the photographs represented them (Hancock & Toma, 2009).

These studies were small and conducted long before today’s dating apps, sophisticated filters, and AI image editing. They don’t tell us how common misleading photographs are now.

But they illustrate a practical question worth asking:

If somebody met you tomorrow, would they recognize the person in your photographs?

A flattering photograph can still be honest.

You might choose a picture where you’re smiling naturally, wearing an outfit you like, or standing somewhere meaningful to you. Good lighting and a pleasant setting aren’t deception.

Consider using reasonably recent photographs that show your current appearance.

A clear face photograph is helpful. Another picture might show your ordinary style or an activity you enjoy. You don’t need an elaborate photo session or an expensive camera.

Avoid making substantial changes to your appearance that would create a false expectation.

If your hair, body, or style has changed considerably since a photograph was taken, consider replacing it.

You also don’t owe strangers pictures revealing more of your body than you’re comfortable showing.

The purpose isn’t to give everyone unrestricted access to your appearance. It’s to provide a truthful introduction that respects your privacy.

What about looking different on a first date?

Maybe you wore makeup in your profile pictures but don’t feel like wearing it to coffee. Maybe your hair looks different because you’ve recently cut it. Maybe you usually wear glasses but took them off for a photograph.

That’s normal variation.

You don’t have to look identical every day.

Honest presentation means not deliberately creating a fundamentally false impression. It doesn’t require you to document every possible hairstyle, outfit, or ordinary change in your appearance.

And if you use a photo simply because you like how you look in it, that doesn’t make you dishonest.

Looking after yourself isn’t the same as hiding yourself

There’s a difference between making yourself presentable and masking.

You might wash your hair, change your clothes, and put on clean shoes because you want to feel prepared.

You might also find yourself rehearsing every movement, suppressing a comfortable posture, wearing painful clothing, or pretending to enjoy a scent you dislike because you’re afraid somebody will judge you.

Those are different kinds of effort.

Basic self-care helps you meet someone as yourself. Hiding or suppressing natural traits to fit in can make it harder for someone to understand the person they’re meeting.

Think back to the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book. People can look at the same person and come away with different impressions. You can take care with your presentation, but you can’t control every interpretation.

Somebody who dislikes your hairstyle may love your humor.

Someone else might appreciate your clothing but find that your everyday habits don’t match theirs.

Neither reaction determines your worth.

Your responsibility is to offer a reasonably honest introduction and treat the other person with consideration.

Their responsibility is to decide freely whether they want to continue getting to know you.

A first-date checklist you can actually use

You don’t need to spend hours preparing. For many casual dates, a short check before leaving is enough.

Use this as a practical checklist rather than a test you must pass perfectly.

Before leaving

You can keep the checklist on your phone and adjust it as you learn what works.

If you routinely forget one step, put a reminder next to the point where you usually need it.

For example, if you often reach the door before noticing your shirt is stained, keep a spare clean shirt ready. If you forget to brush before leaving, put a reminder where you prepare your keys and wallet.

The checklist is a tool. It isn’t a scientific predictor of whether your date will go well.

The ten-minute version

Suppose your date is approaching and you have ten minutes to prepare.

Start with the essentials: wash any areas needing immediate attention, brush your teeth, change into clean clothes, and check that you have what you need to leave.

If you already have a longer routine, keep using it when there’s time.

If not, this shorter sequence gives you a starting point for developing one.

If you’re running late, tell the other person rather than silently disappearing while you try to make everything perfect.

And if you aren’t feeling well enough or don’t have what you need to get ready, it’s reasonable to discuss rescheduling.

A first date doesn’t have to take place at the expense of your health.

When someone else’s hygiene bothers you

Looking after yourself is only half of the situation. You may also date someone whose hygiene routines differ from yours.

Imagine that Nina, 46, has been seeing Chris for a few weeks.

Chris is thoughtful, funny, and enjoyable to spend time with. But Nina has noticed that Chris sometimes arrives wearing clothes with a strong, stale odor.

Nina isn’t sure what to do. She doesn’t want to embarrass Chris, but the smell makes close physical contact uncomfortable.

She has several choices.

She can decide whether this is something she wants to discuss. If she does, she can raise it privately, calmly, and specifically rather than making jokes or criticizing Chris’s character.

For example:

“I like spending time with you, but I’ve noticed a strong smell on your clothes a few times. Is that something you’re aware of?”

Chris might appreciate knowing. There could also be an explanation Nina hasn’t considered, including limited access to laundry, difficulty noticing odors, a health concern, or something else.

Nina can listen without taking responsibility for managing Chris’s personal care.

Chris, in turn, can decide what to do with the information.

Neither person needs to be humiliated or controlled.

Sometimes an issue can be resolved through a small practical change. Sometimes two people’s needs won’t fit comfortably together.

It is possible to communicate a concern respectfully and still decide that physical closeness isn’t something you want.

And if somebody tells you that your hygiene makes them uncomfortable, you don’t have to accept insults. But it’s worth considering whether the feedback identifies something you can reasonably address.

A relationship doesn’t require identical personal-care routines. It does require room for both people’s comfort and dignity.

If you’ve never learned these skills

Perhaps nobody taught you how to choose shampoo for your hair, how often to change your clothes, what deodorant does, or how to establish an oral-care routine.

Maybe your household didn’t have consistent routines. Maybe access to supplies was unreliable. Maybe you were criticized about your appearance without being shown what to do differently.

Or perhaps the usual advice never accounted for your sensory needs, disability, or energy.

Whatever the reason, you can start with what is useful now.

You don’t need to overhaul your entire appearance or buy a cabinet full of products.

Choose one thing that would make everyday care easier.

It might be a toothbrush you can tolerate. A set of clean clothes kept ready for going out. A gentler soap. A laundry reminder. A haircut that requires less maintenance.

Try that change for a while. Notice whether it makes the routine easier to complete.

Then choose another, if you need one.

Learning ordinary self-care is an adult skill, just like cooking a basic meal, keeping an appointment, or managing laundry. You can learn it at 18, 35, 60, or any age.

You don’t have to feel embarrassed about starting.

Good enough is enough to begin

You don’t need perfect teeth, flawless skin, fashionable clothes, or a professionally styled haircut to date.

You don’t need to smell like perfume, hide every sign of aging, or make your body look different from the one you actually have.

You do need a reasonable way of caring for yourself, adjusted to your circumstances.

Pay attention to cleanliness, breath, clothing, comfort, and honest presentation. Get professional help when a health concern or persistent difficulty needs it. Treat personal care as something worth doing for your own well-being, not merely as an attempt to impress another person.

Then allow yourself to be seen.

A date is not an inspection. Your clothes should be comfortable enough to let you sit and talk. Your grooming routine should leave you feeling prepared, not exhausted. Your photographs should introduce somebody the other person can actually meet.

The aim is not to eliminate every possible reason someone might reject you. That’s impossible.

The aim is to give both people an honest opportunity to discover whether they enjoy each other’s company.
