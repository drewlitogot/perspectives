Chapter 31: Touch, space and what's appropriate

Chapter 31 · about 25 minutes

Jules arrives at a coffee shop for a first date with Amir. They’ve been messaging for two weeks, and the conversation has been easy. Amir smiles and opens his arms for a hug. Jules smiles back but stays where they are.

Amir pauses. “Would you rather skip the hug?”

“Yeah, thanks. I’m not much of a hugger when I first meet someone.”

“Okay. Good to meet you.”

They sit down and order coffee.

Nothing went wrong. Amir offered something friendly, Jules had a different preference, and they adjusted. Neither had to explain their personality, apologize for existing, or pretend to enjoy something.

Now imagine Amir had stepped forward and hugged Jules anyway. Perhaps he’d thought that agreeing to a date meant a hug was expected. Perhaps Jules would have stood still, unsure how to respond. Amir might have walked away thinking it was a perfectly ordinary greeting, while Jules remembered being touched without wanting it.

This is why physical boundaries matter even during small, everyday interactions.

You can find someone attractive, enjoy their company, and want to see them again without wanting them to touch you. You can also enjoy physical affection without wanting it everywhere, every time, or from everyone.

The earlier chapters explained what boundaries and consent mean. Here, those ideas become practical: where to stand, whether to hug, what to say about someone’s appearance, when looking becomes intrusive, and what to do when someone seems uncomfortable.

The guiding question is not “Can you get away with this?” It is “Does this person have a genuine choice about what happens to their body and their space?”

The space around a person belongs to them, too

Personal space is the area around your body where another person’s closeness may begin to feel uncomfortable or intrusive.

There is no universal distance that feels right to everyone.

A study involving 8,943 people across 42 countries found that preferred distances differed depending on whether the other person was a stranger, acquaintance, or someone close. Preferences also varied across countries and individual characteristics (Sorokowska et al., 2017).

That means a rule such as “Always stand three feet away” would be misleading.

You might enjoy sitting close to someone you love but need considerable space around strangers. Someone else might enjoy crowded concerts yet dislike a person leaning over their shoulder. Another person might be comfortable standing close during conversation but uncomfortable being touched.

None of those preferences is automatically wrong.

How to give someone space

When you’re getting to know someone, begin with enough room that neither person feels crowded. Leave space for them to move, turn, sit, or walk away.

Some practical habits help:

  • When speaking, avoid leaning directly into the person’s face.
  • Don’t block doorways, paths, or the person’s way out.
  • If someone moves farther away, allow the distance to remain.
  • Don’t repeatedly move closer after they have created more space.
  • When choosing somewhere to sit, let the other person have a say.

A person stepping away does not necessarily mean they dislike you. They might need more room, hear better from a different position, or simply prefer a little distance.

You do not need to determine the exact reason. You can respect the space without knowing it.

Suppose Elena and Marcus are talking after dinner. Elena steps back while Marcus is explaining a funny incident at work. Marcus notices and stops moving forward. The conversation continues.

There is no need for Marcus to demand an explanation. He also doesn’t need to assume he’s been rejected.

He can simply give Elena the room she appears to want.

A person’s movement can tell you to pause or give space. It cannot reliably tell you that you’re allowed to move closer or touch them.

If you’re uncertain, asking is an option. So is maintaining the distance you’re already at.

When your sense of distance differs

Some people don’t automatically notice when they’re standing closer than another person prefers.

In one study, parents reported that autistic children and adolescents were more likely than their siblings to stand unusually close to others. A separate, small laboratory study found that some autistic adults responded differently to personal-space boundaries, although responses varied (Kennedy & Adolphs, 2014).

This does not mean autistic people all stand too close. Some prefer considerable distance themselves.

It means that the distance one person experiences as ordinary might feel intrusive to another.

If someone asks you to move back, moving back is enough. You don’t need to defend the distance you chose or feel ashamed for not noticing earlier.

And if you’re the person who needs more room, you can say so.

“Could you give me a little more space? I can hear you fine from here.”

You aren’t asking the other person to agree that your distance is the correct one. You’re telling them what feels comfortable to you.

If you don't automatically notice personal-space cues

You may find distance easier to understand through direct words than through subtle movement. You can tell someone, “I don’t always notice when I’m standing too close. It’s okay to tell me if you need more room.”

That invitation is not permission for the other person to criticize or embarrass you. It gives both of you a clearer way to communicate. If they ask for space, adjust. If they don’t say anything, avoid assuming that silence means every distance is welcome.

Hugs are invitations, not obligations

A hug can mean hello, goodbye, comfort, celebration, friendship, or romantic affection.

The meaning changes with the people involved.

One person hugs almost everyone they know. Another reserves hugs for close friends and partners. Someone else enjoys hugs only when they have time to prepare for them.

A European study involving 1,368 participants found that people generally permitted touch across more areas of their bodies as their emotional closeness with another person increased. Permission also varied by body area (Suvilehto et al., 2015).

But this study measured people’s reported preferences, not actual consent on American first dates. It does not establish that touching someone’s hand, arm, or shoulder is automatically welcome.

A useful distinction is:

Something being socially common doesn’t make it personally permitted.

Greeting someone on a date

When you meet someone, you have several perfectly acceptable options.

You can smile and say hello. You can wave. You can offer a hug by asking first.

“Would you like a hug?”

If the answer is yes, a brief hug may be welcome. If the answer is no, simply greet them another way.

“No problem. It’s good to see you.”

A person who declines a hug has not necessarily rejected you romantically. They declined a hug.

Likewise, someone who accepts a greeting hug has not agreed to holding hands, kissing, or any other contact.

Consent is specific. RAINN’s guidance emphasizes that agreement must be freely given, can change, and does not carry automatically from one activity to another (RAINN, 2026).

During the date

Perhaps you’re walking together and would like to hold hands.

You can ask.

“Would you like to hold hands?”

“I’m happy just walking for now.”

“Sure.”

Then continue walking.

Notice that the answer doesn’t require a lengthy conversation. The person offering contact isn’t entitled to reassurance, and the person declining doesn’t owe an explanation.

You might also want to put an arm around someone, touch their back, or rest your hand on their knee.

These are different kinds of touch, and the other person may feel differently about each one.

A hand on someone’s lower back may feel affectionate to one person and intrusive to another. A person might enjoy holding hands but dislike being touched unexpectedly.

Don’t use one accepted touch as permission for the next.

Especially while getting to know someone, a straightforward question is more reliable than trying to determine permission from a smile, a laugh, or the absence of resistance.

At the end of the date

A goodbye hug is optional, even if the date went wonderfully.

“Would a hug be okay, or would you rather just say goodnight?”

Either answer can end a good date.

If someone declines, you can still tell them you enjoyed their company. If they accept, don’t treat that as a promise about the next date.

A good evening doesn’t need to end with physical contact to count as successful.

If you touch someone without asking

Sometimes you misjudge a situation. You place a hand on someone’s shoulder and they pull away. You greet them with a hug before realizing they didn’t want one.

Stop the contact and give them space.

You might say, “Sorry. I should have asked.”

Then let them decide whether to continue the interaction.

The apology should not turn into a long explanation about why you thought the touch would be welcome. The person who was uncomfortable should not have to reassure you that you’re a good person.

An apology matters most when your behavior changes afterward.

Accidental contact is different. If you bump someone in a crowded café, a simple apology and making room is usually enough. The goal is not to turn every ordinary movement into a formal negotiation. It is to avoid assuming access to another person’s body.

Touch preferences can change with the situation

Consider two people who have been dating for several months.

At home, Ren loves lying beside their girlfriend, Sofia, while they watch television. Ren enjoys long hugs and sometimes initiates them.

At a crowded restaurant, however, Ren becomes uncomfortable when Sofia unexpectedly wraps an arm around them.

Sofia is confused. “You like cuddling at home. I thought you liked being touched.”

Ren answers, “I do. Just not when there’s this much noise and so many people around.”

Both statements can be true.

A 2026 study of 31 autistic and 43 non-autistic adults found that autistic participants reported less preference for social touch on average, but similar overall frequency and satisfaction with touch. Intimate touch with a partner was experienced more similarly across groups than some other social-touch situations. Predictability and sensory experiences were relevant to differences in touch preferences (Li et al., 2026).

A separate qualitative study found that sensory experiences could make intimacy either enjoyable or difficult for autistic people. Participants described both seeking and avoiding sensory experiences, including touch (Gray et al., 2021).

These studies don’t establish a single autistic preference. They show why it is a mistake to think someone either “likes touch” or “doesn’t like touch,” as though that describes every situation.

If unexpected touch feels different from wanted touch

You might love firm hugs from someone you trust but dislike light, unexpected contact. You might enjoy affection in a quiet room but find the same contact unpleasant when you’re already dealing with noise, bright lights, or overload.

You can make your preferences specific: “I like hugs when I know they’re coming,” or “Please don’t touch my back unexpectedly.” You don’t need to give a diagnosis or justify the sensation. Your preference is enough.

If you're dating someone who needs warning before touch

Your partner may enjoy affection while needing more control over when and how it happens. Instead of interpreting a declined hug as a judgment of your relationship, ask what kinds of touch they enjoy and when.

You also have preferences worth discussing. Perhaps physical affection matters to you, while unexpected touch is difficult for your partner. Neither person’s needs automatically cancel the other’s. The task is to find forms of closeness both people genuinely enjoy, not to convince someone to tolerate contact they dislike.

Public and private are different, but consent still applies

A touch that feels comfortable in private may feel uncomfortable in public.

Imagine two women, Maya and Denise, on their third date. They have held hands while watching movies at Maya’s apartment.

Later, while walking through a busy shopping district, Maya reaches for Denise’s hand.

Denise pulls her hand away.

Maya feels hurt at first. When they have privacy, she asks whether Denise is comfortable holding hands in public.

Denise explains that she isn’t ready for strangers to recognize their relationship.

Maya may wish things were different. She may also need to decide whether that difference works for her. But Denise’s earlier willingness to hold hands at home doesn’t give Maya permission to do it everywhere.

Public affection can carry considerations beyond the touch itself.

A person might be concerned about privacy, safety, cultural expectations, their family seeing them, or whether they want other people to know they’re dating someone.

Another person might feel more comfortable with affection in public than in private.

You cannot know which applies without talking about it.

And being in private doesn’t mean a person is willing to be touched more intimately. A closed door, an invitation inside, or time alone together changes the setting, not the consent requirement.

A useful conversation might be:

“Are you comfortable holding hands when we’re out?”

“Sometimes, but I’d rather decide depending on where we are.”

“Okay. I’ll ask.”

This isn’t a contract for every future outing. It’s a better understanding of the person’s preference.

Perspective: Non-binary

Public affection can raise questions about privacy and how other people may interpret a relationship. You may be comfortable with your partner while not wanting attention, questions, or assumptions from strangers.

You don’t have to disclose your gender, relationship, or personal history to justify a limit on public touch. A partner can respect the boundary even if they don’t face the same considerations themselves.

Compliments are not invitations to evaluate someone’s body

Attraction is part of dating. It’s fine to notice that someone looks beautiful, handsome, stylish, or appealing.

The question is how you express that attraction.

There is a difference between appreciating someone’s appearance and turning their body into an object for your commentary.

Consider these comments on an early date:

“That jacket looks great on you.”

“I really like your style.”

“You look lovely tonight.”

These express appreciation without demanding anything.

Now imagine someone repeatedly commenting on a date’s chest, legs, weight, or other personal physical features. The recipient might feel watched or evaluated rather than appreciated.

You may mean the remarks as compliments. The other person may experience them as intrusive.

Your intention does not determine whether someone welcomes the comment.

Early in dating, a useful starting point is to compliment things people have chosen or shared: their clothing, sense of humor, ideas, enthusiasm, or the activity you’re enjoying together.

This is practical guidance, not a universal rule that appearance compliments are always safe or body compliments are always unwelcome.

As two people get to know each other, they can discover what kinds of romantic compliments each enjoys.

One person might love hearing that their partner finds them attractive. Another might dislike attention to their body, even from someone they are dating.

The distinction matters especially when the relationship is new and you don’t yet know the other person’s preferences.

When someone comments on your body

You’re allowed to dislike a comment even if the other person calls it flattering.

You might say:

“Please don’t comment on my weight.”

“I’d rather not talk about my body.”

“That comment made me uncomfortable.”

You can also leave the conversation.

Someone who responds respectfully may have made a mistake and learned from it. Someone who dismisses your discomfort or continues the comments is showing you something important about how they treat boundaries.

You don’t have to persuade them that your reaction is reasonable.

Looking at someone is different from watching them

Looking at someone while they’re speaking is ordinary. Looking away is ordinary, too.

Staring is harder to define. For this chapter, it means sustained, unwanted visual attention that makes another person feel watched rather than included in an interaction.

There is no scientifically established number of seconds that separates acceptable eye contact from staring on a date in the research used here.

Context matters.

A person listening carefully might look directly at you. Someone concentrating might stare into space. Someone uncomfortable with eye contact might look at the table while following every word.

None of those behaviors reliably reveals romantic interest, dishonesty, or danger.

What matters for boundaries is whether the attention is welcome and whether the person respects another’s discomfort.

You don’t have to maintain constant eye contact

Some people find eye contact distracting, tiring, or too intense. Others use it naturally while listening.

If you look away to concentrate, you can explain that if you want:

“I listen better when I’m not looking directly at someone.”

You don’t need to force an unnatural expression or stare at someone’s eyes to prove you’re interested.

Similarly, if another person doesn’t make much eye contact, don’t assume they dislike you.

The question is whether your interaction is respectful, not whether both people perform the same gestures.

When looking becomes intrusive

Suppose you’re sitting in a café and notice an attractive stranger.

Noticing them is one thing. Continuing to watch them when they’re not engaging with you is another.

The person’s appearance doesn’t make their attention available to you.

On a date, repeatedly examining someone’s body can also create discomfort. Your date might wonder whether you’re listening to them or simply evaluating how they look.

If someone tells you they feel watched, or clearly tries to get away from your attention, stop.

A useful principle is to direct your attention toward a mutual conversation, not toward monitoring another person.

What does “creepy” actually mean?

“Creepy” is a word people sometimes use when someone’s behavior makes them uneasy, particularly when they are uncertain about the person’s intentions.

But the word also carries stereotypes. It should not be treated as a reliable way to identify a dangerous person.

In an exploratory study involving 1,341 online volunteers, researchers asked what characteristics people associated with someone they considered creepy.

Participants frequently associated creepiness with behaviors such as standing too close, touching someone often, watching someone before approaching, asking intrusive personal questions, and steering conversation toward sex (McAndrew & Koehnke, 2016).

These behaviors share an important feature: they can interfere with another person’s comfort and freedom to choose whether to engage.

The same participants also associated creepiness with things such as limited eye contact, unexpected laughter, unusual facial expressions, intense single-topic conversation, and mental illness.

Those are not reliable indicators that someone is unsafe.

The study measured people’s stereotypes, not whether the people they described were actually dangerous. The sample was self-selected, and its results should not become a checklist for judging strangers.

Some people are judged unfairly

In a series of laboratory studies, non-autistic observers formed less favorable first impressions of autistic people after brief exposure to audiovisual material. The difference disappeared when observers evaluated only the words in conversational transcripts (Sasson et al., 2017).

That suggests people can react negatively to how someone communicates even when the content of the conversation isn’t the problem.

Another study found that first impressions of autistic adults improved when observers knew they were autistic, with greater observer knowledge also associated with more favorable impressions (Sasson & Morrison, 2019).

These studies were not conducted on dates. They cannot establish that autistic people are specifically perceived as creepy in dating situations.

They do, however, illustrate something important: being perceived as unusual is not the same as behaving harmfully.

You should not have to imitate another neurotype’s facial expressions, gestures, or eye contact to deserve respect.

At the same time, having difficulty reading social cues doesn’t remove the responsibility to respect another person’s boundaries.

These are two different issues.

If you avoid eye contact, speak enthusiastically about a favorite subject, or laugh unexpectedly, those differences do not make you dangerous.

If you repeatedly touch someone who doesn’t want contact, crowd their space, or refuse to leave them alone, those behaviors need to stop, regardless of your intentions or neurotype.

The goal is not to become someone nobody could ever call creepy. That is impossible, especially when people’s judgments can be biased.

The goal is to act in ways that respect other people’s choices.

If you’re worried about making someone uncomfortable

Focus on what you can control without changing who you are.

You can offer space. You can ask before touching. You can accept a refusal. You can let someone end a conversation without demanding that they continue.

You can also communicate your intentions directly instead of relying on the other person to interpret them.

These are ways to make an interaction clearer and more respectful. They are not methods for persuading someone who doesn’t want your attention.

A person may still decide they don’t want to date you. That’s their choice.

Likewise, you can decline to date someone without needing to label them strange, defective, or dangerous.

When interest is not returned

Finding someone attractive doesn’t mean they are interested in you.

Enjoying a conversation doesn’t mean they want physical contact.

Agreeing to one date doesn’t mean they want another.

Sometimes the answer is direct: “No, thank you.”

Other times someone creates distance, declines touch, ends the conversation, or doesn’t respond to a proposed date.

These situations don’t all tell you exactly what the other person is thinking. But they do tell you what you should not assume.

You cannot treat uncertainty as permission.

If someone says no to a hug, don’t hug them.

If they ask you to stop commenting on their appearance, stop.

If they end an interaction, let it end.

If they don’t respond to an invitation, don’t treat the silence as a reason to keep requesting their attention.

People can have many reasons for declining or not responding. You do not need to discover the reason before respecting the limit.

A 2020 review examining autism and stalking found very little empirical research on the subject. It is not a sound basis for treating autistic people as inherently more likely to pursue someone inappropriately (Mercer & Allely, 2020).

Regardless of neurotype, the practical responsibility is the same: interest must remain mutual.

The next chapter will examine persistence, harassment, and stalking more closely, including how ongoing unwanted attention can become harmful and what to do about it.

For now, remember that wanting a connection doesn’t give you a claim on another person’s time, attention, space, or body.

Unwanted touch and harassment are not rare exceptions

It can be tempting to think of unwanted attention as something that happens only in extreme situations.

National surveys show that unwelcome sexual contact and sexual comments are substantial problems in the United States.

The CDC’s 2023/2024 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey, published in December 2025, reported that 39.0% of women and 13.8% of men had experienced unwanted sexual contact during their lifetimes. The survey defined this as sexual contact without penetration, including experiences such as unwanted sexual kissing or touching (Leemis et al., 2025).

That statistic doesn’t mean every unwelcome hug or shoulder tap belongs in the same category.

The survey also found lifetime verbal sexual harassment in public places reported by 29.5% of women and 7.1% of men (Leemis et al., 2025).

These are self-reported survey estimates, not a prediction about an individual date. The survey used updated methods, so its findings should not be directly compared with older survey rounds as though the methods were identical.

A separate 2024 US study reported widespread sexual harassment and assault, including experiences among transgender, nonbinary, and gender-nonconforming respondents. Its categories and methods differed from those of the CDC, so the numerical estimates are not interchangeable (Raj et al., 2024).

This helps explain why some people approach new physical contact cautiously.

You cannot know what someone has experienced simply by looking at them. You also shouldn’t assume that an individual person is afraid or uncomfortable because of their gender.

But you can recognize that giving people control over contact matters.

Perspective: Women

US national surveys report higher lifetime rates of unwanted sexual contact and public verbal sexual harassment among women than men (Leemis et al., 2025). That is population-level information, not a statement about what every woman has experienced.

If a date makes you uncomfortable, you don’t have to prioritize politeness over leaving, creating space, or declining contact. You also don’t have to explain a personal history to justify a boundary.

Perspective: Men

Men also report unwanted sexual contact and harassment in national surveys (Leemis et al., 2025). Being a man does not mean you’re expected to welcome every hug, sexual comment, or physical advance.

You can refuse contact, change your mind, and ask someone to give you space. Your boundaries are not less important because another person assumes you should enjoy the attention.

What counts as harassment?

In everyday language, harassment can describe unwanted conduct that intrudes on someone’s safety, dignity, or freedom to participate in ordinary activities.

Legal definitions are more specific and depend on the setting and jurisdiction.

For example, the US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission explains that workplace sexual harassment can include unwelcome sexual advances, unwanted sexual conduct, and offensive remarks about sex. Federal workplace law applies particular legal standards concerning severity, frequency, and effects on employment (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, n.d.).

That guidance concerns covered workplaces. It does not create a general legal test for every interaction on a date.

State laws also vary, and different kinds of conduct can raise different legal questions.

You do not need to establish that someone’s behavior meets a legal threshold before deciding you don’t want it.

And when someone tells you that your attention or touch is unwelcome, you shouldn’t wait for a legal consequence before stopping.

Respecting a boundary is a basic requirement of dating, not merely a way to avoid breaking the law.

When you’re the one who feels uncomfortable

Suppose you’re on a date with someone who keeps placing a hand on your leg after you’ve moved it away.

You aren’t certain whether they’re being deliberately disrespectful or simply failing to notice.

You don’t have to determine their intentions before acting.

You can move away. You can say that you don’t want to be touched. You can end the date.

If speaking feels difficult, leaving or seeking help is also an option.

You are not required to have a confrontation, give a detailed explanation, or prove that the situation was serious enough.

You have several options

If you feel safe and want to say something, you might use a short statement:

“Please don’t touch me.”

“I need more space.”

“I’m not comfortable with that.”

“I’m going to leave now.”

If speaking directly feels unsafe, you can leave without announcing your decision.

You might move toward other people, contact someone you trust, or ask a staff member for assistance if that is available and safe.

If you are in immediate danger, seek emergency help as appropriate.

There isn’t one required response that works for every person and situation.

Someone may speak firmly. Another person may go quiet, become confused, or freeze.

Not speaking is not agreement. Not resisting is not permission. RAINN’s consent guidance makes clear that silence and freezing do not establish consent (RAINN, 2026).

You don’t have to prove that you resisted correctly.

What if you only realize later that you disliked it?

You might leave a date thinking something felt strange but not know why.

Hours later, you realize you didn’t like the way the person touched your back. Or perhaps you laughed at a comment about your body because you didn’t know what else to say.

You are still allowed to decide that the behavior was unwanted.

You don’t owe someone another date because you didn’t object immediately.

If you want to continue seeing them, you can communicate the boundary afterward.

“I realized I wasn’t comfortable with the way you touched my back. Please ask before doing that again.”

If you don’t want to continue, you can end contact.

And if you feel unsafe, you do not have to send an explanation at all.

For sexual assault, medical support, and reporting options, Chapter 35 provides more specific information. RAINN also offers confidential support through its National Sexual Assault Hotline at 800-656-HOPE (4673) and at rainn.org/hotline (RAINN, 2026).

What if you recognize yourself crossing these lines?

Perhaps someone has told you that you stand too close, touch too often, stare, or continue conversations after they want to leave.

Hearing that can be embarrassing, especially if you thought you were being friendly.

It doesn’t help to decide that the person must be overly sensitive. It also doesn’t help to spend the interaction trying to prove you meant well.

Focus on what you can change.

First, stop the behavior.

If someone wants space, give it. If someone doesn’t want to be touched, don’t touch them again. If someone doesn’t want contact, don’t continue seeking it.

Second, take responsibility without asking the other person to make you feel better.

A brief apology may be appropriate if it does not prolong unwanted contact.

Third, look at the pattern privately.

If you often misread situations, you may benefit from learning more explicit ways to ask about boundaries. If you find yourself unable to stop thinking about or contacting someone who has shown disinterest, seek support from a qualified professional or another appropriate source that does not involve the person you are pursuing.

You do not need to be certain whether your difficulty comes from inexperience, impulsivity, neurodivergence, or something else before changing the behavior.

Explanations can help you learn. They don’t erase the other person’s experience.

A small practice exercise

Consider three situations. Before reading the suggested response, decide what you would do.

Situation one: The goodbye

You enjoyed a first date and would like a hug. Your date smiles and says they had a good time, but hasn’t indicated whether they want physical contact.

What do you do?

You can ask whether they’d like a hug, or simply say goodnight. Their smile and enjoyment don’t establish permission. Either choice can end the evening respectfully.

Situation two: The crowded room

Your partner usually enjoys holding your hand. At a busy event, they pull away and say they need some room.

What do you do?

Give them room. Don’t interpret their discomfort as a verdict on your relationship. You can discuss what happened later, when they’re ready and neither of you is under pressure.

Situation three: The uncomfortable comment

Someone you’ve been talking with says, “Please stop commenting on my body.”

What do you do?

Stop making those comments. If appropriate, acknowledge the mistake briefly. Don’t argue that the remarks were compliments or insist that the person misunderstood.

Notice what all three situations have in common.

You don’t need to interpret a complicated hidden signal or predict the other person’s feelings perfectly. You need to recognize when you don’t have permission, accept what the person communicates, and leave them free to choose.

Appropriate isn’t one fixed set of rules

You may date someone who loves hugs, someone who needs advance warning before being touched, someone who enjoys public affection, or someone who prefers affection in private.

You might be one of those people yourself.

Your preferences may change with the situation or over time. A partner’s preferences may change, too.

That’s normal territory for two people who are getting to know each other.

What makes an interaction respectful isn’t that everyone follows the same unwritten script. It’s that both people have room to express what they want and what they don’t.

You don’t need to read minds. You don’t need perfect eye contact, the correct facial expression, or an instinctive understanding of every social custom.

You do need to pay attention when someone communicates discomfort, ask rather than assume permission, and stop when attention or contact isn’t welcome.

A date is an opportunity to find out whether two people enjoy being together. It isn’t an entitlement to anyone’s body or attention.

Once you understand that difference, showing interest becomes less about guessing how far you’re allowed to go and more about finding out whether the other person wants to meet you there.
