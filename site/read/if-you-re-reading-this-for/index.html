If you're reading this for someone else

Maybe your partner asked you to read this book. Maybe someone you’re interested in mentioned being autistic, having ADHD, or finding relationships difficult because of past experiences. Maybe your adult child is dating for the first time, and you’re looking for a way to support them without giving another lecture.

Or maybe you picked up this book because you’re tired of misunderstanding someone you care about.

You’re welcome here.

You don’t have to be neurotypical to be reading for someone else. You might be autistic and dating someone with ADHD. You might have experienced trauma and be dating someone who hasn’t. You might both be autistic but have very different needs. Or neither of you may know what, if any, labels fit.

The important thing is that two people are trying to understand each other. Both have something to learn, and both deserve to be understood.

This book is written for people doing the dating, but much of it is useful to anyone who cares about them. The sections below can help you find what matters to your situation without reading all sixty chapters first.

Understanding someone isn’t the same as knowing them

There’s one rule worth establishing before anything else:

This book is for understanding people, not diagnosing, fixing, managing, or handling them.

Reading about autism doesn’t make you qualified to decide that someone is autistic. Recognizing an attachment pattern doesn’t mean you’ve discovered why someone behaves a certain way. And learning what helps some people with ADHD doesn’t mean you now know what your partner needs.

Those are possibilities to explore, not conclusions to announce.

If your partner doesn’t make much eye contact, you might learn that eye contact can be uncomfortable for some autistic people. That information gives you a reason to reconsider what you assumed. It doesn’t tell you whether your particular partner is autistic, uncomfortable, distracted, shy, or simply looking at something else.

The next step is to ask.

“You don’t look at me much when we’re talking. Does eye contact feel uncomfortable, or is that just how you prefer to listen?”

Then take the answer seriously.

The same applies to labels. Someone may identify as autistic without a formal diagnosis. Someone else may have a diagnosis they don’t want to discuss. Another person may be uncertain, or have no interest in finding a label.

Their identity and medical information are theirs to share. You don’t need to settle those questions to respect how they experience the world.

And if someone tells you what helps them, believe that information over a general description in a book, including this one.

Two people can misunderstand the same moment

The earlier section, “How perspectives work,” introduced a sculpture viewed from different positions, through different glasses.

Remember that picture when a conversation goes wrong.

You think your partner’s silence means they’re angry. Your partner thinks they’re giving your question careful thought. You interpret their pause as disapproval. They interpret your repeated questions as pressure.

Neither person knows what the other meant until they communicate.

This is an example of the double empathy problem: misunderstandings between people with different ways of experiencing the world can go both ways. Chapter 19 explores this in detail.

It isn’t a rule that both people are equally responsible for every disagreement. Someone can misunderstand you while also treating you badly. The double empathy problem explains why communication can fail; it doesn’t excuse cruelty or erase responsibility.

But it does offer a more useful starting question than “What’s wrong with them?”

Try: “What might each of us be understanding differently?”

Where to start reading

You can begin with whichever difficulty feels most familiar. These chapters are particularly useful when you’re dating someone whose communication, attention, sensory needs, or past experiences differ from yours.

  • Chapters 3, 4, and 6: Your sensory profile; Feelings you can’t name yet; Energy and executive function. Start here if noise, unexpected changes, delayed emotional answers, planning, or remembering messages are recurring difficulties.

  • Chapters 19, 20, and 24: The double empathy problem; Direct and indirect communication; Repairing misunderstandings. These are a good starting point when conversations regularly end with both people feeling misunderstood, or when hints, literal meanings, and assumptions cause trouble.

  • Chapter 25: Disclosure. Read this if someone is considering telling you about their neurotype, has already told you, or prefers not to discuss it. Understanding the choice doesn’t give you a right to the information.

  • Chapters 26–30: Boundaries and consent. These explain how to identify, communicate, and respect limits, including what to do when someone is quiet, uncertain, or says no. They’re important for every relationship, regardless of neurotype.

  • Chapters 38, 40, and 41: Why signals are hard; Reading interest; Friend or more? When you’re not sure. Useful when one person assumes interest is obvious and the other genuinely cannot tell, or when friendliness has been mistaken for romance.

  • Chapter 56: Mixed-neurotype and neurodivergent couples. Start here if you’re already in a relationship and trying to understand differences in routine, affection, solitude, responsibility, or how each person expresses care. It includes relationships where both partners are neurodivergent.

  • Chapters 2 and 57: Childhood, trauma and attachment; Trauma in relationships. These may help when earlier experiences affect present-day trust, closeness, or feelings of safety. They’re not a reason to diagnose your partner or explain away their choices.

If you’re getting ready for a date together, Chapter 49, “Planning a date that works for you,” may be more immediately useful than anything else. If you’re arguing often, Chapter 54, “Conflict without harm,” may matter more.

You don’t have to study the entire book before making dinner plans.

How to read the partner boxes

Throughout the book, you’ll find short sections with titles beginning “If you’re dating someone who…”

These are written specifically for you.

For example:

  • Chapter 20 includes “If you’re dating someone who takes you at your word.”
  • Chapter 29 includes “If you’re dating someone who goes quiet instead of saying no.”
  • Chapter 49 includes “If you’re dating someone who asks a lot of questions beforehand.”
  • Chapter 56 includes “If you’re dating someone who shows care in practical ways.”

The titles describe experiences rather than diagnoses. You don’t have to know whether someone is autistic, has ADHD, or has experienced trauma to recognize a situation and find the advice useful.

Each box offers another way of understanding what may be happening and what could help. It isn’t a set of instructions for making your partner behave differently.

Suppose someone asks six questions before agreeing to a date: where you’re going, whether it’s crowded, how long you’ll stay, where to park, what happens if the restaurant is closed, and whether you can leave early.

You might experience that as unnecessary planning. They might be trying to make an unfamiliar situation comfortable enough to enjoy.

The box in Chapter 49 can help you consider that possibility. But the person you’re dating is still the best source of information about why they’re asking.

And you can have preferences of your own. You might like spontaneous plans. A workable compromise could be agreeing on the essentials while leaving room to choose what to do afterward.

The boxes are invitations to communicate, not reasons to suppress your own needs.

Pay particular attention to boxes about consent, silence, and boundaries. Understanding why someone struggles to answer does not turn their silence into permission. When consent is unclear, ask plainly or wait. Don’t interpret a person’s difficulty saying no as a reason to continue.

You may also find yourself recognizing your own experiences in a box written for partners. That’s fine. These aren’t separate categories of people. The person who needs extra time to answer one question may be the person who needs a quick answer to another.

If you’re a friend or family member

Watching someone you care about date can be uncomfortable, especially when their choices seem confusing or risky.

Maybe your adult daughter has never been in a relationship and you’re worried she’s lonely. Maybe your brother keeps choosing partners who treat him poorly. Maybe your friend has spent months talking about someone who hasn’t returned their interest.

You may want to explain what they’re doing wrong, recommend a chapter, or make decisions for them.

Before you do, remember that support and control are different things.

An adult is allowed to make dating choices you wouldn’t make. They’re allowed to move slowly, remain single, date someone you don’t understand, end a relationship you liked, or stay in a relationship while they figure out what they want.

You can offer information, express concern, and be available. You cannot responsibly take ownership of their romantic life.

A parent who gives an adult child this book might say:

“I found something that explains dating pretty clearly, including situations people don’t usually talk about. You’re welcome to read it if you think it would be useful.”

That’s an offer.

Handing them a stack of marked chapters about their perceived shortcomings, then checking whether they’ve finished them, is pressure.

For friends, the same principle applies. If someone asks for help understanding a confusing interaction, offer to talk it through. Ask what happened, what they understood, what the other person actually said, and what remains uncertain.

Avoid declaring that you know what the other person secretly wants.

And don’t turn someone’s private disclosure about autism, ADHD, or trauma into information for other friends or family members. Let them decide who knows.

When you’re genuinely worried

Respecting autonomy doesn’t mean pretending that harmful behavior is harmless.

If someone describes a partner repeatedly ignoring their boundaries, controlling their friendships, frightening them, or pressuring them into sex, you can take those concerns seriously without making decisions on their behalf.

Focus on what happened rather than assigning a label to the partner.

You might say, “You said you asked them to stop, and they kept going. That concerns me. Do you want to talk about what would help you feel safe?”

Listen without making continued friendship or support conditional on following your advice.

Chapter 36, “Recognizing manipulation and abuse,” explains warning signs. Chapters 59 and 60 cover therapy, support, and crisis resources.

If immediate safety is at risk, helping someone reach appropriate emergency or professional support matters more than discussing a book.

Your needs count too

Understanding someone’s differences doesn’t require accepting behavior that hurts you.

Suppose your partner often forgets plans. You learn that remembering commitments and managing time may be difficult for them. That may help you stop interpreting every forgotten plan as a deliberate insult.

But the forgotten plans still affect you.

You can understand the difficulty and still need a reliable way to make arrangements. You might agree to shared reminders, written plans, or a confirmation the day before. The goal is a system both people can use, not one person endlessly accommodating the other.

Similarly, someone may need solitude after spending time together, while you need reassurance that the relationship is secure. Those needs aren’t automatically incompatible. You can discuss what amount of contact works for both of you.

If your partner experiences overload or shutdown during conflict, giving them time to recover may help. That doesn’t mean difficult subjects must disappear forever. You can agree to return to the conversation when both people can participate.

Your partner’s differences deserve respect. So do yours.

There is also a firm distinction between accommodating a difference and tolerating mistreatment.

A person may struggle to interpret facial expressions. They still need to respect a clearly stated no.

A person may have painful experiences with abandonment. That doesn’t give them permission to monitor or control you.

A person may find conflict frightening. They still have responsibility for how they treat others, just as you do.

Chapter 37, “Green flags and red flags,” helps you evaluate actual behavior rather than trying to decide whether someone’s diagnosis makes them a good or bad partner.

You are allowed to decide that a relationship isn’t right for you, even when you understand why the difficulties exist.

Reading together without turning it into homework

If both of you want to use this book, choose something small.

One chapter about a disagreement you’ve had may be more useful than a month of reading about every possible difference between you.

You could each read Chapter 20, “Direct and indirect communication,” then compare what stood out.

One person might recognize that they frequently expect others to understand hints. The other might realize they sometimes take a question literally when the speaker intended something else.

Instead of deciding who needs to change, ask:

“What would make this easier for both of us next time?”

You don’t have to agree on every explanation. You may interpret the same example differently. That’s an opportunity to describe what each of you sees, just as with the sculpture.

Don’t assign your partner chapters as punishment after an argument. Don’t quote the book to prove that your interpretation is correct. And don’t insist that someone discuss a topic simply because you’re ready.

Reading together is useful only when both people are free to participate, disagree, take breaks, or decline.

If you support people professionally

If you’re a therapist, counselor, coach, educator, or other professional working with adults who are dating, this book can serve as a shared starting point for conversation. Let the person choose the chapter that reflects a difficulty they want to address, rather than prescribing sections based on what you think their diagnosis or relationship history means.

A concrete passage about a missed signal, a confusing date, or an unspoken boundary may help someone explain an experience that has been difficult to describe. The examples are discussion tools, not diagnostic tests or treatment plans.

Stay within your professional scope, and use appropriate clinical assessment, safeguarding practices, and individualized support when needed. The book is not a substitute for professional care.

Let the person remain a person

You can learn a great deal about sensory needs, communication differences, attention, attachment, and trauma. You can recognize patterns you hadn’t noticed before. You can become more thoughtful about how you ask questions, make plans, and respond to disagreement.

But no book can tell you everything about the person sitting across from you.

They will have preferences that don’t match the examples. They may change their mind. They may surprise you. They may understand you better in some situations than you understand yourself.

The purpose of reading is not to become an expert on them.

It’s to become someone who can ask, listen, explain, and respect an answer, while expecting the same care in return.
