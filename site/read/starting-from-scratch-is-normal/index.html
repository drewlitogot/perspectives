Starting from scratch is normal

Before you start · about 11 minutes

Maybe you’re 22 and have never been on a date. Maybe you’re 38 and have never had a serious relationship. Maybe you’re 55, recently divorced, and realizing that you don’t know how to meet someone outside the life you built with your former partner.

Or perhaps you’ve dated for years, but you keep running into the same problems. You get attached to someone who barely knows you. You mistake friendliness for romantic interest. You lose interest when someone treats you well. You know how to get a first date but have no idea how to build a relationship afterward.

You might have had sex but never felt close to someone. You might have been in a relationship without ever having had a proper date. You might have neither experience.

This book is for all of those starting points.

You don’t need to catch up before you begin reading. You don’t need a diagnosis, a particular relationship history, or an explanation for why you’re starting here.

And you don’t need to pretend you already know things that nobody ever explained to you.

You’re not the only one

It’s easy to imagine that everyone else learned how dating works in high school, practiced through their twenties, and arrived at adulthood knowing what to do.

The numbers tell a more complicated story.

In a 2019 Pew Research Center survey, 35% of never-married adults who were currently single said they had never been in a committed romantic relationship. Among people aged 40 or older in that same group, 21% reported never having had one (Brown, 2020).

Those figures don’t mean that 35% of all adults have never had a relationship. They don’t tell us whether someone has ever been on a date, either. But they do establish something important: adults without a history of committed relationships exist at many ages, including well beyond their twenties.

The wider timetable for adult relationships has changed, too. Pew reported that 25% of American 40-year-olds had never married in 2021, compared with 6% in 1980 (Schaeffer, 2024). Marriage isn’t the same as dating, and someone who has never married may have had many relationships. Still, the comparison is a useful reminder that the milestones people expect aren’t fixed across generations.

A person who began dating at 16 and someone who begins at 46 have different histories. Neither history, by itself, tells you how kind, thoughtful, compatible, or capable of learning that person is.

There is also no requirement to follow every milestone. Dating, committed relationships, marriage, and sex are different experiences. You can want one without wanting the others. You can have some without having experienced all of them.

If you’ve never had sex, that doesn’t make your questions about dating less legitimate. If you’ve had sex but never been in love, you still belong here. If you’ve been married three times and struggle to recognize a healthy relationship, there’s something here for you, too.

You are allowed to begin with the part you don’t know.

There are many ways to arrive here

Some people grow up with friends who talk openly about crushes, relationships, rejection, and sex. Others grow up in homes where those subjects are forbidden, embarrassing, or never discussed.

Some people spend their teenage years managing school, family responsibilities, illness, isolation, or simply getting through the day. Dating may not be available, appealing, or particularly important during those years.

Others want relationships but find the usual social expectations confusing.

Perhaps you prefer someone to say exactly what they mean, while the people around you rely on hints. Perhaps crowded places make conversation exhausting. Perhaps you need more time to recognize what you’re feeling, or you have trouble telling when someone is interested in you.

You may identify as autistic, have ADHD, or recognize aspects of yourself in descriptions of those experiences. You may have a history of childhood or relationship trauma. You may be exploring a diagnosis, rejecting an old one, or have no interest in labels at all.

None of those labels is an entry requirement.

A systematic review examining 13 studies notes that previous research has found broadly similar interest in romantic relationships among autistic and non-autistic people. The review itself found that social and communication challenges were associated with difficulties starting relationships and lower relationship satisfaction, while partner support and reciprocal liking were associated with more positive relationship experiences (Yew et al., 2021).

That review drew on a relatively small and varied collection of studies. It doesn’t tell us that every autistic person wants romance, or how many have never dated.

A smaller qualitative study involving 31 autistic and 26 non-autistic adults also described varied experiences and preferences around romantic and physical intimacy (Sala et al., 2024). It wasn’t a national survey, but it adds another reminder: people cannot be reduced to a diagnosis or a single relationship pattern.

Some people want a romantic relationship intensely. Some are curious but uncertain. Some don’t want one at all.

Your preferences matter more than whether they match someone else’s expectations.

This book is also for the person on the other side of a relationship. If you’re dating someone whose communication, attention, sensory needs, or emotional responses differ from yours, understanding each other is a shared responsibility. One person does not automatically have the correct way of seeing things.

You don’t need to make your brains work identically. You need ways to explain what matters to each of you and understand the differences.

Having experience isn’t the same as having skills

Consider two people.

Celeste is 36. She’s had several relationships, but she repeatedly chooses partners based on immediate attraction. When the excitement fades, she assumes the relationship must be wrong. She has plenty of dating experience, but she’s never really considered what compatibility means.

Franklin is 44. He’s never been in a committed relationship. He knows he wants one, but he has difficulty recognizing interest, starting conversations, and asking someone on a date. He’s afraid people will judge him for learning these things now.

Both have something to learn. Neither needs to be embarrassed about learning it. Chapter 10, Starting late: dating as a beginner adult, addresses the particular experience of learning dating skills later in life.

Dating experience can give you opportunities to practice, but experience alone doesn’t mean you’ve developed every useful skill. It’s possible to repeat an unhelpful pattern for years without understanding it.

It’s also possible to arrive at a first date with qualities that matter in relationships: curiosity, patience, honesty, reliability, or consideration for someone else’s needs.

What you haven’t practiced is not the whole of who you are.

And some useful skills can be developed through practice. In a small randomized trial involving 22 autistic young adults aged 18–24, participants receiving a structured, caregiver-assisted social-skills program improved in measured social skills, social engagement, and knowledge of social situations. Many improvements remained at a 16-week follow-up (Laugeson et al., 2015).

That study doesn’t prove that every adult can learn every dating skill, and it certainly doesn’t promise that completing a program will lead to a relationship. Its participants were young adults in a specialized, caregiver-assisted program, not a representative sample of people starting to date at 40, 50, or 60.

But it offers a grounded reason to distinguish what you currently find difficult from what you might become better at doing.

This book approaches dating that way.

Starting a conversation can be practiced. Asking a clear question can be practiced. Recognizing your own preferences, expressing a boundary, responding respectfully to rejection, and repairing a misunderstanding are things you can learn about and work on.

You don’t have to become someone else to practice them.

Learning to communicate more clearly isn’t the same as hiding your personality. Learning what another person needs doesn’t mean abandoning your own needs. And learning to show interest never means learning to pressure someone who isn’t interested.

The goal is to make your choices more informed and your interactions more respectful, not to perform a version of yourself that you cannot comfortably sustain.

You may recognize some uncomfortable patterns

Sometimes starting from scratch means learning something for the first time. Sometimes it means reconsidering something you’ve believed for years.

You might recognize yourself in a few of these situations.

You choose almost entirely by appearance. Attraction matters, and there’s nothing wrong with wanting to find a partner attractive. But appearance cannot tell you whether someone shares your values, respects your boundaries, or wants a compatible life. Chapter 11, Attraction, looks and what lasts, and Chapter 13, Compatibility, help you consider what else matters.

You expect romance to happen the way it does in movies. Perhaps you’ve been waiting for one extraordinary person who will understand you without explanation, never disappoint you, and make every difficult part of life easier. Chapter 14, Realistic expectations, examines the distance between romantic fantasy and an actual relationship between two people.

You become deeply attached before you really know someone. You might replay conversations, imagine a shared future, or find it difficult to think about anything else. Strong feelings are real, but they don’t establish that two people are compatible or equally interested. Chapter 16, Crushes, infatuation and fixation, helps separate those experiences.

You pull away when someone treats you well. Maybe a patient, consistent person seems unexciting, while someone unpredictable occupies all your attention. Or perhaps closeness feels uncomfortable even when you want it. Chapter 17, Running from a good thing, explores how to notice these reactions without forcing yourself into a relationship you don’t want.

You struggle to tell friendliness from romantic interest. Someone remembers your favorite food, laughs at your jokes, or regularly asks about your day. You wonder whether it means something more. It might, or it might not. Chapter 41, Friend or more? When you’re not sure, explains how to approach uncertainty without making assumptions.

None of these patterns belongs to one gender, orientation, or type of brain.

Recognizing a pattern doesn’t mean you must find a diagnosis or blame yourself for having it. It gives you something specific to examine.

It also doesn’t mean every uncomfortable feeling is a mistake. Sometimes you don’t like someone because they’re genuinely not right for you. Sometimes uncertainty deserves more time rather than immediate action.

Part of learning is distinguishing what you can change from what you should respect about yourself.

What this book won’t ask you to do

There are plenty of places to find dating advice that promises to make someone attracted to you, teach you how to gain the advantage, or explain why an entire gender is the problem.

This book won’t do that.

It won’t teach manipulation. Another person’s interest must be freely given. A refusal is not a puzzle to solve, and uncertainty is not permission to proceed without asking. The chapters on boundaries, consent, and safety take this seriously.

It won’t diagnose you. Autism, ADHD, trauma, and other experiences are discussed where they help explain a difficulty or difference. You can use the practical advice whether you have a diagnosis, suspect one, or don’t identify with any label.

It won’t judge people by gender. People of every gender can be considerate or careless, interested or unavailable, inexperienced or knowledgeable. Where genuine differences in experiences matter, the book addresses them without turning them into stereotypes.

It won’t promise you a partner. You can improve how you communicate, choose, and treat people. You cannot control whether another person wants a relationship with you. Nobody owes anyone romance, affection, sex, or another chance.

And it won’t insist that you date at all.

You might decide you want a relationship now. You might want to wait until other parts of your life are more settled. You might discover that friendship, independence, or a different kind of connection suits you better. You might not want romance or sex.

Those are choices worth understanding, not problems this book needs to correct.

The purpose is to help you make informed choices and respect other people’s choices, too.

Where to begin

You can read the book in order, beginning with how your own mind and experiences affect relationships. Or you can start with the question that’s most useful to you right now.

  • If you don’t know whether you want to date, or whether you’re ready: Begin with Chapter 7, What do you actually want?, and Chapter 8, Readiness, in Part 1, Know yourself. You don’t need to settle every question about your future before making a small decision about your present.

  • If dating seems full of rules everyone understands except you: Start with Part 2, Dating basics nobody taught you, particularly Chapter 10, Starting late: dating as a beginner adult. Then turn to Part 3, Communication, and Part 5, Signals and interest, for the practical skills of expressing yourself and understanding another person’s responses.

  • If past relationships have hurt you, or you find it difficult to recognize what is safe and respectful: Start with Chapter 2, Childhood, trauma and attachment, in Part 1, and Part 4, Boundaries and consent. You can also find guidance about professional support in Part 9, Support.

If you’re reading because you’re dating someone whose brain works differently from yours, Chapter 19, The double empathy problem, and Chapter 56, Mixed-neurotype and neurodivergent couples, are particularly relevant. They’re about understanding differences from both directions, not deciding whose way of communicating is better.

Content note: Later chapters discuss childhood trauma, abuse, manipulation, harassment, stalking, sexual assault, sexual consent, sex and sexual health, and difficult relationship experiences. The front-matter section How to use this book explains the content notes and how to skip or pause sections.

One final distinction matters.

Starting from scratch doesn’t mean erasing everything you’ve lived through. Your friendships, work, interests, values, and experiences of caring for other people are still part of you. So are the difficult experiences that may have taught you what you do or don’t want.

You don’t have to prove that you’re experienced enough to deserve a relationship. And you don’t have to pretend that wanting one makes every choice you’ve made a good one.

You can acknowledge what you don’t know, reconsider what hasn’t worked, and begin with the next skill you want to learn.

That’s a perfectly reasonable place to start.
