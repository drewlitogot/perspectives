Chapter 42: Flirting without games

You like someone. They make you laugh. You want them to know that you enjoy their company, but you aren’t ready to announce that you’re interested in dating them.

So you try flirting.

Perhaps you make a small joke. Perhaps you compliment something they said. Perhaps you look at them for an extra second, then immediately worry that you’ve been staring.

Now you have two problems: figuring out what you meant to communicate, and wondering what they thought you meant.

Flirting is often presented as a performance. You are supposed to be clever, confident, a little mysterious, and somehow know exactly when to say the right thing. If you need to ask what is happening, some dating advice suggests you’ve already ruined the moment.

That is a lot of pressure to put on a conversation.

Flirting can be much simpler. You can enjoy someone’s company, express a little extra warmth, share a joke, or let them know you find them attractive. The interaction can be playful without becoming confusing on purpose.

Good flirting leaves both people free to enjoy the conversation, change the subject, or decide they aren’t interested.

You do not need to become a different person to do it.

What flirting actually is

Flirting is communication that expresses, suggests, or plays with the possibility of attraction or a closer connection.

That definition includes a lot of different behavior.

Two people might exchange jokes that feel slightly more personal than their usual conversation. Someone might give a thoughtful compliment. A person might show unusual enthusiasm about spending time with someone else.

But none of those actions has one guaranteed meaning.

Researchers who studied flirting identified six possible motivations: sex, having fun, exploring whether attraction exists, strengthening a connection, feeling appreciated, and obtaining some other benefit. A person might have more than one motivation, or might not fully understand their own motivation yet (Henningsen, 2004; Henningsen et al., 2008).

Consider three conversations.

Nina tells Alex, “You always find the most interesting little bookstores.” She genuinely likes Alex’s taste and wants Alex to feel appreciated.

Later, Marcus tells David, “You have a way of making the most ordinary stories entertaining.” Marcus is attracted to David and hopes David might feel the same way.

At a party, Priya and Samir spend several minutes joking about their terrible attempts at cooking. Both enjoy the exchange. Neither is thinking about dating the other.

All three interactions could look flirtatious to an observer. They don’t necessarily mean the same thing to the people involved.

This matters because a common beginner’s mistake is treating flirting as a declaration.

Someone laughs at your jokes. You conclude they’re attracted to you.

Someone compliments your appearance. You conclude they want a date.

Someone enjoys a playful conversation. You conclude something sexual might happen.

Those conclusions go beyond what the behavior establishes.

A person can flirt without wanting a relationship. They can enjoy being flirted with without wanting to flirt back. They can be attracted to you without wanting sex, or interested in dating without enjoying flirtation at all.

Someone who is asexual, aromantic, questioning what they want, or simply uninterested in romance at that moment may still enjoy playful conversation. Their enjoyment does not create an obligation or reveal their private intentions.

Flirting is an invitation to enjoy an interaction, not a promise about what happens afterward.

If you need to know what someone wants, you can ask. You do not have to interpret the entire conversation as a puzzle.

There isn’t one correct way to flirt

Some people enjoy playful teasing and quick jokes. Others prefer sincere conversation, thoughtful compliments, or gentle expressions of affection.

In a large study developing the Flirting Styles Inventory, researchers described five self-reported styles: sincere, playful, polite, physical, and traditional (Hall et al., 2010).

The names describe tendencies, not five kinds of people.

Someone who favors sincere flirting might focus on connection and getting to know another person. A playful flirter might enjoy the interaction without necessarily seeking a relationship. A polite flirter might be cautious about expressing attraction. The traditional category reflects conventional expectations about initiation, while the physical category describes a communication tendency, not permission to touch.

People can use several styles.

The study found associations between some styles and participants’ reported experiences, but it did not establish that any style guarantees romantic success. Its historical dating context and self-report design also limit how widely its findings can be applied (Hall et al., 2010).

You don’t need to identify your style or take a quiz.

Instead, consider which interactions feel comfortable and natural.

If you enjoy playful conversation, that can be part of how you express interest. If you prefer sincerity, you can express interest sincerely. If flirting feels unnatural, you can skip it and communicate plainly.

For example:

“I really enjoyed talking with you.”

“That was a great story. I like your sense of humor.”

“You have interesting ideas about this. I’d like to hear more.”

These aren’t magic phrases. They are ordinary ways of expressing genuine reactions.

You might say something similar to a friend. That is fine. A sentence does not need to contain a secret romantic meaning to be worthwhile.

What matters is that you mean it and that the other person has room to respond however they choose.

You don’t have to pretend you care less

Some dating advice suggests that showing genuine interest makes you less attractive. It treats emotional distance as a sign of confidence.

You do not need to follow that rule.

If you enjoy talking with someone, it is reasonable to say so. If you are looking forward to seeing them, you don’t need to manufacture indifference.

Being sincere also doesn’t require revealing every feeling immediately.

There is a difference between saying, “I’ve enjoyed getting to know you,” and expecting someone you barely know to respond to an intense declaration of love.

You can be interested without being demanding. You can be warm without assuming the feeling is mutual.

The goal isn’t to hide attraction. It is to express yourself while leaving the other person’s choices intact.

You don’t need a clever opening line

Imagine you’re sitting next to someone at a community event.

You would like to talk with them. Before speaking, you begin searching for the perfect opening sentence.

Something funny? Something memorable? A compliment? A joke that makes you seem confident?

Meanwhile, the event has ended and the person is putting on their coat.

Sometimes the search for a clever line becomes harder than the conversation itself.

Older studies offer a useful reality check.

In a 1986 study involving three samples of university students and employees, participants generally preferred ordinary or direct opening lines over cute or flippant ones (Kleinke et al., 1986).

A later experiment had 642 college students watch a staged interaction and evaluate different approaches. Direct introductions were rated more appropriate than exaggerated compliments or flippant introductions. Introductions made through another person also received favorable ratings (Weber et al., 2010).

Neither study followed actual couples to see whether these approaches led to relationships. The research involved mostly heterosexual scenarios, and the later experiment used only one example of each type of introduction.

So these findings do not establish a universal best opener.

They do challenge the assumption that you must be unusually clever to begin a pleasant conversation.

Ordinary conversation is a legitimate starting point.

You can comment on a shared experience, ask about something the person mentioned, or introduce yourself.

If a connection develops, you can express your interest more clearly.

Flirting does not have to happen before two people can start talking. It can grow out of a conversation that both already enjoy.

And if you don’t know what to say, that doesn’t mean you’ve missed a secret skill everyone else possesses.

Playful is not the same as manipulative

There is an important difference between flirting and playing games.

In this chapter, games means deliberately creating confusion, insecurity, jealousy, or emotional pressure to influence another person’s feelings or choices.

That is different from an ordinary playful interaction.

A playful exchange can involve humor, surprise, and a little uncertainty. Neither person knows exactly where the conversation will go.

But both people remain free to participate or stop.

A manipulative interaction is organized around controlling the other person’s response rather than respecting their choice.

You may encounter dating advice that treats the other person as an opponent to outsmart, or teaches that someone else’s confidence must be weakened before they will become interested.

That is not the kind of connection this book teaches.

The research on flirting motives, conversation openers, and flirting styles does not directly test the long-term outcomes of entire systems of manipulative dating advice. It would be inaccurate to claim that science has proved every such tactic ineffective (Hall et al., 2010; Kleinke et al., 1986; Weber et al., 2010).

The reason to reject manipulation is more basic.

Someone else’s uncertainty or insecurity is not a resource for you to exploit.

You want someone to be interested because they genuinely enjoy being with you, not because you have engineered a situation where declining feels difficult.

This also protects you.

If someone else’s attention leaves you repeatedly feeling confused, belittled, or responsible for proving your worth, you do not have to treat that discomfort as romantic excitement.

You can decide that the interaction isn’t enjoyable and leave it.

A useful question

Before you say or do something flirtatious, ask yourself:

Would I still be comfortable with this interaction if the other person turned out not to be romantically interested?

Suppose you compliment someone’s sense of humor.

If they smile, say thanks, and continue talking as friends, you can accept that response.

Suppose you share a joke.

If they don’t find it funny, you can move on without making them responsible for your embarrassment.

Suppose you express that you enjoy someone’s company.

If they don’t express the same feeling, you can respect that without trying to change their mind.

These are examples of expressing yourself without demanding a particular result.

You are not trying to earn someone’s affection through a sequence of correct moves. You are finding out whether you enjoy interacting with each other.

If you recognize yourself playing games

Perhaps you have tried making someone jealous because you were afraid to express your interest directly. Perhaps you have deliberately made someone feel uncertain because you believed it would keep their attention.

You can change that behavior.

Stop the pressure. Take responsibility for the effect it had without demanding forgiveness or reassurance. Respect the other person’s decision about whether they want further contact.

If the pattern keeps repeating, a qualified counselor can help you examine why direct, respectful communication feels difficult.

You don’t need to condemn yourself as a person. You do need to stop using another person’s feelings as a tool.

Humor should be something you share

Humor can make flirting enjoyable.

Two people notice the same absurd detail. One makes a joke, and the other adds something even funnier. They develop a shared reference that makes later conversations feel familiar.

Neither needs to perform like a comedian.

In a study involving participants from the United States and Norway, researchers found that people rated various flirtatious behaviors differently depending on the hypothetical situation. Humor received favorable evaluations in some contexts (Kennair et al., 2022).

But those were ratings of imagined effectiveness, not observations of people developing actual relationships.

The older opening-line experiment also found that one attempted humorous introduction received relatively poor ratings. Its authors acknowledged that the particular joke might simply not have been funny (Weber et al., 2010).

These findings are not contradictory. They measure different situations, and neither supports a rule that being funny guarantees attraction.

The more useful distinction is between laughing together and making someone the target of the laughter.

When teasing isn’t welcome

Teasing is playful commentary directed at someone, sometimes involving a mild joke about them.

People who already know and trust one another might enjoy some kinds of teasing. But the person making a joke and the person receiving it may experience it differently.

In a study of teasing, researchers found that people making teasing comments often interpreted their own behavior more positively than recipients did. The speaker’s intention to be playful did not ensure that the recipient experienced the exchange that way (Kruger et al., 2006).

That study was about teasing generally, not flirting specifically.

Still, it gives a useful reason to be careful.

Consider an example.

Jordan and Lee are discussing their favorite movies. Jordan makes a joke about Lee’s favorite director. Lee laughs and makes a joke about Jordan’s favorite film. Both enjoy the exchange.

Later, Jordan makes a personal comment about something Lee feels insecure about.

Lee goes quiet.

Jordan could defend the comment by insisting it was only a joke. Or Jordan could recognize that the joke wasn’t enjoyable for Lee.

A better response is simple:

“Sorry. That was unkind. I won’t joke about that.”

Then Jordan changes course.

An apology doesn’t require a long explanation about why the joke was supposed to be funny.

And Lee doesn’t have to laugh, reassure Jordan, or explain the insecurity.

If you have only recently met someone, it is especially reasonable to avoid jokes about their body, identity, abilities, personal vulnerabilities, or expressed boundaries.

You may not know which subjects are painful to them.

If someone tells you they dislike a particular kind of teasing, believe them. Don’t treat that boundary as another opportunity for a joke.

You can enjoy humor without making another person uncomfortable.

When flirting doesn’t come naturally

Some people can improvise playful conversation easily. Others need more time to think.

Perhaps you have trouble telling when someone is joking. Perhaps eye contact is uncomfortable. Perhaps indirect compliments feel dishonest, or you don’t realize until later that a conversation might have been flirtatious.

You may prefer to communicate through writing or to say what you mean without disguising it as humor.

None of those preferences prevents you from expressing attraction.

Research involving autistic adults offers useful accounts of the difficulties created by unclear courtship expectations.

In one small U.S. interview study, 24 autistic adults discussed difficulties with courtship and described needs that were not always addressed by conventional relationship education (Barnett & Maticka-Tyndale, 2015).

Another U.S. qualitative study with 28 autistic young adults included participants who wanted opportunities to practice meeting potential partners and learn about flirting. Participants also described strengths, including empathy and acceptance (Kohn et al., 2025).

A U.K. study interviewed 16 autistic adults who were gender- or sexually diverse. Participants described relationships that did not always fit conventional scripts, and some emphasized the value of explicit communication and individually negotiated expectations (Ciric et al., 2026).

These studies cannot tell us how every autistic person flirts. Their samples were small, and some groups were underrepresented.

They do show why teaching only one conventional flirting script would leave some readers without useful options.

You can choose a different style

If you don’t enjoy rapid joking, you don’t have to force yourself to become quick-witted.

If eye contact makes it harder to follow a conversation, you don’t have to maintain it to prove you’re interested.

If you prefer clear language, you can use clear language.

For example:

“Sometimes I miss flirting. I tend to be more direct.”

“I’m enjoying this conversation, but I’m not always sure when someone is flirting.”

“I like compliments that are straightforward rather than teasing.”

These are ordinary preferences. You don’t have to explain them through a diagnosis.

They can also help the other person understand your communication style.

One person might see friendly teasing as affection. Another might experience it as criticism.

Neither has to guess forever.

You can discuss what feels enjoyable to each of you.

When flirting feels like a performance

There is another possibility: you can recognize what flirting looks like, but trying to imitate it leaves you exhausted.

Perhaps you rehearse facial expressions, monitor your voice, hide your natural reactions, or spend the entire conversation wondering whether you look interested enough.

That can become a form of masking, meaning hiding or suppressing natural traits to fit in.

A systematic review of eight qualitative studies examined autistic people’s experiences of masking while pursuing friendships and intimate relationships. It identified concerns about authenticity and fatigue, although the strongest available evidence concerned friendships rather than romantic relationships (Ridgway et al., 2025).

The review doesn’t establish that abandoning masking leads to more dates.

It does provide a reason not to assume that performing a socially expected style is free of personal cost.

You can decide how much effort feels reasonable.

A brief social adjustment may be comfortable. Maintaining a completely different personality for an entire evening may not be.

If you consistently need to hide how you naturally communicate to keep someone’s interest, that is useful information about the interaction.

It doesn’t automatically mean either person is wrong. It may mean that you need a different way of communicating, or that the connection is not comfortable for both of you.

If you’re dating someone who communicates differently

Suppose you enjoy playful banter, but someone you’re dating prefers direct statements.

You might be tempted to conclude that they lack a sense of humor or aren’t interested.

That conclusion isn’t necessary.

They may enjoy humor in different situations, need more time to respond, or simply prefer to know when a comment is meant romantically.

Try asking about their preferences.

“Do you enjoy playful teasing, or would you rather I say what I mean?”

Or, if you genuinely aren’t sure what they intend:

“Are you flirting with me, or are we just having a fun conversation?”

Ask with genuine openness to either answer.

You can also explain your own preferences: “I like joking around, but I don’t want to make you uncomfortable.”

Different styles can coexist. The goal isn’t for one person to become the other’s preferred version of a romantic partner.

It is to find communication that both can enjoy.

This is a practical application of the communication experiences described in the qualitative research, not a scientifically tested intervention for mixed-neurotype couples (Barnett & Maticka-Tyndale, 2015; Ciric et al., 2026).

If flirting looks like rules nobody gave you

Flirting is mostly deniability: saying something while leaving a route to claim you didn’t. If that strikes you as a strange way to tell someone you like them, you aren’t missing the point.

You can skip the layer entirely. Warmth, attention, remembering what someone said and being pleased to see them do the same job, and are harder to misread.

Plain interest works on plenty of people. Someone who only responds to the performed version may be harder work than they look.

Flirting is optional

Imagine two people who like each other.

One enjoys romantic banter. The other prefers long conversations about books, occasional compliments, and simply spending time together.

They don’t need to adopt the same approach to enjoy the relationship.

Flirting can add playfulness, but it is not a requirement.

You can be attracted to someone without being good at flirting. You can build affection without clever exchanges. You can enjoy a conversation without wanting it to become romantic.

You can also decide that you dislike flirtatious attention.

If someone flirts with you and you want to remain friendly, you can say:

“Thanks. I’m enjoying talking, but I mean it as a friendly conversation.”

Or simply:

“I’d rather keep things friendly.”

You do not owe a detailed explanation for that preference.

If you’re the person who was flirting, accept the answer.

There is no need to make the other person feel guilty for having enjoyed the conversation before clarifying their intentions.

The same principle applies when someone is uncertain. You don’t have to settle what their uncertainty means. You can let the conversation remain friendly.

An interaction can be enjoyable even when it doesn’t lead to romance.

Flirting is not consent

One boundary deserves special attention.

Flirting does not give someone permission for sexual activity.

Someone may compliment you, laugh at your jokes, express attraction, or enjoy a conversation with romantic undertones without wanting physical or sexual contact.

RAINN’s U.S. consent guidance emphasizes that agreement to sexual activity must be voluntary, specific, and ongoing. Flirting and previous interactions do not establish that agreement (RAINN, 2026).

This distinction matters whether you have known someone for ten minutes or ten years.

Likewise, romantic interest doesn’t give you permission to ignore someone’s discomfort or stated boundary.

If someone asks you to stop flirting, stop. If they seem uncomfortable and you aren’t sure what they want, give them space rather than increasing the intensity.

You don’t need to interpret a hesitant reaction as a challenge.

When you need clarity, use a question. When someone doesn’t want to answer, respect that too.

A conversation can stay playful without moving toward touch, sex, or a date.

The next chapter deals with when and where expressing romantic interest more directly is appropriate. For now, remember that enjoying attention and agreeing to something are different things.

If you're dating someone who doesn't flirt back

Not returning a flirt is often not knowing it happened. The signal may simply not have arrived, and escalating subtly makes it less likely to.

Try the direct version once and watch what happens: “I’m flirting with you, in case that wasn’t obvious.” Most people who missed it are delighted rather than embarrassed.

Also check how they do show it. Remembering details, arranging things around you and sustained attention are all interest; they just aren’t the form you were looking for.

A few ways to make flirting your own

If you have never deliberately flirted, the idea can feel intimidating.

You might picture a confident stranger delivering a perfectly timed compliment. Or perhaps you remember watching friends exchange jokes and wondering what rule everyone else seemed to understand.

You do not need to reproduce those scenes.

Start with the kind of positive interaction you already know how to have.

Appreciation

You can tell someone what you genuinely appreciate about them.

“You explain things in a way that makes them interesting.”

“I like how enthusiastic you get about your projects.”

These comments focus on something you have actually noticed.

They do not need to be romantic declarations.

Shared amusement

If you both find something funny, you can enjoy that moment together.

For example, Elena and Rachel are assembling furniture for a community event. The instructions are confusing.

Elena remarks, “Apparently this table was designed by someone who has never met a table.”

Rachel laughs and adds, “Maybe it’s supposed to be a very small bridge.”

They continue joking while working.

Perhaps they are flirting. Perhaps they are simply having a good time.

They don’t have to decide immediately.

The pleasure comes from sharing the moment, not from calculating what the laughter means.

Curiosity

You can invite someone to talk more about something they enjoy.

“What got you interested in that?”

“What’s your favorite thing about it?”

You might already do this naturally in friendships.

In a flirtatious interaction, genuine curiosity can be one way of showing that the person has your attention.

But questions are not a substitute for listening. You should care about the answer, not merely about keeping the conversation going.

Straightforward warmth

Sometimes you may want to express your feelings without disguising them.

“I’ve really enjoyed spending time with you.”

“You make conversations fun.”

These statements allow someone to know something about your experience without requiring them to share it.

If you eventually want to ask for a date, you can do that plainly, in an appropriate setting. Chapter 43 covers that decision.

For now, you don’t need to add a request to every pleasant interaction.

A small exercise: finding your version

Think of someone you have enjoyed talking with, whether a friend, a past date, or someone you have recently met.

You don’t need to contact them. This is simply a way to notice what feels natural.

Ask yourself:

  1. What do I genuinely enjoy about our conversations? Perhaps their humor, curiosity, kindness, or enthusiasm.
  2. How would I comfortably express that appreciation? A straightforward sentence, a shared joke, or an interested question may feel more natural than an elaborate compliment.
  3. Could they respond in any way without feeling pressured? Your expression of interest should not require a matching declaration, a date, or an explanation.
  4. Would I still feel like myself? If the interaction requires you to pretend to be someone entirely different, consider another approach.

These questions are not a scientific test. They are a way to choose behavior that fits your values and communication preferences.

You can practice one ordinary expression of appreciation in a suitable conversation without expecting a romantic result.

You can also decide that you don’t want to practice flirting at all.

The purpose is to become more comfortable expressing genuine interest, not to collect successful responses.

When the moment goes awkwardly

Sometimes you will make a joke that doesn’t land.

You might say something more intense than you intended. You might miss a flirtatious remark entirely and realize what happened three hours later.

Or you might ask whether someone is flirting and discover they meant the conversation to be friendly.

These moments can be uncomfortable. They don’t have to become disasters.

You can acknowledge a misunderstanding briefly.

“I think I misunderstood what you meant. Thanks for clarifying.”

Or, after an awkward remark:

“That came out differently than I intended. Sorry.”

Then allow the conversation to move on, if the other person wants to continue.

You don’t need to explain every detail of your thinking. You also don’t need to demand reassurance that you haven’t made a bad impression.

If someone expresses a boundary, respect it. If they prefer to end the interaction, accept that.

Chapter 44 covers rejection and what to do when the feeling isn’t mutual.

For this chapter, the important point is that a small misunderstanding can be handled with honesty rather than more performance.

Flirting isn’t a test you pass by never being awkward.

The point is connection, not performance

Picture two people having a good conversation.

One notices something amusing, and the other laughs. They exchange a thoughtful compliment. They become a little more curious about one another.

Maybe attraction is developing. Maybe not.

Neither has to know everything the other person is thinking. Neither needs to control the outcome.

They can simply enjoy what is happening and communicate clearly when they want something more.

That is enough.

You don’t need a special line, a mysterious personality, or a strategy for making someone pursue you.

You need room to be yourself and a willingness to let the other person be themselves.

Flirting can be part of that. So can directness, quiet affection, shared interests, and ordinary conversation.

The useful question isn’t “How do I make someone interested?”

It is “Can I express my interest in a way that respects both of us?”

When you can do that, you have something much more useful than a collection of clever lines.

You have a way to begin a connection without turning another person into a problem to solve.

The next step is learning when the setting is appropriate for making your interest more explicit, and how to ask without pressure.
