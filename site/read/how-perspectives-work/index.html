How perspectives work

Before you start · about 12 minutes

Imagine a sculpture in the middle of a room.

From the entrance, it looks like a tall, smooth column. Walk around to the left and you discover a hollow opening. From the right, you can see rough edges and a second piece hidden behind the first. Stand directly behind it and the shape looks different again.

Now imagine three people standing in different places around that sculpture.

One describes the smooth surface. Another describes the hollow opening. The third describes the rough edges. They disagree about what the sculpture looks like.

But nobody is lying. Nobody is necessarily confused. Each person is describing something real that the others cannot see from where they stand.

None of them can see the entire sculpture at once.

Dating works much the same way. Two people can share a conversation, a date, or a relationship and come away with different understandings of what happened. Each may have noticed something the other missed. Each may also have made assumptions about the parts they couldn’t see.

There is one more complication.

Everyone is wearing glasses.

The same moment, two different views

The sculpture is the shared situation. Where you stand determines which parts are visible to you. Your glasses affect how you see those parts.

Consider a first date between Daria and Felix.

They meet at a coffee shop. Daria asks questions, smiles, and moves the conversation along whenever there’s a pause. Felix listens carefully and takes several seconds to answer. Sometimes Daria starts another question before he finishes thinking.

Afterward, Daria wonders whether Felix was interested. He seemed hesitant and didn’t have much to say.

Felix has a different concern. Daria kept changing topics. He wonders whether she was bored by his answers.

Both noticed something real.

Felix really did pause. Daria really did change topics quickly. But Daria was trying to keep the conversation comfortable, while Felix needed time to organize his thoughts. Neither could see the other’s intentions.

On their next conversation, Daria mentions what she noticed.

“I realized I kept jumping to another question. Was I giving you enough time to answer?”

Felix replies, “I was still thinking about some of them. I thought you wanted to move on.”

“Not at all,” Daria says. “I thought the silence meant you weren’t enjoying yourself.”

One conversation clears up what two people might otherwise have spent days guessing about.

Notice what they didn’t have to do. Daria didn’t need to diagnose Felix. Felix didn’t need to decide Daria was impatient. Neither had to prove that their interpretation was correct.

They described what they saw and asked about the part they couldn’t see.

That is the central habit behind this book.

Where you stand

Your position around the sculpture represents your social position: how other people tend to treat you, what they expect from you, what opportunities you have, and what risks you may need to consider.

Gender is part of that position. So are age, culture, race, disability, sexual orientation, financial circumstances, and the communities you belong to.

These things do not tell you exactly what a person thinks. They help explain why different details might stand out to different people.

For example, imagine someone suggests a quiet, secluded walking trail for a first date.

One person may picture a peaceful place where conversation will be easier than in a crowded restaurant. Another may immediately wonder whether they could leave comfortably if the date went badly.

The first person isn’t necessarily careless. The second isn’t necessarily distrustful. They are considering different parts of the situation.

Both considerations matter. The answer might be to choose a public, quiet location that meets both people’s needs.

Gender can influence where people stand because social expectations and experiences are not distributed identically. Someone may have learned that they are expected to initiate romantic interest. Someone else may have learned to be especially careful about unwanted attention. Another person may worry about how their gender identity will be received.

But gender is not a set of instructions for understanding an individual.

Not every man approaches dates with the same expectations. Women do not share one universal experience of safety or attraction. Nonbinary people do not all encounter the same social situations.

People within the same gender can stand in very different places.

A 24-year-old man who has never dated may notice different things from a 55-year-old man returning to dating after divorce. Two women may have different expectations about who initiates. Two nonbinary people may have entirely different experiences of being understood by strangers.

Nor does any one position automatically make someone more insightful, considerate, or correct.

Your position gives you a view, not complete knowledge.

The useful question is not “What do people of this gender think?”

It is “What might this person be noticing that I haven’t considered?”

The glasses you wear

Your position is only half the picture.

Imagine that everyone around the sculpture wears glasses with different lenses. Some make small details easier to notice. Some make bright light difficult to tolerate. Some affect which parts draw attention first.

The glasses represent how your brain processes the world and how your past experiences influence what you notice and expect.

If you notice patterns, details, and inconsistencies that other people seem to overlook, those details may be central to your experience of a date. If your attention moves rapidly between a conversation, background sounds, and passing thoughts, the same date may feel very different.

Autism and ADHD can be part of these differences. A person may be autistic, have ADHD, or experience both. But there is no single autistic view or ADHD view, and not every difference needs a diagnostic explanation.

Neurotypical people wear glasses too. Neurotypical means their development and processing are broadly consistent with what society usually treats as typical. That does not mean their perception is neutral, complete, or automatically correct.

The fact that someone’s way of communicating is familiar to more people does not make it the only reasonable way.

Some lenses come from experience

Not all the glasses come from how your brain developed.

Growing up in a household where disagreements were discussed calmly may influence what you expect when someone is upset. Growing up where arguments involved shouting, punishment, or unpredictable reactions may leave you expecting something different.

If previous partners repeatedly ignored your limits, you may pay close attention to signs that someone could do so again. If you’ve rarely experienced affection, you may have difficulty telling whether someone’s warmth is romantic interest or ordinary friendliness.

If nobody explained dating to you, you may be working from movies, online advice, or whatever you’ve observed in other people’s relationships. Those examples may have left important parts out.

These experiences can affect what seems normal, safe, attractive, or concerning.

Some lenses may change with time, learning, supportive relationships, or professional help. Others reflect enduring ways of processing the world that deserve accommodation and respect rather than attempts to erase them.

You can wear several lenses at once

A person can be autistic and have ADHD. Someone can have ADHD and a history of trauma. Someone can have no diagnosis and still find crowded dates exhausting or indirect communication confusing.

Different influences may overlap. Sometimes it’s difficult to know which one explains a particular experience.

A label can be useful if it helps you understand your needs, find support, or explain something to a partner. Sometimes a label turns out not to fit. And sometimes you simply don’t know.

You don’t need to settle that question before using this book.

Start with what actually happens to you.

Do you need more time to answer? Do you miss hints? Do you find some settings difficult to tolerate? Do you struggle to recognize your feelings until hours after a date?

Those experiences are worth understanding whether you have a name for them or not.

The room matters too

The sculpture doesn’t change when someone turns down the lights, but seeing it becomes harder.

A conversation in a noisy restaurant may be difficult for someone who follows speech easily in a quiet room. Someone comfortable talking with a close friend may have trouble finding words on a first date. Tiredness, stress, excitement, and unfamiliar surroundings can change what anyone notices.

So before deciding what another person’s behavior means, consider the setting.

Sometimes changing the room helps more than trying to change the person.

Different views are not different truths about everything

The sculpture picture has an important limit.

It does not mean every interpretation is equally accurate. It does not mean facts become optional because people see things differently.

If one person says a conversation lasted ten minutes and another says it lasted an hour, both accounts cannot be accurate in the same way. They can check the time.

If someone says they didn’t intend to hurt their partner, that may be an accurate description of their intention. It does not erase the harm their partner experienced.

And if someone says no to being touched, their refusal is not an interpretation to debate.

Safety, consent, and respect are not matters of personal perspective.

A person who feels unsafe does not have to remain in a situation to understand the other person’s intentions. Someone who has refused a kiss does not need to explain their reasoning until the other person agrees.

Understanding why someone acted a certain way is different from excusing what they did.

The same distinction matters when you recognize a harmful pattern in yourself. You can examine what you misunderstood or what led to your behavior without treating that explanation as permission to continue. You are responsible for stopping harmful actions and respecting other people’s limits.

The sculpture picture is a tool for understanding people, not an argument for overlooking harm.

Understanding goes both ways

A useful idea you will meet in Chapter 19 is the double empathy problem.

Put simply, misunderstandings between people who experience the world differently can go both ways.

You might have trouble understanding why someone uses indirect hints. That person might have just as much trouble understanding why you need a direct explanation.

You may think you’re being considerate by leaving someone alone when they’re quiet. They may think you’re withdrawing affection. Or you may want quiet yourself and discover that your partner interprets it as anger.

Neither person can solve every misunderstanding simply by trying harder to guess.

And neither person should automatically be assigned the entire responsibility for translating between them.

That doesn’t mean responsibility is always equal. One person may have caused harm, ignored a clear explanation, or held more power in the situation. Those things still matter.

But when two people genuinely want to understand each other, communication is a shared task.

Chapter 19 develops the double empathy problem in more detail. For now, remember that the other person has a position and glasses of their own, even if their way of seeing the world seems ordinary or obvious to you.

A practical habit: describe, ask, check

You cannot see through another person’s glasses by concentrating harder.

You can, however, learn more about their view.

Try three steps when something feels unclear.

Describe what you noticed.

Begin with something observable instead of a conclusion about someone’s intentions.

“You got quiet after I suggested another date” describes an event.

“You obviously don’t want to see me again” assumes an explanation.

Ask what they experienced.

Give the other person room to describe their view.

“Was there something about that suggestion that didn’t work for you?”

They might need time to think. They might be unsure themselves. They might prefer not to discuss it.

A question is an invitation to clarify, not a demand for an answer.

Check what you’ve understood.

You can say, “So you’re interested in meeting again, but you’d prefer somewhere quieter. Did I get that right?”

Then listen to the answer.

If the answer is no, accept it. If the person cannot answer yet, allow room for that. If a decision requires their consent, wait until you have it rather than filling the silence with your own preferred interpretation.

This habit doesn’t require a formal conversation every time someone changes expression or takes a few minutes to reply. Not every difference needs investigating.

Use it when an assumption could affect your choices, your relationship, or someone else’s comfort and safety.

You won’t always get an explanation. People have privacy, and sometimes they don’t know why they reacted as they did. You can still make respectful decisions with the information available.

The goal is not to discover every hidden thought. It is to avoid treating your first guess as a fact.

How to read the boxes in this book

Throughout the book, you’ll find short boxes that look at certain situations from a particular position around the sculpture or through a particular set of glasses.

Most advice stays in the main text because it applies across genders, neurotypes, and experiences. The boxes appear when an additional viewpoint helps explain something that the shared advice alone might miss.

There are three kinds.

Perspective boxes

These boxes are labeled for men, women, or nonbinary people.

They explore situations where social expectations, treatment, or experiences may differ. You might encounter one about safety, another about expectations around initiating a date, and another about how people react to disclosure.

A perspective box is not a rule about everyone in that group.

It doesn’t mean all men feel one way, all women feel another, or all nonbinary people share the same experiences. It identifies something that may matter for people standing in a particular social position.

You can read the box for your own gender, but don’t stop there.

Reading another perspective can help you notice a side of the sculpture that isn’t visible from where you stand.

Experience-titled boxes

These boxes have titles such as “If you lose track of time” or “If loud places wear you out.”

They address experiences that may be associated with autism, ADHD, a combination of the two, trauma, or uncertainty about a diagnosis.

The title describes the experience, not who you’re required to be.

You do not need to be autistic to find sensory advice useful. You do not need an ADHD diagnosis to benefit from a way to remember plans. And you don’t have to disclose anything about yourself to use a suggestion.

Read the boxes that describe something familiar to you. Skip those that don’t.

A label inside the book is a way of organizing information, not a diagnosis of the reader.

Partner boxes

These boxes begin with “If you’re dating someone who…”

They speak to the other person in an interaction.

For example, a box might explain what can help if you’re dating someone who needs extra time to respond or finds last-minute changes difficult.

These boxes aren’t instructions for managing, correcting, or controlling a partner. They help you understand what the other person might be experiencing, while respecting that the person knows their own needs better than a general guide does.

They are also not just for neurotypical partners. An autistic person dating another autistic person may still encounter different sensory or communication needs. Two people with ADHD may have different approaches to planning. Partners with different histories may need different things even when they share a diagnosis.

Reading a partner box can be useful regardless of your own neurotype.

You don’t have to choose one view

The boxes are not separate versions of the book, and you don’t have to decide which category you belong to before reading.

You might find that several apply to you. You might recognize your partner in a box that doesn’t describe you. You might discover that a perspective you had never considered makes an earlier disagreement easier to understand.

You can read only the boxes relevant to your current question or read all of them.

Often, the box written for someone unlike you is the one that teaches you something new.

That is why the book includes multiple perspectives instead of dividing readers into separate groups who never hear from one another.

When you return to the sculpture

As you move through the book, the sculpture-and-glasses picture will appear again.

It will help you examine your own needs, think about compatibility, understand communication differences, recognize boundaries, and make sense of situations where two people remember the same interaction differently.

You don’t need to memorize the picture. Just keep its central question available:

What am I seeing, and what might the other person be seeing that I can’t?

Sometimes the answer will show you that you’ve misunderstood someone. Sometimes it will help you explain something they misunderstood about you. Sometimes it will reveal a genuine incompatibility, a safety concern, or a boundary that needs to be respected.

Understanding another person’s perspective doesn’t require agreeing with them or staying in a relationship that doesn’t work.

It gives you better information for making your own choices.

One person looking at the sculpture can describe a piece of it. Two people willing to listen may be able to describe more.

Neither has to surrender their own view to learn from the other.
