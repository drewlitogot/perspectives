Mina has been on three dates with Rowan. They’re thoughtful, funny in a quiet way, and interested in getting to know her. Rowan remembers things she says. They don’t disappear for days and then return with an elaborate excuse. When Mina disagrees about something small, Rowan listens instead of making it an argument.

Nothing is obviously wrong.

After their third date, Rowan says they’d like to see Mina again. Mina says she’d like that too. She means it.

Then something changes.

On the way home, Mina starts thinking about whether Rowan is really that attractive. Yesterday she liked their laugh; tonight it seems irritating. Their messages suddenly feel too frequent. She wonders whether dating someone so predictable would become dull.

By the next morning, she’s considering ending things.

She might be discovering a genuine lack of compatibility. Three dates are not enough to know someone well, and kindness alone does not make two people a good match.

But there’s another possibility. Mina might be comfortable with dating until it begins to offer something she wants: a real chance of being known, wanted, and eventually depended on. Now that the possibility is getting closer, she wants distance.

The difference matters.

Sometimes leaving is the right choice. Sometimes leaving is a way to avoid finding out what could have happened.

The challenge is learning to tell those situations apart.

When something good starts to feel threatening

Wanting a relationship and feeling comfortable when one becomes possible are two different things.

You might spend years wishing someone would choose you. Then someone does, and you feel strangely uneasy.

You might be enthusiastic while getting to know a person, then lose interest after they show clear affection.

You might enjoy someone when you’re together but want to disappear as soon as they ask about making plans for next week.

None of these reactions automatically means you have a problem. But if they happen repeatedly, especially when someone becomes more emotionally available, they’re worth examining.

Relationship researchers describe a tension between two ordinary human concerns: wanting closeness and wanting protection from rejection.

In the risk regulation model, people are more willing to depend on a partner when they feel confident that the partner cares about them. When that confidence is uncertain, protecting themselves can become more important than moving closer (Murray et al., 2006).

Consider what closeness makes possible.

Someone you barely know cannot disappoint you in all the ways someone important can. Their opinion of you may matter less. They haven’t seen your habits, your insecurities, or how you behave after a difficult day.

A person who becomes important gains the ability to hurt you, even without intending to.

There is no way to make a meaningful relationship completely free of that possibility.

For some people, the uncertainty is manageable. For others, it can create an urge to regain distance.

That urge might sound like:

They’re getting too attached.

Something about this feels wrong.

Maybe there’s someone more exciting.

It was easier before they started liking me.

These thoughts might identify real concerns. They might also be attempts to explain an uncomfortable feeling.

A feeling deserves attention, but it is not always an accurate explanation of its own cause.

You can take your discomfort seriously without immediately accepting the first story your mind produces about it.

Kind is not the same as compatible

Before going further, an important distinction needs to be made.

Someone can be kind, respectful, emotionally available, and entirely wrong for you.

They may want children when you don’t. They may want to live somewhere you would never consider. Their preferred amount of time together may be exhausting for you. You might enjoy their friendship but have no desire for a romantic relationship.

You do not owe someone a relationship because they treat you well.

You also do not need a dramatic reason to stop dating them.

Consider Tarek, who is 46 and recently started dating Julian. Julian is considerate, reliable, and easy to talk to. They share several interests.

After a few dates, Tarek realizes he looks forward to their conversations but doesn’t want romantic intimacy with Julian. He doesn’t feel frightened by Julian’s interest. He simply doesn’t want the relationship Julian is hoping for.

Ending things kindly would be reasonable.

Now consider someone who enjoys kissing their date, looks forward to spending time together, and wants similar things from life. Each time the other person becomes more affectionate, however, they abruptly feel trapped. The same pattern has happened with several previous partners.

That is a different situation.

The question is not, Is this person good enough that I should stay?

It is, What am I responding to?

A genuine mismatch deserves respect. So does a need for space. Fear of closeness deserves understanding rather than an automatic decision that the relationship must end.

These possibilities can also overlap. Someone might be partly compatible and partly frightening to get close to. You do not need to force every dating experience into one explanation.

Attachment is not a personality sentence

You may have heard people describe themselves or their partners as anxious, avoidant, or secure.

These terms come from attachment research. In adult relationships, attachment anxiety means being especially concerned about whether someone will remain available, loving, or supportive. Attachment avoidance means being uncomfortable with emotional dependence or closeness.

People can experience both.

You might want reassurance when someone seems distant and then feel uncomfortable when they become very affectionate.

You might feel independent and relaxed in one relationship but worried about abandonment in another.

Popular descriptions sometimes turn attachment into a set of personality categories: you’re one type, your partner is another, and the relationship follows a predictable script.

The research is more complicated.

A study examining attachment measures in two samples found stronger support for continuous differences than for separate, fixed categories (Fraley et al., 2015).

In plain language, attachment anxiety and avoidance are better understood as things you can experience to different degrees, not permanent boxes that determine your future.

A large study following 1,036 established couples over 20 months found that attachment avoidance was generally stable, while attachment anxiety showed small average decreases among women. Partners did not generally change together, and measured relationship characteristics did not consistently explain changes (Traut et al., 2025).

This matters because two popular promises are both too simple:

That’s just how I am. Nothing can change.

Once I find a secure partner, all my fear will disappear.

Neither is a useful rule for dating.

Your past does not have to dictate every future choice. But another person’s kindness is not a treatment they can provide simply by being available.

And needing solitude, moving slowly, or enjoying independence does not automatically mean you have attachment avoidance.

The important issue is not which label fits. It is what you actually do when closeness becomes uncomfortable, and whether those choices respect both people.

When calm feels boring

Some people describe meeting someone reliable and feeling almost disappointed.

There is no uncertainty about whether the person will appear. No need to interpret wildly inconsistent messages. No dramatic reunion after a painful argument.

Just a pleasant evening, followed by an ordinary plan to meet again.

The person thinks, Is this all there is?

One possibility is that the date really is uninteresting to them.

Another is that they’ve become accustomed to treating intensity as an important sign of romantic attraction.

But the familiar advice that people with difficult pasts automatically find healthy partners boring has a serious evidence problem: the research reviewed for this chapter does not directly establish that general pattern.

It is a possible personal experience, not a proven rule about trauma, attachment, or human attraction.

There is also a difference between several feelings that can look similar at first.

Peace is being able to relax around someone without fearing what they’ll do next.

Boredom is feeling little interest in the interaction or wanting more engagement.

Lack of attraction is not wanting a romantic or physical relationship with that person.

Fear is anticipating something unwanted, such as rejection, pressure, losing independence, or being hurt.

You can experience more than one of these at once.

A peaceful date can also be boring. An exciting date can also be safe. A person can be trustworthy without being romantically interesting to you.

There is no requirement to find everyone who treats you well attractive.

Yet there is a reason to question the idea that a date has failed unless it produces extraordinary excitement.

Some experiences become more rewarding as you learn about the other person. A conversation that begins awkwardly might become interesting once you discover a shared interest or a kind of humor you both enjoy.

You are allowed to be curious before you are certain.

When the excitement fades

Early dating often contains novelty. You’re discovering someone, imagining possibilities, and encountering experiences that haven’t yet become familiar.

For some people, the novelty itself is a particularly important part of the excitement.

A 2026 qualitative study of adults with ADHD in Ireland included accounts of intense early romantic excitement followed by fading interest as the relationship became more familiar. Some participants also described rejection concerns, distancing, and the comfort of partners who understood their needs (O’Brien et al., 2026).

These were participants describing their experiences, not a finding that everyone with ADHD loses attraction when novelty fades.

It would be equally mistaken to conclude that a drop in excitement means the relationship should end or that it must continue.

Ask what remains when the surprise becomes smaller.

Do you still enjoy talking to this person?

Are you curious about what they think?

Do you like how you feel when you’re together?

Do you want to know them beyond the most exciting version of a date?

You do not need fireworks every time you meet. But you do need some reason of your own to want the relationship.

If newness makes someone especially exciting

If you notice that new people capture your attention intensely and familiar interactions are harder to stay interested in, separate the excitement of discovery from your feelings about the person. You might enjoy an ordinary afternoon together more than a dramatic first date, even if the ordinary afternoon feels less exciting.

You can also be honest with yourself when the attraction really has faded. No one should be expected to supply constant novelty to keep your attention, and you don’t have to stay simply to prove that you can.

Your past can influence what you notice

A new person is not always experienced as completely new.

You bring memories of earlier relationships, family experiences, friendships, and rejection into the interaction.

One study found that people carried expectations from previous romantic partners into reactions to descriptions of potential new partners. The effect was stronger when the new person resembled someone from the past (Brumbaugh & Fraley, 2006).

This was an experimental study using descriptions of possible partners, not observations of people choosing relationships in everyday life.

It supports the narrower idea that previous expectations can influence how someone new is perceived. It does not prove that people are unconsciously searching for copies of harmful partners.

Still, the idea offers a useful question:

Does something about this situation remind me of a previous experience?

Perhaps your former partner responded to affection by becoming distant. When someone new takes a few hours to reply, you begin preparing for another rejection.

Perhaps expressing a need in your childhood home led to criticism. When a new partner invites you to explain what you prefer, you feel anxious instead of relieved.

The new person might be behaving differently from the people you remember.

Or the similarity might be real.

The task is to notice the difference, not convince yourself that every alarm is false.

A large meta-analysis published in 2026 found modest associations between childhood maltreatment and both attachment anxiety and avoidance in adulthood. The review combined 211 studies and found correlations of .23 with anxiety and .20 with avoidance (Li et al., 2026).

These are associations across groups. They do not establish what any particular person’s relationship will look like, and they do not demonstrate that someone with a difficult childhood will prefer unstable partners.

What happened earlier can be relevant without becoming your destiny.

If affection makes you brace for something bad

If kindness once came with unpredictable criticism, demands, or disappointment, being cared for now may bring up uncomfortable expectations. You may find yourself searching for the part that hasn’t gone wrong yet.

You do not have to disclose your history to someone you’re newly dating. You can choose a slower pace, keep your usual supports and routines, and pay attention to how the person behaves over time. If fear repeatedly makes relationships difficult or interferes with your sense of safety, a qualified mental health professional can help you explore it without assuming a particular diagnosis.

When self-protection starts damaging the relationship

Romantic self-sabotage means behaviors that undermine a relationship a person may otherwise want.

The word sabotage sounds deliberate. Sometimes the behavior is deliberate, but people may also act defensively without fully understanding why.

In interviews with 15 Australian psychologists who worked with relationship difficulties, researchers identified several behaviors the practitioners associated with self-sabotage, including withdrawal, criticism, defensiveness, jealousy, and difficulty trusting partners. The practitioners described possible motivations such as fear of hurt, self-protection, expectations about relationships, and difficulty managing problems (Peel et al., 2019).

A separate research program developed a questionnaire measuring relationship sabotage. Across three studies totaling 1,365 respondents, researchers identified three related areas: defensiveness, trust difficulty, and lack of relationship skills. Some of these measures were associated with greater attachment insecurity and lower reported relationship quality (Peel & Caltabiano, 2021).

These studies relied heavily on self-reports, and most respondents were women. They cannot identify why any particular person pulls away, or establish that one behavior inevitably causes a breakup.

A later model-comparison study also found that insecure attachment, perceptions of the relationship, and defensive responses were statistically interconnected rather than fitting one simple explanation (Peel, 2025).

That complexity matters.

A person might behave defensively because they’re afraid, because they misunderstood something, because they haven’t learned how to discuss disagreement, or because their partner really has behaved badly.

The same outward behavior can have different explanations.

But understanding the explanation does not erase the consequences.

If you repeatedly dismiss a partner’s concerns, criticize them for expressing affection, or withdraw without explanation, the other person experiences those actions regardless of what motivated them.

You are responsible for how you treat someone, even when the impulse behind your behavior makes sense to you.

The temptation to test someone

Imagine that someone has shown you steady interest, but you still can’t believe they genuinely care.

Instead of telling them you’re uncertain, you begin watching every interaction for proof.

You question whether ordinary delays mean they’ve lost interest. You expect them to demonstrate affection again and again. You may feel tempted to create situations in which they have to prove themselves.

This is different from asking for reassurance or discussing a genuine concern.

An honest question gives another person room to answer.

A covert test makes the other person participate in an examination they did not agree to take.

The research reviewed for this chapter does not establish how common partner-testing behavior is, whether it belongs to a particular attachment pattern, or which intervention works best. The practical distinction here is ethical rather than statistical.

If you recognize yourself trying to make someone prove their affection through confusion, jealousy, pressure, or repeated demands, stop that behavior.

You can speak about your uncertainty without creating a problem for the other person to solve.

For example:

“I like spending time with you. Sometimes I get worried about whether the interest is mutual, and I realize I’ve been making assumptions. Can you tell me how you’re feeling about getting to know each other?”

The other person may reassure you. They may be uncertain. They may tell you that they don’t want the same thing.

All three responses are information.

A respectful conversation does not guarantee the answer you want. It gives both people an opportunity to be honest.

When a small uncertainty feels enormous

You send someone a message. Usually they reply within an hour. Today several hours pass.

You notice yourself becoming anxious.

Perhaps you think you’ve said something wrong. Perhaps you’re angry that they haven’t answered. Perhaps you decide to end things before they can reject you.

There is a name for one possible contributor to that experience: rejection sensitivity.

It means being especially inclined to expect rejection and interpret uncertain situations as evidence of it.

In a series of four studies, researchers found connections between rejection sensitivity, interpreting ambiguous behavior as rejection, and relationship dissatisfaction (Downey & Feldman, 1996).

But the evidence isn’t uniform.

A 2024 study involving 149 couples expecting their first child collected repeated reports of their daily experiences. Contrary to the researchers’ expectations, greater rejection sensitivity did not predict stronger negative emotions or slower emotional recovery after perceived negative interactions (Richter et al., 2024).

These studies involved different populations and methods. They do not establish a simple rule that rejection-sensitive people always react more strongly or for longer.

They do suggest a distinction worth making between what happened and what you believe it means.

They have not replied is an observation.

They’ve stopped caring is an interpretation.

Sometimes the interpretation is correct. Sometimes the person is working, resting, distracted, dealing with a problem, or simply communicating at a different pace.

You can notice uncertainty without treating it as a verdict.

And if you eventually learn that the person is not interested, that information is disappointing, but it is not proof that you were wrong to show interest in the first place.

Space is not always avoidance

Imagine two autistic adults, Jess and Dev, who enjoy spending time together.

They both like conversation, but a long date involving a crowded restaurant leaves Jess exhausted. Afterward, Jess wants a quiet evening alone.

Dev enjoys spending time apart too, but feels uncertain when Jess becomes less talkative after an otherwise pleasant evening.

Dev might interpret Jess’s quietness as loss of interest.

Jess might interpret Dev’s questions about the evening as pressure.

Neither interpretation has to be correct.

The actual question is what each person needs and means.

Research specifically connecting autism and adult romantic attachment remains limited. A 2025 systematic review found 12 relevant studies, but only three specifically recruited autistic adults who were in romantic relationships. The studies differed considerably in what they measured and whom they included (Sonfelianu et al., 2025).

Another review covering 42 articles described challenges involving sensory experiences, stigma, and communication, alongside strengths such as truthful communication. The authors also identified gaps in research on relationships between two autistic partners and on queer relationships (Neu & Bradford, 2025).

This evidence does not support assuming that autistic people are emotionally avoidant or that asking for space reflects a fear of intimacy.

That distinction matters for anyone whose energy, sensory processing, attention, or communication needs make conventional dating expectations difficult.

You might need time alone because you are tired.

You might need extra time before answering a personal question because you are thinking carefully.

You might dislike unexpected touch while deeply enjoying emotional closeness.

You might experience shutdown after overload and have difficulty speaking for a while.

None of those experiences, by itself, demonstrates that you’re afraid of a relationship.

The familiar sculpture-and-glasses picture applies here: two people can encounter the same moment and understand it differently.

The best available explanation might come from describing your experience and hearing the other person’s, not deciding which attachment label fits them.

If you're dating someone who needs time or space after a date

If someone seems interested while you’re together but needs quiet time afterward, don’t automatically interpret that as rejection. They may be recovering from sensory or social demands, processing the interaction, managing other responsibilities, or simply enjoying time alone.

You can ask what kind of contact works for them and explain what helps you feel connected. A need for space is legitimate, and so is your desire to understand where you stand. Neither person has to abandon their needs to prove affection.

How to stay long enough to find out

If you’ve recognized yourself in this chapter, you might wonder what to do the next time you want to end a promising connection.

The answer is not to force yourself through an arbitrary number of dates.

There is no research-supported rule that you must date someone three, five, or ten times before knowing how you feel. Nor is there a proven waiting period that turns fear into attraction.

Instead, consider a small experiment in curiosity.

It is appropriate only when you feel safe, genuinely have some interest, and freely want to learn more about the other person.

1. Notice when your feelings changed

Return to Mina’s experience.

She enjoyed Rowan’s company. She liked their attention. She began questioning the relationship immediately after Rowan expressed stronger interest.

That timing might be meaningful.

A different person might lose interest after discovering a major disagreement about children, religious beliefs, or where to live.

Someone else might feel uncomfortable after a date repeatedly ignored a request for personal space.

Those situations call for different responses.

Ask yourself what actually happened before you wanted to pull away.

Was it something the person did?

Something you learned about them?

Something they asked of you?

Or a possibility that suddenly felt more real?

There may not be an immediate answer. The point is to replace a vague sense that something is wrong with a more specific question.

2. Separate the facts from your predictions

Try writing three short sentences about the situation.

What happened: “They asked whether I’d like to meet their friends sometime.”

What I’m predicting: “If I say yes, they’ll expect us to be serious immediately.”

What I need: “I want to keep getting to know them without making a big commitment yet.”

The prediction might be correct. But you have not yet established that it is.

You can ask what meeting their friends means to them.

You can say that you’re not ready.

You can decline without ending the entire relationship.

Separating those pieces may reveal an option you couldn’t see while reacting to them all at once.

3. Choose a pace that works for you

You don’t have to move at the speed someone else prefers.

If weekly dates feel manageable but seeing someone every evening feels exhausting, you can say so.

If you’re interested but not ready to meet someone’s family, that can be a reasonable boundary.

If you need a day to decide how you feel after a date, you don’t have to pretend you’re certain.

A useful statement might be:

“I’m enjoying getting to know you, and I’d like to continue. I move a little slowly when things become more personal. I’d be more comfortable keeping our dates fairly relaxed for now. How does that sound to you?”

Notice that this makes your preference clear without demanding that the other person agree.

They might want a faster pace. They might not enjoy uncertainty. They might decide this isn’t the relationship for them.

That doesn’t make either of you wrong.

Making room for your comfort does not mean placing someone else’s dating life on indefinite hold.

4. Have another ordinary interaction, if you want one

Suppose you feel safe and interested, but you’re unsure whether a sudden wish to withdraw reflects incompatibility or fear.

You could choose to spend a little more time with the person.

Not a dramatic weekend away or a major commitment. Just another ordinary date that gives you more information.

Choose something that lets both of you be comfortable enough to notice the interaction.

Perhaps a walk, a quiet meal, a museum visit, or an afternoon doing something you both enjoy.

Pay attention to what the experience is like rather than trying to manufacture excitement.

Are you comfortable enough to be yourself?

Can you express a preference?

Do you enjoy their company?

Does your interest increase, decrease, or stay about the same?

An ordinary afternoon cannot guarantee long-term compatibility. But it can help you learn about the actual person rather than only the imagined relationship.

If you choose not to go on another date, that is also a valid decision.

5. Reconsider with new information

Afterward, return to the original question.

Perhaps you discover that the person is more interesting than you initially thought.

Perhaps you enjoy them but still don’t want romance.

Perhaps you feel closer and notice the same fear returning.

Perhaps they respond poorly to a reasonable request, giving you important information about compatibility or safety.

You do not need to achieve a particular outcome.

The purpose of staying a little longer is to learn, not to make yourself fall in love.

And if the other person decides to stop dating, respect that decision. Your effort to understand your own reactions does not obligate them to participate.

If you’re on the other side

Sometimes you’re the person who has been kind and consistent, only to encounter sudden distance.

You may wonder what you did wrong.

Perhaps someone was enthusiastic last week and now seems uncertain. Perhaps they tell you they enjoy your company but aren’t comfortable getting closer.

You cannot determine their motives from that alone.

They might be afraid of intimacy. They might need more time. They might be discovering incompatibility. They might have unrelated difficulties, or they might simply have changed their mind.

You can ask what they want and whether continuing to date still makes sense for both of you.

You can also decide what level of uncertainty you’re comfortable with.

Being patient does not require accepting repeated disrespect, unexplained hostility, or a relationship that consistently leaves you unhappy.

You are not responsible for convincing someone that they deserve love, proving that you are different from everyone who hurt them, or staying until they become ready.

A relationship cannot be built on one person’s willingness to wait forever.

That remains true when the person has a trauma history, is autistic, has ADHD, or describes themselves using an attachment label.

Understanding a reason for someone’s behavior can help you respond thoughtfully. It does not require you to accept behavior that harms you.

When leaving is the healthier choice

This chapter is about recognizing the possibility of unnecessary withdrawal. It is not an argument for remaining in relationships that make you unsafe.

Respectful relationships include privacy, independence, communication, and the ability to make decisions freely. U.S. Department of Health and Human Services guidance identifies controlling behavior, intimidation, threats, isolation, and sexual coercion as warning signs of relationship violence (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2026).

If someone pressures you, disregards your boundaries, threatens you, or tries to control your life, you do not need to investigate whether your wish to leave is an attachment reaction.

You do not need a psychological explanation to justify protecting yourself.

A person who frightens or controls you is not demonstrating romantic intensity that you should learn to appreciate.

Likewise, if you recognize yourself becoming controlling, intimidating, or repeatedly pressuring someone for reassurance or contact, stop. Respect their limits, including a decision to end contact, and seek appropriate professional support.

An attachment history may help explain a reaction. It does not excuse harming another person.

If you need confidential support about relationship abuse in the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at 1-800-799-7233. In immediate danger, call 911. Later chapters cover safety, abuse, therapy, and support resources in greater detail.

You don’t have to decide everything today

Mina eventually decides she’d like to see Rowan again.

Not because Rowan has passed a test. Not because she’s decided this must be a healthy relationship. And not because she has promised herself she’ll stay no matter what.

She’s noticed that her sudden loss of interest began after Rowan expressed affection, and she’s curious about that reaction.

Before their next date, she tells Rowan that she likes spending time together but isn’t ready to make assumptions about where the relationship is going.

Rowan appreciates knowing that. They agree to keep getting to know each other.

The fourth date might be wonderful. It might be ordinary. Mina might eventually decide that Rowan isn’t the right person.

What has changed is that she can make her choice with a little more information.

You can do the same.

You can notice an urge to retreat without obeying it immediately. You can question a sudden judgment without dismissing your discomfort. You can ask for time, express uncertainty, and discover that another person doesn’t see things exactly the way you do.

You can also leave when you genuinely don’t want the relationship.

The goal isn’t to become someone who never feels afraid or uncertain.

It’s to make decisions that reflect what you want, what you know, and how the other person actually treats you, rather than allowing one uncomfortable moment to make the entire decision.

And if you decide that a connection is worth exploring, the next question is whether both people are willing to participate in it. That’s where effort and reciprocity begin.
