You can want a relationship and still not have enough room for dating this month.

You can also be struggling with money, mental health, loneliness, or the demands of daily life and still be capable of building a good relationship.

Both things can be true.

Readiness is not a certificate you earn after fixing everything about yourself. Nobody reaches adulthood with every problem solved. People fall in love while changing careers, caring for relatives, managing disabilities, recovering from painful experiences, and figuring out who they are.

But wanting a relationship and having room for one are not quite the same thing.

Imagine two people.

Nina is 34. She has a demanding job, a small apartment, and an unreliable sleep schedule. She sometimes needs an entire Saturday to recover from the working week. She would like a partner, but she knows that three dates a week would be too much. She’s willing to meet someone, communicate her limits, and make room for a relationship gradually.

Marcus is 42. He has a comfortable income, close friends, and plenty of free time. On paper, his life looks settled. But he believes that having a partner is the only way he’ll ever feel worthwhile. He is so frightened of another rejection that he thinks he should accept almost any relationship someone offers.

Who is more ready?

There is no scientific formula that can answer that question. Nina has practical limitations, but she understands them. Marcus has practical resources, but his fear may make it difficult to decide whether a relationship is actually good for him.

The question is not whether your life looks impressive from the outside. It is whether you have enough room, choice, and support to approach another person as a person, rather than as the solution to everything that hurts.

Readiness is not a personality trait

You might feel ready to date in March, completely unable to face it in April, and interested again in June.

Perhaps a family member becomes ill. Perhaps work gets harder. Perhaps you’ve been dealing with overload for weeks, and even responding to a friend’s message takes more effort than usual.

Or perhaps you finally have enough stability to notice that you’d enjoy someone’s company.

None of these changes means your basic worth has changed.

Researchers have examined one part of readiness: how prepared people feel to enter a committed relationship. In five samples, Hadden and colleagues found that people who reported greater commitment readiness were more interested in starting relationships, more likely to pursue them, and, in longitudinal samples, more likely to enter relationships later. Readiness was also associated with commitment once a relationship formed (Hadden et al., 2018).

That is useful evidence, but it answers a narrower question than the one facing you.

The researchers measured people’s sense of readiness for commitment. They did not establish a minimum income, a required number of friends, a mental-health standard, or a checklist you must pass before going on a date.

For everyday purposes, it helps to think of readiness as three overlapping questions:

  • Do I want to date? Not because I am supposed to, but because getting to know someone interests me.
  • Do I have room to date? Enough practical and emotional capacity to participate at a pace I can manage.
  • Can I date with care? Enough willingness to respect another person’s choices, communicate about difficulties, and protect my own limits.

You do not need perfect answers. You need answers that are reasonably honest.

Someone who says, “I’d like to date, but I can only manage an occasional quiet afternoon,” may have a clearer starting point than someone who promises unlimited availability and discovers a week later that they cannot sustain it.

Readiness is partly about recognizing what you can offer today, rather than promising what you wish you could offer.

Stable enough does not mean completely stable

The word stable can sound intimidating.

It might bring to mind a good job, a certain amount of savings, a tidy home, a reliable car, excellent mental health, and a calendar filled with wholesome activities.

That’s a picture of one kind of life. It is not an entrance requirement for love.

You can have a disability and date. You can be unemployed and date. You can live with family, receive assistance, see a therapist, or struggle with tasks that other people appear to do easily.

The more useful question is what your current circumstances mean for your ability to participate.

Suppose you have a difficult month ahead. Your bills are paid, but money is tight. You have enough energy to meet someone twice a month, provided the plans are inexpensive and arranged in advance.

That may be perfectly workable. You can say so without apologizing for the circumstances of your life.

Now suppose you are facing an immediate housing emergency. Most days are consumed by finding somewhere safe to sleep, making urgent calls, and protecting your belongings.

You might still want companionship, perhaps desperately. But adding the demands and uncertainty of meeting new romantic partners could be more than you want to manage right now.

Choosing to concentrate on immediate needs would be a practical decision, not a judgment that people without secure housing are unworthy of relationships.

The same distinction applies to mental health.

Feeling anxious, depressed, distracted, or exhausted does not automatically make you unready. But if distress is seriously interfering with eating, sleeping, caring for yourself, or carrying out basic tasks, it deserves attention.

The US National Institute of Mental Health recommends seeking professional help when distress is substantial or persistent and affects daily functioning. It also recommends immediate crisis help when someone is at risk of suicide or self-harm (National Institute of Mental Health, 2025).

Those recommendations concern health and safety. They are not dating eligibility rules.

You can seek care while dating. You can receive care while in a relationship. You can also decide that concentrating on your health is enough for the moment.

If you are in immediate danger or experiencing a crisis, your safety comes first. The support resources described later in this handbook can help you find appropriate assistance.

What about ordinary difficulties?

A surprising number of readiness questions are really scheduling questions.

Can you make plans without repeatedly sacrificing sleep? Can you afford the kinds of dates you’re considering? Do you have some way to communicate when you are unavailable?

If you routinely need several days to recover from a social outing, you might choose one date every few weeks rather than a crowded weekend of introductions.

If remembering appointments is difficult, you might use a calendar and ask for plans to be confirmed in writing.

If your reactions take time to become clear, you can give yourself that time before agreeing to another meeting.

These are ways of arranging dating around your actual life. They are not attempts to conceal difficulties until someone becomes attached to you.

The earlier chapters discussed feelings that take time to identify, the costs of masking, and the practical challenges of energy and executive function. You can use that knowledge here without turning dating into another exhausting performance.

You are allowed to choose a pace that leaves you enough energy to live the rest of your life.

When a relationship starts looking like a rescue

There is nothing wrong with wanting love because you are lonely.

Loneliness can hurt. Wanting affection, companionship, sex, emotional closeness, or someone to share everyday experiences with is an ordinary reason to consider dating.

You do not need to become completely satisfied with solitude before you are allowed to want someone beside you.

The trouble begins when a relationship stops being something you want and becomes something you believe you cannot be okay without.

Consider these two thoughts:

“I miss having someone to spend Sunday mornings with. I’d like to build that kind of connection.”

“If nobody chooses me soon, it means there’s something fundamentally wrong with me.”

Both people may be lonely. But the second thought places far more weight on the outcome.

If getting a relationship becomes proof that you matter, the possibility of losing it can start to feel unbearable.

That can affect your decisions.

Spielmann and colleagues studied fear of being single across several settings. They found associations between greater fear and behaviors such as remaining dependent on unsatisfying relationships, being less likely to initiate a breakup in a less satisfying relationship, and showing greater interest in prospective partners who appeared less responsive (Spielmann et al., 2013).

An important complication emerged: participants did not consistently report lower standards when asked what they wanted in a partner.

In other words, what someone says they would accept and what they actually choose can be different.

This does not mean that everyone afraid of being single will enter a bad relationship. Nor does it mean that loneliness makes someone incapable of good judgment.

It means that fear is worth noticing, especially when your actions begin contradicting your own needs.

A decision made from fear

At 29, Leah has spent years hoping to find a partner. She meets someone who seems interested, and the attention feels wonderful.

After a few dates, she notices that the person frequently dismisses her preferences. When Leah suggests something simple, such as choosing an accessible meeting place, the response makes her feel that her needs are an inconvenience.

Leah knows she wants a relationship in which both people take each other’s needs seriously.

But another thought keeps interrupting:

What if nobody else ever wants me?

She starts wondering whether she should stop bringing up her preferences.

The important question is not whether this person might eventually become more considerate. It is whether Leah is beginning to give up something important because she is frightened of being alone.

She can choose not to continue. She does not need proof that the other person is terrible. The absence of a workable mutual relationship is enough.

Now imagine the same situation without the fear.

Leah might still feel disappointed. She might wish the person had responded differently. But she would have more freedom to consider whether continuing is right for her.

That freedom matters.

The relationship on a pedestal

Another set of studies examined the belief that a romantic relationship is necessary for real happiness. Across four studies, researchers found that stronger beliefs of this kind were associated with greater fear of being single, which was associated with lower life satisfaction. Among partnered participants, the pattern also related to lower relationship satisfaction (Dennett & Girme, 2025).

These were associations, not proof that a particular belief directly causes unhappiness.

Still, the findings suggest a useful question:

Are you hoping a relationship will add something meaningful to your life, or are you asking it to establish that your life is meaningful at all?

The distinction is not absolute. Relationships meet needs. A loving partner can provide emotional support, companionship, and a sense of belonging.

But another person cannot reasonably be responsible for proving your entire worth.

A partner is someone with their own needs, struggles, boundaries, and choices. They cannot promise never to disappoint you, never to need space, or never to change their mind.

When you need another person to eliminate every fear you have about yourself, ordinary relationship uncertainty becomes much harder to bear.

You do not have to eliminate loneliness before dating. You can, however, begin questioning the idea that being chosen is what makes you valuable.

If being on your own has never felt safe

If solitude registers as danger rather than quiet, dating can become a way of not being alone rather than a way of meeting someone. The urgency feels like readiness, but it tends to pick badly.

A useful test: would this person still appeal if you felt steady? If you can’t tell, that’s worth knowing before someone else becomes the thing holding you up.

This isn’t a rule that you must be fully settled first. It’s a reason to build at least one source of steadiness that isn’t a partner, so a relationship can be something you choose rather than something you need.

What is holding you up besides romance?

Think about an ordinary bad day.

You get home exhausted. Something important went wrong. You want to talk, but the person you were dating is busy, asleep, or dealing with a problem of their own.

What happens next?

Having a partner can make difficult days easier. But if that partner is your only available source of comfort, advice, companionship, or practical help, their temporary unavailability may leave you with nowhere else to turn.

This is one reason to consider support while thinking about readiness.

A review by Girme and colleagues found substantial differences in how single people experience their lives. Supportive friendships and family relationships were among the factors associated with greater well-being and satisfaction with singlehood (Girme et al., 2023).

Support also appears in broader research on relationship safety. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identifies positive social relationships and access to support and services among protective factors in its framework for preventing intimate partner violence. These factors work within a larger social context and cannot predict what will happen in one person’s relationship (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

Neither finding means you need a large social circle before dating.

For some people, building even one dependable connection takes considerable effort.

Your support might come from a longtime friend, a sibling, a trusted online community, a therapist, a neighbor, a support group, or someone you share an interest with.

Different people may be able to help with different things.

A friend might enjoy hearing about a date but be unable to help during a crisis. A counselor might help you think through emotional reactions but not be available when your car breaks down. An online community might offer belonging even when nobody lives nearby.

You do not need every form of support from every person. It helps to know what you have, what you can reasonably ask for, and where there are gaps.

When belonging has been difficult

For some people, loneliness is not just the absence of close relationships. It is the experience of repeatedly feeling out of place.

Maybe conversations move too quickly. Maybe your interests rarely match those around you. Maybe you spend much of your social life masking, so that even friendly interactions leave you feeling unseen.

A systematic review of 34 studies on loneliness in autistic adults identified social acceptance, relationships, and self-acceptance as potentially important factors. The authors also discussed collective loneliness, a lack of belonging within wider society, while noting that this experience has received too little direct research attention (Grace et al., 2022).

Much of the evidence was cross-sectional, so it cannot establish which factors cause loneliness or which approaches reliably reduce it.

But it highlights a distinction worth making.

Someone can want a romantic partner and also want a community where they do not constantly have to explain themselves.

Those are related needs, but they are not identical.

If you have spent years feeling excluded, a romantic relationship may seem like the only available route to belonging. It does not have to be.

You can look for people who share your interests, ways of communicating, or experiences while also exploring romance. You may find community in places that look nothing like conventional social life.

And if your support network is currently very small, that does not disqualify you from dating.

It simply gives you another part of life worth developing alongside it.

You can be happy single without having to prefer it

Some dating advice treats singlehood as a problem to solve. Other advice insists that you must become delighted with being single before you can date responsibly.

Neither expectation leaves much room for real people.

In a US survey conducted in 2019, half of single adults said they were not currently looking for dates or a committed relationship. Among people who were not looking, 47% cited having more important priorities as a major reason, and 44% said liking being single was a major reason (Brown, 2020).

Some people simply do not want a relationship at a particular point in their lives.

Others would enjoy one but do not want to pursue it actively.

Others are content much of the time and still feel lonely occasionally.

A review of research on singlehood found considerable variation in single people’s well-being, preferences, and social circumstances (Girme et al., 2023). In two separate studies, Pepping and colleagues also identified different patterns of attachment-related experiences among single adults, associated with differences in well-being and attitudes toward singlehood (Pepping et al., 2025).

Those patterns were statistical groupings, not permanent categories into which every person must fit.

The point is simpler: single people do not all feel the same way about being single.

Some research finds higher average well-being among partnered people, but an average does not describe every individual. It also does not prove that obtaining a relationship will automatically make a particular person happier.

You can enjoy your independence and still want romance. You can dislike being single without accepting an unsuitable relationship. You can decide that you do not want to date this year without making a prediction about the rest of your life.

A relationship is one possible part of a good life. It is not the only possible foundation for one.

What can you bring to another person?

So far, most of the questions have concerned your own needs.

Readiness also involves recognizing that someone else will have needs.

This does not mean becoming a perfect partner in advance. It means being willing to participate in a relationship between two people rather than treating the other person as someone who exists to meet your expectations.

You might need regular reassurance. The person you’re dating might need quiet time after work.

You might prefer written messages. They might find extended texting exhausting.

You might need plans made several days ahead. They might have responsibilities that sometimes change unexpectedly.

Neither person’s needs automatically cancel the other’s.

The question is whether you can discuss the differences and find arrangements both people freely accept.

Different needs do not automatically mean incompatibility

Consider Sam and Oliver, two autistic men in their thirties who have recently started dating.

Sam finds unfamiliar restaurants exhausting. Oliver enjoys going out, but he has trouble making decisions when presented with too many options.

They initially struggle to plan dates. Sam worries that suggesting another quiet evening will seem boring. Oliver worries that his indecision will be interpreted as disinterest.

Eventually, Sam explains that quieter places help him enjoy their time together. Oliver says that choosing between two suggestions is easier than inventing a plan from scratch.

They agree to alternate who proposes a couple of possible activities.

Neither person needs to suppress their preferences. Both have made an adjustment.

Now consider Tasha, a neurotypical woman dating Ren, who has ADHD.

Ren sometimes forgets to confirm plans. Tasha feels unsettled when she does not know whether an arrangement is definite.

Ren explains the difficulty and begins using reminders. Tasha explains what information she needs and agrees that a clear confirmation is more useful than frequent casual messages.

They are working on a shared practical problem. Tasha does not have to become Ren’s personal calendar, and Ren does not have to pretend remembering comes easily.

An Irish qualitative study of adults with ADHD described concerns involving rejection, everyday responsibilities, and unequal partner-caregiver roles. Respondents also identified self-understanding as important. Because participants were specifically asked about negative relationship effects, the findings should not be treated as a description of everyone with ADHD (O’Brien et al., 2026).

The point is not that every difficulty can be solved with reminders or planning. Sometimes two people’s needs genuinely cannot be reconciled.

But knowing your needs gives you a better chance of finding out what is workable together.

A US study involving 106 autistic adults in relationships found no statistically significant difference in average relationship satisfaction between groups reporting autistic, neurotypical, or other-neurodivergent partners. Participants described different strengths and difficulties, but the research relied on autistic respondents’ reports, with a self-selected sample and relatively high satisfaction overall (Khaw & Vernon, 2026).

That finding does not establish that every neurotype pairing works equally well. It does challenge the assumption that a partner’s neurotype alone determines whether a relationship will be satisfying.

The sculpture-and-glasses picture from earlier in this book applies here. You bring your perspective, and the other person brings theirs. Neither person can assume that their view explains the whole situation.

Readiness includes being willing to describe what you see and listen to what the other person sees.

Being available is not the same as being constantly accessible

You do not have to answer every message immediately, enjoy every activity, or be capable of providing support at any hour.

You do need to be reasonably honest about what another person can expect.

For example:

“I like spending time with you. I usually need a quiet evening after work, so I’m better at making weekend plans.”

Or:

“I’m interested in seeing you again. Sometimes I need a day to think about how I feel. I’ll let you know rather than leaving you guessing.”

These statements give the other person information.

They do not require that person to agree.

Someone who prefers more frequent contact may decide that your available pace does not work for them. You might likewise discover that another person’s communication pattern does not work for you.

That can be disappointing without making either person wrong.

The goal is not to promise more than you can provide in order to keep someone interested.

It is to let both people make informed choices.

What if your past makes dating frightening?

Sometimes the question “Am I ready?” is tangled with memories of what happened before.

Perhaps someone repeatedly criticized your needs. Perhaps affection came with unpredictable anger. Perhaps you learned early in life that expressing disagreement was dangerous.

Those experiences may influence what closeness feels like now.

A meta-analysis combining 201 effect sizes from 23 reports found a modest average negative association between childhood emotional maltreatment and well-being in adult romantic relationships. The association was smaller in studies with stronger methodological quality (Cao et al., 2022).

That finding is about an average across studies. It cannot tell you whether your particular past will determine your future relationship.

It also does not mean that everyone who experienced childhood maltreatment has the same difficulties or needs treatment before dating.

You might already have developed ways of recognizing when something feels unsafe. You might have supportive relationships that have helped you learn what respect looks like.

Or you might notice that situations that seem ordinary to someone else produce intense fear for you.

In the latter case, support from a qualified mental-health professional may help you understand your responses and make choices that feel more manageable.

You do not need to share painful experiences with a new date before you want to. You can ask for slower pacing or clearer communication without explaining every reason.

And you can decide that you need a break from dating without deciding that you are incapable of relationships.

Current danger is different from past difficulty

If someone is currently threatening you, monitoring you, frightening you, ignoring your refusal, or making it difficult for you to leave, the immediate issue is safety, not whether you are emotionally ready to be a better partner.

You do not need to become more accommodating to make threatening behavior acceptable.

Seek support from someone safe or a professional service experienced in relationship abuse. The support chapters later in this book cover options for immediate help.

The CDC emphasizes that intimate partner violence arises from interacting individual, relationship, community, and societal factors. No single factor explains or predicts it (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

A history of trauma does not make someone responsible for abuse directed at them.

There is another side to this responsibility. If you recognize that you are ignoring another person’s boundaries, repeatedly pressuring someone, or responding to rejection with threatening behavior, stop the behavior and seek appropriate professional support. Another person’s refusal must be respected, regardless of how frightening rejection feels.

Readiness never includes a right to someone else’s attention or consent.

If you're dating someone who is still rebuilding

Someone can be in the middle of a hard stretch and still be good company, honest, and worth your time. “Not fully settled” is not the same as “not ready”.

What’s worth watching is direction and share. Are they doing their own work, and is the relationship one part of their life rather than the whole structure holding it up?

You are not their treatment plan. Caring about someone doesn’t require becoming the only thing keeping them steady, and noticing when it’s tipping that way is a kindness to you both.

A readiness check without a passing grade

The following exercise is not a psychological test. It has no score, no scientifically established cutoff, and no correct number of answers.

Its purpose is to help you notice what is true about your life right now.

You can write your answers, say them aloud, or simply think about them. You do not have to decide everything immediately.

1. What am I hoping dating will add?

Try to name something specific.

Maybe you want companionship, physical affection, a long-term partner, shared activities, or the possibility of building a family.

Then ask whether you also hope dating will remove a painful belief about yourself.

“I want to feel wanted” is an understandable answer. It is also worth asking what happens to your sense of worth when someone does not choose you.

2. How much room do I actually have?

Think about your current week rather than an imaginary version of yourself with unlimited energy.

What could you realistically manage? One short date every couple of weeks? A regular evening? An occasional conversation while you concentrate on other commitments?

What would you have to sacrifice to create that time?

If the answer is sleep, medication, essential responsibilities, or your only recovery time, reconsider the pace.

3. What happens when dating becomes disappointing?

Imagine that someone you like declines a second date.

You would probably feel something: disappointment, embarrassment, sadness, irritation, or perhaps an emotion you cannot identify until later.

Who or what could help you through that experience?

You might speak to a friend, use a familiar routine, spend time on an interest, write down what happened, or get support from a counselor.

The goal is not to avoid feeling bad. It is to have options besides asking the other person to change their answer.

4. What needs would another person need to know about?

Think about practical information that would make spending time with you easier.

Maybe you need advance notice for plans, time to respond to questions, a quiet setting, or explicit communication.

You do not have to give a new person your complete personal history. What matters is being able to communicate relevant needs when they become important.

5. Can I respect another person’s freedom to choose?

Imagine someone saying they like you but do not want a relationship.

Would you be willing to accept that answer, even if it hurt?

Now reverse the situation. If someone wanted more closeness than you did, would you be willing to state your own limits?

You do not need to enjoy rejection or difficult conversations. But respecting both people’s choices is essential.

6. What currently needs more attention than dating?

This question might have no urgent answer.

Or you might identify a health concern, a practical emergency, an unsafe situation, an important obligation, or a lack of support that you would like to address.

Notice the difference between “I have a difficulty” and “this difficulty currently needs most of my attention.”

You are deciding how to use your resources, not making a judgment about your value.

What your answers might tell you

There are several reasonable next steps.

You might decide to date now. You have some interest, enough room to participate, and a realistic sense of your needs. You do not have to wait for a stronger feeling of certainty.

You might decide to date gently. You are interested, but your available energy, finances, schedule, or emotional capacity are limited. You can choose fewer dates, slower communication, or activities that are easier to manage. You can tell people what your pace looks like and let them decide whether it suits them.

You might decide not to date right now. Something else needs your attention, or dating simply does not appeal to you at present. You can spend time building stability, finding support, or enjoying your life without setting a deadline for returning.

None of these choices is a permanent identity.

You can change your mind when your circumstances change.

When readiness changes after you begin

A decision to start dating is not a promise to continue indefinitely.

Perhaps you feel interested in someone, but after a few meetings you realize you are becoming exhausted.

Perhaps a new responsibility appears. Perhaps the relationship is developing faster than you want. Perhaps you simply discover that you need more time for yourself.

You can acknowledge the change.

If you want to continue at a slower pace, you might say:

“I’ve enjoyed getting to know you, but I have less time and energy than I expected. I’d like to slow down to meeting every other week. Would that work for you?”

The other person is free to agree or decline.

If you need to stop, you can be clear:

“I’ve realized I don’t have the capacity to keep dating right now. I don’t want to keep making plans I can’t follow through on, so I’m going to stop.”

You do not need to construct an elaborate justification. Being respectful means communicating clearly and not making promises you cannot keep.

It does not mean that the other person must approve of your decision.

The reverse also applies. Someone you are dating may discover that they are not ready or do not want to continue. Their choice is not proof that you were inadequate.

It is possible for two people to want good things and still be unable to make the same relationship work at the same time.

You are allowed to begin as you are

The idea of readiness can become another way to postpone life indefinitely.

You might decide that you will date once you lose weight, earn more money, organize your home, become more outgoing, get a diagnosis, recover from every painful memory, or learn to behave exactly as you think a partner would want.

Some of those goals may be worthwhile for their own reasons.

But there will always be another improvement you could make.

You do not have to reach a final, polished version of yourself before another person can know you.

What you can do is take responsibility for your current circumstances.

You can acknowledge your limits instead of hiding them. You can build support rather than expecting a new partner to supply all of it. You can notice when fear is pushing you toward choices you do not actually want. You can respect another person’s boundaries while maintaining your own.

And you can give yourself permission to pause when dating stops being workable.

The question is not whether you deserve a relationship. Your circumstances, history, health, and experience do not determine your worth.

The question is whether dating is a choice you can participate in with enough freedom, capacity, and consideration for both people.

You already have a clearer picture of what you want, what you need, and what you can realistically offer.

Now that you know your needs, here’s what dating actually is.
