Chapter 11: Attraction, looks and what lasts

Someone catches your attention across a room. Maybe it’s their face, their smile, their clothes, or the way they move. You haven’t spoken to them, but you’d like to.

Now imagine someone else. Their photograph didn’t particularly interest you. Then you spend an hour talking. They’re funny in a way you appreciate. They listen when you explain something complicated. You discover that you enjoy how their mind works. Later, you find yourself looking forward to seeing them again.

Both experiences are possible. Neither tells you everything you need to know about whether a relationship would work.

Attraction is the feeling of being drawn toward someone. Sometimes that feeling is immediate and physical. Sometimes it develops through conversation, familiarity, or affection. Sometimes you like someone enormously but never want to date them.

If you’re new to dating, it can be difficult to know which feelings deserve your attention. A beautiful face might seem like proof you’ve found someone special. A pleasant first date without immediate excitement might seem like proof there’s nothing there.

Neither conclusion is necessarily right.

The useful question isn’t just “Are they attractive?” It’s “Am I drawn to this person, what am I drawn to, and what am I actually learning about them?”

Yes, looks matter

There’s no need to pretend physical appearance doesn’t matter.

In a review and meta-analysis covering 97 studies, physical attractiveness was meaningfully associated with romantic evaluations of real potential partners. The association was approximately the same for heterosexual men and women. In the same analysis, earning prospects had a much smaller association with romantic evaluations (Eastwick et al., 2014).

A later replication involving 307 speed-dating participants also found that attractiveness and earning potential were related to romantic interest, without significant differences between men and women in those associations (Selterman et al., 2015).

So if you notice someone’s face first, there’s nothing unusual or inherently wrong with that. Physical attraction is one possible reason to want to meet someone.

And if physical attraction is important to you, you don’t have to apologize for wanting it.

But there’s a distinction between finding someone attractive and deciding they’re a good person to date.

A person can be beautiful and inconsiderate. They can be ordinary-looking to most observers and wonderful to spend time with. Someone can be attractive to you without being attractive to your friends.

Attraction is information about your response to someone. It isn’t a certificate of their character.

Conventional attractiveness isn’t the same as personal attraction

Conventional attractiveness means having an appearance that many observers tend to rate highly.

There is evidence that people agree to some extent about physical beauty. A review bringing together 11 meta-analyses found agreement within and across cultures, as well as a tendency for conventionally attractive people to receive more favorable judgments and treatment (Langlois et al., 2000).

That matters. Appearance can affect how people are received before anyone knows their personality.

It would be dishonest to tell someone who feels overlooked because of their appearance that looks simply don’t matter. Sometimes they do influence who gets noticed or approached.

But agreement about who looks attractive doesn’t mean everyone has identical preferences.

Think back to the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of this book. People can stand in different positions, notice different features, and bring different experiences to what they’re seeing.

One person might love a broad smile. Another notices a distinctive voice. Someone else finds enthusiasm, facial expressions, or a particular way of speaking increasingly attractive.

These are personal responses, not votes in a competition.

You don’t need to find the person whom the largest number of strangers would choose. You need to discover whom you enjoy, and whether that person feels similarly about you.

Attraction isn’t one single feeling

Imagine three people.

At 29, Jordan sees a photograph of a handsome man on a dating app. Jordan would enjoy looking at him again, but there’s no particular desire to meet him.

At 46, Elena has been talking with another woman for several weeks. She loves their conversations and feels a growing wish to spend time together. She isn’t yet sure whether she wants romantic or physical closeness.

At 34, Priya is drawn immediately to someone at a party. The physical attraction is strong. After talking, though, she realizes she doesn’t particularly enjoy the interaction.

All three people are responding to something different.

For practical purposes, it helps to separate four experiences:

  • Physical attraction: You find someone’s appearance appealing.
  • Enjoyment: You like their company, conversation, humor, or personality.
  • Romantic or sexual interest: You want some form of romantic or physical closeness with them.
  • Curiosity: You want to learn more about them, even if you’re unsure what you feel yet.

These aren’t rigid scientific categories or a test you have to pass. They’re simply useful questions to ask yourself.

They can overlap, but they don’t have to.

You can admire someone’s looks without wanting physical intimacy. You can enjoy a deep conversation without wanting romance. You can want romantic companionship without experiencing sexual attraction. As discussed in Chapter 7, people also differ in whether and how they experience sexual or romantic attraction at all.

You don’t need to manufacture a missing feeling to make an otherwise appealing relationship possible.

A common beginner mistake is treating one kind of attraction as proof of all the others.

“They’re gorgeous, so this must be love.”

Or:

“They were really nice to me, so I should want to date them.”

Or even:

“I don’t feel intensely excited, so this can’t become anything.”

Instead, try describing what is actually present.

“I find them physically attractive, but I don’t know them.”

“I enjoy talking with them, and I’d like another conversation.”

“They’re kind, but I don’t want a romantic relationship with them.”

These statements are more useful because they don’t force a conclusion before you have enough information.

Your type isn’t a prediction

Many people have a type.

You might usually notice tall people, people with particular facial features, someone with a certain style, or people who seem outgoing and confident.

You might also have a mental picture of your future partner that’s been developing for years.

There’s nothing wrong with noticing patterns in your attraction. The problem begins when you assume those patterns can predict exactly whom you’ll enjoy dating.

What people say they want versus whom they like

In a 2008 speed-dating study, participants described the qualities they wanted in a partner before meeting potential dates. Traditional differences appeared in what heterosexual men and women said they preferred. But those stated preferences did not predict their actual romantic interest in the same way when they interacted with real people (Eastwick & Finkel, 2008).

That doesn’t mean everyone is confused about what they want.

A much larger study published in 2025 examined 10,358 people across 43 countries. It found that matching someone’s stated ideals had a modest association with how positively they evaluated a partner. The association became smaller after accounting for qualities that people generally liked, and the effect of matching any particular trait was very small on average (Eastwick et al., 2025).

So the evidence supports a middle position.

Your preferences may contain some useful information. They just aren’t a reliable instruction manual for every person you’ll meet.

Consider Marcus, who is 41 and has recently started dating men.

He has always imagined himself with someone tall, athletic, and highly outgoing. Those are the people he tends to notice first.

Then he meets Daniel, who’s shorter, quieter, and enthusiastic about restoring old radios. Daniel doesn’t resemble Marcus’s usual type.

But their conversation is comfortable. They laugh at similar things. Marcus finds himself thinking about something Daniel said the next day.

Marcus doesn’t need to decide that Daniel is secretly his ideal partner. He also doesn’t need to reject Daniel because he fails to match the picture Marcus had in mind.

If both are interested, Marcus can choose to learn more.

A type can help you describe what usually catches your attention. It shouldn’t automatically overrule an enjoyable experience with a real person.

And the reverse matters too. Meeting someone who perfectly matches your type doesn’t mean you’ve found someone compatible.

A preference isn’t a requirement

Suppose you usually prefer dark hair.

That’s a preference.

Suppose you know you don’t want a relationship involving sexual intimacy.

That’s a different kind of consideration, tied to the relationship you actually want.

A flexible appearance preference isn’t necessarily worth treating as a non-negotiable condition. At the same time, you aren’t required to abandon a genuine limit or force yourself into a relationship you don’t want.

Chapter 13 will examine preferences, compatibility, and dealbreakers more carefully.

For now, ask whether a preference helps you recognize what you enjoy or prevents you from discovering something you might enjoy.

Attraction can change as you get to know someone

First impressions feel important because they’re often all you have.

After a photograph or a brief conversation, you may have a strong opinion about someone. But you’re judging a very small amount of information.

Longer acquaintance can change the picture.

Researchers studying romantic evaluations found that as people became more familiar with one another, their judgments of desirability became increasingly personal rather than based on widely shared impressions (Eastwick & Hunt, 2014).

Another study of 167 couples found that partners who had known each other longer before dating tended to be less closely matched in observer-rated physical attractiveness than couples who started dating shortly after meeting (Hunt et al., 2015).

The findings suggest that knowing someone can make the relationship between appearance and attraction more complicated than it appears at first glance.

In laboratory conversations, another study found that greater familiarity increased interpersonal liking. Feeling comfortable, knowing more about the other person, and perceiving them as responsive were associated with that liking (Reis et al., 2011).

That last study examined nonromantic interactions between people of the same sex. It doesn’t prove that sexual or romantic attraction develops just because people spend time together.

In fact, none of these studies establishes that an initially unwanted romantic relationship will eventually become desirable.

Attraction can change. That doesn’t mean it will.

When another date makes sense

Suppose Mei and Alex meet for coffee.

Mei doesn’t feel an immediate burst of excitement. Alex isn’t the sort of person Mei usually notices.

But the conversation is enjoyable. Both people seem comfortable, and Mei would like to know more.

Mei might think:

“I’m not sure whether there’s romantic attraction here, but I’d enjoy seeing Alex again.”

That’s enough reason to consider another date, provided Alex wants one too.

It isn’t a promise of future attraction. It’s simply a decision to explore something that feels pleasant and interesting.

Now imagine a different first date.

Mei feels uncomfortable throughout, dreads the idea of meeting again, or knows that a romantic connection isn’t wanted.

There’s no obligation to arrange another date.

You don’t have to perform an experiment on yourself to prove that attraction could grow.

No particular number of dates is required. There’s no rule saying you must give someone three chances, or five, or keep going until they do something wrong.

You can continue because you’re curious. You can stop because you aren’t interested.

The other person has exactly the same freedom.

You cannot make someone become attracted to you

This is the other side of the familiarity question.

Perhaps you’ve known someone for months. You’ve been friendly, attentive, and available. You feel increasingly attached.

They’ve told you they aren’t interested romantically.

It’s tempting to think that if getting to know someone can increase attraction, spending more time with them might change their mind.

But research about familiarity isn’t permission to pursue someone who has declined.

A person’s no isn’t a problem to solve.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly trying to change someone’s answer, stop the romantic pursuit. You may need some distance to let your feelings settle. Respect their decision without asking them to defend it.

Friendship is meaningful in its own right, but it isn’t a waiting room for a future relationship.

The strongest attraction isn’t necessarily the best relationship

Imagine two people you’ve dated.

One is extremely attractive to you. You look forward to seeing their face and feel excited before every meeting. But conversations are difficult, and you don’t enjoy how they treat you.

The other person isn’t as immediately striking, but you look forward to spending time together. You’re interested in what they think. You feel comfortable being yourself, and some romantic attraction is present.

Which relationship would be more rewarding?

You can’t answer that from appearance alone.

What long-term studies found

In one study of heterosexual US newlyweds, observer-rated facial attractiveness wasn’t a consistent predictor of marital satisfaction. Being similar in attractiveness also wasn’t associated with greater marital satisfaction. Some specific associations did appear, but they didn’t establish a general rule that more attractive couples were happier (McNulty et al., 2008).

A different analysis pooled four longitudinal studies of heterosexual newlyweds. It found that husbands tended to report greater marital satisfaction when their wives were rated more physically attractive. The same association wasn’t found between husbands’ attractiveness and wives’ satisfaction (Meltzer et al., 2014).

These results need to be considered together.

It would be inaccurate to say that appearance never affects relationship satisfaction. But it would also be inaccurate to suggest that choosing the most conventionally attractive available partner is a reliable route to a happy relationship.

Both studies examined married heterosexual couples in the United States. They don’t establish what will happen in every relationship, and they don’t show that attractiveness causes marital success.

More importantly for a beginner, they don’t tell you how a particular person will communicate, respond to disagreements, share responsibilities, or treat you when life becomes difficult.

Those qualities require actual knowledge of the person.

A good-looking stranger is still a stranger.

You can appreciate their appearance while recognizing how much remains unknown.

Gender, orientation, and the stories people tell about attraction

Dating advice often makes sweeping claims about gender.

You may have heard that men primarily care about appearance while women primarily care about money or stability.

The actual research is more complicated.

Some studies do find differences between heterosexual men and women when they describe an ideal partner in the abstract. But the speed-dating studies and meta-analysis discussed earlier found much smaller or no statistically significant gender differences in how attractiveness predicted interest in people participants actually met (Eastwick et al., 2014; Eastwick & Finkel, 2008; Selterman et al., 2015).

The large international study also found that participants of both sexes underestimated how much physical attractiveness was associated with their romantic evaluations, with the underestimation particularly pronounced among women (Eastwick et al., 2025).

And the newlywed research found a different pattern of average associations in established marriages than the studies of initial romantic interest (Meltzer et al., 2014).

These findings aren’t a reason to replace one stereotype with another. They are a reason to stop treating a gender label as a substitute for learning about an individual.

Perspective: Men

If you’ve been taught that appearance is supposed to be your main concern, you can question that expectation. The research on initial attraction doesn’t require you to prioritize looks over every other quality. You can want physical attraction, emotional closeness, enjoyable conversation, and a partner who treats you well. None of those preferences makes you less masculine.

Perspective: Women

If you’ve been taught that caring about looks is shallow, or that you should choose someone mainly because they’re dependable and interested in you, you don’t have to accept that rule either. Physical attraction may matter to you. You can want both a relationship that feels good and a partner you find appealing. Kindness doesn’t create an obligation to feel romantic desire.

Perspective: Non-binary

Much of the research on attraction sorts participants into men and women. That leaves important gaps about nonbinary people’s experiences. The findings about heterosexual men and women cannot tell you what you’re supposed to find attractive. Your preferences deserve to be understood on their own terms, without being forced into either category.

Sexual orientation matters to the evidence, too.

A German survey of 710 lesbian, gay, and bisexual participants found variation in stated partner preferences associated with gender, sexual orientation, relationship orientation, and other factors (Klümper et al., 2024).

Another German study of 893 lesbian, gay, and heterosexual adults found both similarities and differences in preferred partner body shapes, as well as associations involving body-image concerns (Cordes et al., 2021).

These were surveys of preferences, not observations of whom participants actually dated or how their relationships turned out.

They reinforce a simple caution: people differ, and a preference measured across a group shouldn’t be treated as a rule about the person sitting across from you.

The most useful way to understand someone’s attraction is to learn what that individual experiences and wants.

When first impressions are especially misleading

For some people, a short social encounter tells others remarkably little about who they are.

Imagine Samira, an autistic woman meeting another autistic woman, Beth, for coffee.

Samira’s expressions don’t always change much while she’s listening. Beth sometimes looks away when she’s thinking and takes a while to answer questions.

Someone watching a short recording might mistake these behaviors for disinterest.

But during the conversation, both women are engaged. Beth is thinking carefully about what Samira said. Samira enjoys the conversation and wants to continue it.

Nothing about their level of interest can be reliably settled from one facial expression or moment of eye contact.

There is relevant research on how autistic adults are perceived.

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2025 brought together 29 studies from 21 articles about first impressions of autistic people. On average, the studies found less favorable first impressions of autistic targets, although results varied considerably. The differences were generally less evident when observers received written transcripts rather than visual or vocal information (Wanigasekera et al., 2025).

That is an important distinction.

The findings concern how people are judged, not whether autistic people are inherently less attractive, worthwhile, or capable of rewarding relationships.

A smaller study involving 32 autistic and 32 nonautistic observers found that both groups rated brief videos of autistic adults less favorably on certain first-impression traits. Yet autistic observers expressed greater social interest overall, and their less favorable ratings of autistic targets didn’t translate into the same reduction in desire to interact (DeBrabander et al., 2019).

These studies measured first impressions and social interest, not actual romantic relationships. They can’t tell you whom an autistic person will attract or whom they will find attractive.

They do show why a quick judgment of someone’s social presentation should be treated cautiously.

A different way of communicating isn’t proof of disinterest

If eye contact is uncomfortable for you, you don’t have to pretend it feels natural.

If you need a few seconds to find the words for what you’re thinking, that doesn’t mean you lack interest in the conversation.

And if someone else communicates differently from what you expect, you don’t have to decide immediately that they’re cold, unfriendly, or dismissive.

You can notice what you observe without pretending to know the reason.

For example:

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you like to meet again?”

That question gives the other person an opportunity to answer directly. It doesn’t require either person to interpret every gesture correctly.

If your date's expressions or responses are different from what you expect

A person who looks away, speaks with an unfamiliar rhythm, or takes longer to answer may still be interested in you. Those behaviors alone aren’t enough to determine what someone feels. Rather than assuming, pay attention to what they say and ask when something matters.

You don’t need to become an expert in someone’s neurotype to date them respectfully. You need to be willing to learn how that particular person communicates. If you’re genuinely unsure whether they want another date, ask. Give their answer the same respect you’d want for yours.

This works in both directions. Two autistic people can misunderstand each other. So can an autistic person and a nonautistic partner. No pairing guarantees perfect interpretation.

You also don’t have to disclose a diagnosis or explain your entire personal history to justify ordinary preferences about conversation and communication.

And you’re allowed to decide that an interaction isn’t working for you, even when neither person has done anything wrong.

Sometimes the setting changes the experience

Suppose loud music makes it hard to follow a conversation.

You’re sitting across from someone at a crowded bar. You’re concentrating so much on the words that there’s little attention left for noticing whether you enjoy the person.

You might leave feeling disconnected.

That’s a reason to consider whether the setting influenced the experience, not evidence that you secretly liked the person.

If you’re uncertain and both of you want another meeting, you can suggest somewhere quieter.

“I’d like to talk again. Would you be comfortable meeting somewhere less noisy?”

You aren’t asking the other person to fix you or compensate for something. You’re asking for a setting in which you can participate more comfortably.

If a different setting helps, useful information has emerged. If it doesn’t, that’s useful information too.

You don’t have to force repeated dates to find out.

Don’t reject yourself on someone else’s behalf

So far, the question has mostly been whom you find attractive.

But many beginners also worry intensely about their own appearance.

You might look in the mirror and conclude that nobody you find attractive could possibly want you.

You might compare yourself to other people on dating apps, in public, or on social media. Maybe you believe you need a different body, face, height, age, or level of confidence before dating is worth attempting.

Appearance does influence first impressions, as the research discussed earlier makes clear (Langlois et al., 2000).

That doesn’t mean you can predict every individual’s attraction from your own opinion of your looks.

Consider Ren, who’s 52 and returning to dating after many years.

Ren has gray hair, uses a cane, and feels conspicuous in dating photographs. Before meeting anyone, Ren has already decided that people they’re interested in won’t be interested back.

But those decisions are being made without consulting the people involved.

Some people may not feel attracted to Ren. Others may. There is no way to establish an individual person’s interest simply by imagining their judgment in advance.

Ren doesn’t have to pretend rejection is impossible. It is a normal possibility in dating.

But Ren can allow another adult to express their own interest rather than rejecting every opportunity on that person’s behalf.

This isn’t a guarantee that someone will find you attractive.

It’s permission to stop treating your own harshest judgment as if it were everyone else’s opinion.

Likewise, another person’s rejection doesn’t establish your standing in some universal ranking. It tells you that this particular connection wasn’t mutual.

You don’t need to compete with everybody

When attraction becomes a ranking exercise, it’s easy to lose track of the person you’re actually meeting.

You might wonder:

“Am I better-looking than their last partner?”

“Could they find somebody more attractive?”

“Should I choose someone who gets more attention?”

Those questions don’t tell you much about whether the relationship is enjoyable or wanted by both people.

Try replacing the competition with direct observations.

Do you enjoy their company? Do they seem interested in knowing you? Are you interested in knowing them? Are both people freely choosing to continue?

Those questions concern a possible relationship rather than an imaginary contest.

The details of presenting yourself through clothing, hygiene, and photographs belong in Chapter 12. For now, remember that becoming more confident in your appearance doesn’t require believing that everyone will find you attractive.

It requires allowing attraction to be personal rather than treating it as a verdict on your value.

When someone is attractive but the connection isn’t good

Strong attraction can make ordinary warning signs easy to dismiss.

Imagine Luis, who’s 31, meeting a man he finds extraordinarily handsome.

The physical attraction is immediate. Luis wants the date to succeed.

But his date repeatedly interrupts him, makes jokes at his expense, and shows little interest when Luis tries to talk.

Afterward, Luis thinks:

“Maybe I didn’t come across well enough. Maybe if he gets to know me, he’ll treat me differently.”

There are two separate questions here.

The first is whether Luis finds the person attractive. Clearly, he does.

The second is whether Luis enjoys being treated this way. He doesn’t.

Physical attraction doesn’t answer the second question.

Luis doesn’t need to prove the other man is a terrible person or explain why the behavior occurred. He can simply decide he doesn’t want another date.

The important skill is not learning to ignore attraction. It’s learning to notice what attraction doesn’t tell you.

In the other direction, suppose someone treats you thoughtfully but you feel no wish for romantic closeness.

You still have permission to decline.

You can say:

“Thank you for spending time with me. I enjoyed talking, but I don’t feel a romantic connection.”

You don’t need to mention their appearance, compare them with others, or provide a detailed explanation of why attraction isn’t there.

A respectful rejection gives someone an answer. It doesn’t require you to supply an evaluation of their body or personality.

A small exercise: what are you actually responding to?

The next time you find someone attractive or finish a date, try separating your observations from your assumptions.

You can do this privately in a note, or just think through the questions.

1. What caught my attention?

Be specific.

Maybe it was their smile, voice, humor, knowledge, confidence, or the way they asked questions.

Don’t turn the observation into a character judgment.

“I liked their smile” is different from “They must be a wonderful person.”

2. What do I know from actual interaction?

What have you experienced rather than imagined?

Perhaps they listened to you, answered a question thoughtfully, respected a request, or shared something interesting.

If you’ve only seen a photograph, the answer may be: “Very little.”

That’s fine. Curiosity can exist without certainty.

3. What kind of interest do I feel?

Do you enjoy looking at them? Talking with them? Learning about them? Do you want romantic or physical closeness?

You can answer “I’m not sure.”

Uncertainty isn’t a problem that must be solved before every possible date.

4. What would I like to happen next?

Perhaps you want another conversation.

Perhaps you want to ask them on a date.

Perhaps you’re happy to remain acquaintances or friends.

Perhaps you don’t want further contact.

Choose based on your actual interest, not what you think someone else would expect you to feel.

This exercise isn’t a scientific test of attraction. It simply helps you make a decision without treating one powerful feeling as the entire story.

Three reasonable outcomes after a first date

A date doesn’t need to end with certainty about the future. Usually, you’re deciding only whether another interaction is worth exploring.

Here are three possibilities.

You’re attracted and want to continue

You enjoyed the person and would like another date.

You can be clear:

“I had a really good time. Would you like to get together again?”

If they say yes, you can discuss another meeting. If they say no, respect it.

You’re unsure, but curious

You didn’t feel intense attraction, yet you enjoyed yourself and want to learn more.

You can say:

“I enjoyed talking with you. I’d be interested in seeing you again, if you’d like that.”

You don’t have to promise that romantic attraction will develop.

If uncertainty persists over further dates, pay attention to whether you’re genuinely enjoying the connection or continuing only because you feel obligated.

You don’t want to continue

You may like the person, admire their appearance, or appreciate how they treated you, yet still know you don’t want another date.

You can decline politely.

“Thanks for meeting me. I don’t want to continue dating, but I wish you well.”

You don’t owe an additional meeting simply because the other person is interested.

And if someone declines you, their decision deserves the same respect.

What lasts beyond the first impression

Attraction matters because it can make you want to get closer to someone.

But getting closer reveals things appearance alone cannot.

You discover what conversations feel like after the introductions are over. You notice how someone responds when plans change. You learn whether spending time together is enjoyable when neither of you is trying to make an impressive first impression.

You also learn more about your own feelings.

Someone who seemed extraordinary from across the room may become less appealing once you know them. Someone who didn’t immediately catch your attention may become important to you.

And sometimes your first impression remains exactly right. A person you found attractive at the beginning may become someone you love. Another person may remain a good friend without romantic attraction ever developing.

There is no obligation for attraction to follow a particular schedule.

The aim isn’t to disregard appearance, force yourself to feel something, or find the person everybody else would choose.

It’s to let attraction open a possibility without allowing it to make the whole decision.

You can care about looks and still pay attention to character. You can be open to unexpected attraction without ignoring your own no. You can recognize someone else’s beauty without assuming they’re right for you, and recognize your own worth without expecting universal attraction.

A lasting relationship needs two people who actually want to be there. Attraction may help begin that connection, but discovering whether it works requires getting to know the person behind the first impression.
