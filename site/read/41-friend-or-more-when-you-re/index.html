Chapter 41: Friend or more? When you're not sure

Nadia and Malik have been friends for six months. They met at a board-game group, discovered they enjoy the same terrible science-fiction movies, and now exchange messages almost every day.

When Malik has a difficult week, Nadia checks on him. She remembers his birthday. She laughs at jokes other people don’t understand. Last Saturday, they spent four hours together at a café.

Malik is beginning to wonder whether Nadia likes him romantically.

There is a problem: everything Nadia has done is also something a good friend might do.

Meanwhile, Nadia has noticed that Malik sometimes seems nervous around her. She wonders whether something is bothering him. She has no idea he is considering asking her on a date.

Neither person is being dishonest. They simply understand the same friendship differently.

This can happen at any age, whether you’ve dated dozens of people or have never been on a date. You might think someone is romantically interested when they’re being friendly. You might miss someone’s interest entirely. You might develop feelings for a friend who doesn’t share them, or discover that a friendship has slowly become something different for both of you.

The difficulty isn’t that friendship and romance are opposites. It’s that they share so much.

Why friendship and romance can look alike

Think about what people do when they like spending time together.

They remember details. They make plans. They listen. They joke. They sometimes choose each other’s company over other options. They may share personal information, offer help, or seek comfort.

Those behaviors can appear in close friendships and romantic relationships.

A person who remembers your favorite coffee order might have a crush on you. They might also be an attentive friend who remembers everyone’s coffee order.

Someone who sends you a message every morning might want to date you. They might simply enjoy talking to you.

Even physical affection can mean different things to different people. Some friends hug regularly or hold hands. Others rarely touch anyone, including romantic partners.

The behavior tells you something about the connection. It doesn’t necessarily tell you what kind of connection the other person wants.

Researchers have studied this confusion for decades.

In a 1982 experiment involving American college students, men tended to interpret women’s friendly behavior as more sexually suggestive than women did. That study was limited to a particular heterosexual, binary-gender setting, but it demonstrated something important: two people can understand the same interaction differently (Abbey, 1982).

More recent research involving heterosexual and lesbian, gay, and bisexual participants also found experiences of friendliness being mistaken for sexual interest. The patterns differed across groups, and one small study of gay men did not find the overperception pattern being tested (Moran et al., 2024).

The point isn’t that one gender is bad at reading signals. People of different genders and orientations can misunderstand one another.

Researchers studying brief speed-dating encounters have also found that observers can sometimes recognize romantic interest, but not reliably for everyone. Accuracy varies, and what seems obvious to one person may be unclear to another (Place et al., 2009).

There is another distinction worth keeping straight: sexual attraction, romantic interest, and willingness to go on a date are not the same thing.

Someone might find you physically attractive without wanting a relationship. Someone might want romance but experience little sexual attraction. Someone might be curious about dating you without knowing whether romantic feelings will develop.

And someone can care deeply about you without wanting any of those things.

That is why the earlier chapters emphasized reading signals cautiously and asking when you need clarity. A signal may be useful information, but it isn’t a promise.

Sometimes friendship really does become romance

You may have heard two contradictory pieces of dating advice:

“Never date your friends.”

“Be friends first, and romance will happen naturally.”

Neither is a rule.

In research by Stinson and colleagues, approximately two-thirds of participants describing a current or previous romantic relationship reported that they had been friends before becoming romantic partners. The finding came from seven surveys involving 1,897 participants, primarily in Canada and the United States (Stinson et al., 2022).

In a follow-up study involving university students whose relationships began as friendships, many participants reported that neither person had entered the friendship intending it to become romantic.

The attraction developed later (Stinson et al., 2022).

This matters because it challenges the idea that everyone knows immediately whether someone is a potential romantic partner.

For some people, attraction comes quickly. For others, it grows after familiarity, trust, shared experiences, or an unexpected change in how they see someone.

Consider Rowan, 52, and Casey, 49.

They become friends through a community gardening project. Rowan is a woman who has dated men and women. Casey is nonbinary and has been single for several years.

For the first year, neither thinks about dating the other. They enjoy gardening, occasionally have lunch, and help one another with home projects.

Eventually, Rowan realizes she’d like to spend time with Casey in a different way. She isn’t certain whether it’s romantic interest, but she notices that the possibility of dating Casey now feels appealing.

Casey might be experiencing something similar. Or Casey might still see Rowan entirely as a friend.

The friendship hasn’t been a mistake, and neither person’s feelings are automatically wrong.

What happens next depends on what each person actually wants.

The statistic that does not mean what people think

The friends-first research is encouraging if you worry that romance must begin with an immediate spark. But it does not mean that two-thirds of friendships eventually become romantic relationships.

Those are completely different questions.

The researchers studied people whose relationships had already become romantic and asked how those relationships began.

They did not follow every friendship to see what proportion became a couple.

You cannot use their finding to calculate the odds that your friend will eventually fall in love with you.

Most importantly, friendship is not a waiting period that earns you romance.

If someone has told you they want friendship, continuing to be kind to them doesn’t create an obligation for their feelings to change.

Friendship can sometimes lead to romance. It can also remain friendship, and that is a complete relationship in its own right.

Three ways people get confused

1. You think someone is interested, but they’re being a friend

Return to Malik and Nadia.

Malik has collected what he considers evidence: frequent messages, shared jokes, long conversations, and Nadia remembering personal details.

None of it is imaginary. Nadia really does enjoy his company.

The mistake would be treating that enjoyment as proof that she wants to date him.

One study of friendships between men and women found that attraction could exist unevenly between friends. One person might experience feelings the other didn’t share. Some participants described attraction as a complication rather than a benefit of friendship (Bleske-Rechek et al., 2012).

That study was limited to friendships between men and women, but the basic possibility is easy to understand: two people can value the same connection without wanting the same future.

The useful question for Malik isn’t, “How many signs can I collect?”

It’s, “Would I like to explore dating Nadia, and is there an appropriate way to ask?”

He doesn’t have to apologize for developing feelings. He does have to recognize that his interpretation isn’t Nadia’s answer.

Perspective: Women

If people have mistaken your friendliness for romantic or sexual interest, you may have felt that ordinary kindness was being treated as an invitation.

Some research has documented this pattern among men interpreting women’s behavior, although it does not describe every interaction or every gender (Abbey, 1982; Moran et al., 2024).

You don’t need to become cold, unfriendly, or less generous to prevent other people from making assumptions. You can say that you meant something as friendship. Your friendliness was not a promise.

2. Someone is interested, but you don’t notice

Dev, 34, enjoys spending time with Luis, a man he met through a hiking group.

Luis begins to wonder whether they could date. He invites Dev to dinner, sometimes compliments his appearance, and makes an effort to spend time with him individually.

Dev likes Luis, but he interprets everything as friendship. He doesn’t realize Luis might mean something different.

Luis becomes discouraged. He believes he has been making his feelings obvious.

To Dev, he hasn’t.

Neither person necessarily knows what the other is thinking.

Research involving speed-meeting participants has found misreadings in both directions, including people underestimating someone else’s sexual interest. But those findings don’t establish a universal pattern or tell you which specific hints someone will miss (Perilloux et al., 2012).

Dev might be someone who takes invitations at face value. Luis might communicate interest indirectly because he worries about changing their friendship.

If Luis wants Dev to know he means a date, the most useful change is to put the meaning into words.

“You’ve probably been interpreting our dinners as friendly. I have been wondering whether we might enjoy going on a date. Would you be interested?”

Dev can then answer the actual question instead of being expected to reconstruct weeks of hidden intentions.

If Dev says no, Luis has an answer. If Dev says yes, both know what they’re agreeing to. If Dev needs time to think, Luis can respect that without treating it as a delayed yes.

Not noticing romantic interest is not a moral failure. Neither is declining it after somebody finally explains.

If you understand the task but not the invitation

You might be excellent at recognizing what someone wants in a situation with clear rules. At work, a request to finish a report by Friday has an obvious meaning. But “We should spend more time together” could mean friendship, romance, or simply an enjoyable conversation.

This difference doesn’t establish a diagnosis. It is an experience some people have, including people who prefer literal, explicit communication.

You can ask, “Did you mean as friends or as a date?” You are not required to decipher a hidden message before you’re allowed to respond.

3. You want romance, but the other person wants friendship

You might tell a friend that you’re interested in dating and hear:

“You’re important to me, but I only see us as friends.”

That can hurt, especially if you’ve imagined what a relationship together might be like.

You may be tempted to ask why.

Why did they spend so much time with you? Why did they tell you private things? Why were they affectionate? Why did they say you were one of their favorite people?

The answer might be simple: they meant those things as friendship.

There doesn’t have to be a hidden reason that turns their friendship into something else.

The phrase friend zone is often used to describe wanting romance from someone who sees you as a friend. But it can make the situation sound as though someone has placed you in an unfair category or denied you something you earned.

A clearer description is: your romantic feelings aren’t mutual.

That doesn’t mean you aren’t attractive, lovable, or worth dating. It means this person doesn’t want the same kind of relationship.

It also doesn’t mean that kindness was wasted.

If you only valued the friendship because you expected romance in return, that is something to examine honestly. If you genuinely valued the friendship and later developed feelings, that is different.

You can want more and still respect the other person’s answer.

What if friendship and romance aren’t separate boxes?

For some people, the distinction between friend and romantic partner feels obvious.

For others, it doesn’t.

A relationship might contain affection, closeness, commitment, or shared routines without fitting someone else’s expectations about romance.

Some people experience romantic attraction without sexual attraction. Others prefer relationships that don’t use conventional romantic labels. Two people may agree on a form of closeness that outsiders find difficult to categorize.

In a qualitative study involving 16 autistic adults in the United Kingdom who were members of gender and/or sexual minorities, participants described relationships that did not always fit neatly into separate friendship and romance categories. Their accounts included the importance of mutually understood definitions and choosing relationship terms that fit their own experiences (Ciric et al., 2026).

This was a small interview study, not a description of every autistic or LGBTQ person’s experience.

Its practical lesson is useful more broadly: you and another person don’t necessarily have to use the same relationship categories that movies, friends, or family expect.

You do need to understand what each other means.

For example, if you call someone your partner, do you both understand that word the same way? Does one of you expect exclusivity while the other doesn’t? Do you both know what kinds of affection and commitment are welcome?

Those questions become especially important as a relationship develops. For now, the starting point is simpler: you don’t have to guess which label another person wants.

You can talk about it.

Why the setting changes what friendliness means

Imagine three interactions:

A classmate spends an hour helping you understand an assignment.

A barista remembers your usual drink and asks how your weekend went.

A friend invites you to an activity you both enjoy.

Each person is being friendly. But the setting helps explain why.

Your classmate might enjoy collaborating. The barista is providing a service and may also be naturally sociable. Your friend may simply want your company.

An older study comparing working and student samples found differences in how participants interpreted motivations behind flirtatious behavior. People in the worker sample were less likely than students to attribute sexual or relationship-seeking motives to such behavior (Henningsen et al., 2008).

That doesn’t mean everyone correctly understands social situations at work. It means the surrounding circumstances can affect what people think a behavior means.

This is especially relevant if you’re comfortable understanding conversations with a clear purpose but uncertain about conversations where the purpose is unspoken.

A supervisor discussing your performance is doing something recognizable. A friendly acquaintance asking whether you’re free on Saturday leaves more possibilities open.

You don’t need to turn either situation into a complicated investigation.

Start with the ordinary explanation supported by the setting.

And remember that politeness from someone performing a job is not evidence of romantic interest. Workplaces can also involve authority, employment consequences, and rules about unwanted conduct (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, n.d.).

Chapter 43 covers the additional care needed when deciding whether and where a romantic invitation is appropriate.

When different ways of communicating meet

If you prefer direct language, a person who expects you to understand hints may find you confusing.

If you express affection through enthusiasm, long conversations, or sharing detailed information, someone else might interpret that as romantic attention when you mean friendship.

And if you become quiet around someone you like, they may interpret your quietness as disinterest.

These are possibilities, not reliable diagnostic signs.

In one study involving 67 autistic and 58 non-autistic adults, participants had short conversations with autistic or non-autistic partners. Their experiences and social impressions depended partly on who they were paired with. Autistic participants disclosed more to autistic conversation partners, while evidence of some other preferences was less conclusive (Morrison et al., 2020).

The study wasn’t about dating. It does support looking at interaction as something that happens between people, rather than attributing every misunderstanding to one person’s shortcomings.

That connects to the double empathy problem introduced earlier in the book.

You can be sincerely communicating one thing while another person sincerely understands something different.

Masking can complicate this further. A systematic review of eight qualitative studies found that autistic adolescents and adults sometimes described masking in their attempts to form friendships, frequently reporting exhaustion or a sense of inauthenticity. The review found little direct evidence about romantic outcomes (Ridgway et al., 2025).

So copying what you think romantic behavior is supposed to look like isn’t necessarily the answer.

Clearer mutual understanding is a better goal than performing a different personality.

If you're interested in someone who communicates differently

If someone enjoys talking to you but doesn’t respond to indirect romantic hints, don’t assume they’re deliberately ignoring you. They may understand your behavior as friendly, or they may simply not share your interest. You cannot tell which from their communication style alone.

When it’s appropriate, explain your interest clearly and leave room for a genuine answer. You can also ask what they meant by an invitation rather than assigning your own meaning to it.

Directness is something both people can contribute. Your way of communicating isn’t automatically the correct one, and neither is theirs.

The same general approach can help if you experience intense attention, uncertainty, or difficulty recognizing feelings. Those experiences can occur with ADHD, trauma histories, and without any diagnosis. The research available for this chapter doesn’t establish that those groups have a particular pattern of confusing friendship with romance.

There is no need to assign yourself a label to benefit from asking a clear question.

Deciding whether you actually want to ask

Before trying to find out whether your friend likes you romantically, consider a more basic question.

Do you want to date this person?

That may sound obvious. But wanting to know whether someone finds you attractive isn’t necessarily the same as wanting to build a romantic relationship with them.

You might be curious. You might enjoy their attention. You might be lonely. You might feel competitive after discovering they are interested in someone else.

Or you might genuinely want to explore dating because you enjoy their company, feel attracted to them, and see the possibility of a relationship.

You don’t need to be certain that a relationship would work. Dating is partly how people find that out.

But it helps to identify what you want before making another person responsible for answering it.

Consider these questions:

  • Would you still want to date this person if nobody else knew about it?
  • Are you interested in who they actually are, including their needs and limitations, rather than only how their attention makes you feel?
  • If they said they wanted friendship only, could you respect that answer without trying to change their mind?

The last question is particularly important.

You can be disappointed. You can need distance. You don’t have to promise that you will feel exactly the same afterward.

But you do need to be willing to accept another person’s choice.

If you already know they aren’t interested, the question has been answered. Don’t keep asking in different ways.

If the circumstances make a free answer difficult, such as a workplace power difference, don’t use the friendship as an excuse to create pressure.

Sometimes the appropriate action is to leave the question alone.

Asking without turning it into a test

Suppose you’ve considered your feelings, the other person’s boundaries, and the circumstances. You haven’t already received a no, and it’s appropriate to bring up the possibility of dating.

You don’t need a speech.

You don’t need to present a collection of evidence proving they must like you.

And you don’t need to ask mutual friends to investigate their feelings.

A simple invitation gives the other person a question they can actually answer.

“I’ve enjoyed getting to know you, and I’ve started feeling interested in you romantically. Would you want to go on a date sometime?”

You could add:

“If you’d rather stay friends, I understand.”

Only say the second part if you genuinely mean it. You don’t have to guarantee that you’ll never feel awkward or disappointed, but you shouldn’t offer a choice that you intend to argue against.

If you’re trying to clarify an invitation they made, the question can be even shorter:

“When you suggested dinner, did you mean as friends or as a date? I wasn’t sure.”

If you don’t want a date yourself, say that too:

“I’d enjoy dinner as friends, but I’m not looking for anything romantic.”

The purpose of these sentences isn’t to produce a particular answer.

It is to replace a misunderstanding with information.

Make room for an answer you don’t want

A person might find it difficult to decline someone who seems kind, vulnerable, or emotionally invested.

In two experimental studies, researchers found that participants considering a seemingly real dating opportunity were less willing to reject a poorly matched potential partner than participants considering a hypothetical situation. Concern for the other person’s feelings helped explain the difference (Joel et al., 2014).

This wasn’t research specifically about asking established friends on dates. But it offers a reason to avoid putting someone in a position where they feel responsible for protecting your feelings.

Don’t demand an immediate answer. Don’t ask in front of an audience that makes refusal embarrassing. Don’t explain how devastated you’ll be if they decline.

If someone says they aren’t sure, that isn’t a yes.

You can say, “That’s fine. You don’t have to decide now,” and then let them decide whether to reopen the topic.

Avoid treating uncertainty as permission to keep asking.

There is an important distinction between making an invitation and trying to obtain agreement.

An invitation offers a choice.

Trying to obtain agreement makes the person’s original answer seem insufficient.

For this chapter, the principle is simple: a clear, appropriate invitation is enough. The next chapters discuss flirting, choosing appropriate settings, and handling rejection in greater detail.

What if you don’t know your own feelings yet?

Sometimes the confusion isn’t about what another person wants.

It’s about what you want.

Maybe you have a close friend and occasionally imagine dating them, but other days the idea doesn’t appeal to you.

Maybe you enjoy their affection but don’t know whether you experience romantic attraction.

Maybe you’ve been told that closeness must mean romance, even though friendship feels like the right description.

You are allowed to be uncertain.

You don’t have to make a romantic declaration just because the possibility crosses your mind.

If someone asks how you feel, you can answer without inventing certainty:

“I care about you, but I don’t know whether what I’m feeling is romantic.”

Or:

“I enjoy our friendship, and I don’t want to promise something I’m not sure I want.”

Those answers don’t create an obligation for the other person to wait while you decide.

They are entitled to make their own choices, just as you are.

If you’re uncertain, don’t begin treating the person as a romantic partner while leaving them unaware that you’re still deciding what the connection means. Clarify what each of you is comfortable with.

Not every close connection needs to become dating.

And not every friendship needs a permanent label.

When you and a friend are afraid to talk about it

Sometimes both people suspect that romantic feelings might exist, but neither wants to raise the question.

Why?

Because the friendship matters.

You might worry that asking will make future conversations uncomfortable. You might fear losing someone who has become important to your daily life. You might not want the other person to feel watched or evaluated.

Research on uncertainty and topic avoidance in friendships between men and women has documented that people sometimes avoid discussing matters whose answers could be uncomfortable or unwelcome (Afifi & Burgoon, 1998).

That study does not establish whether asking is always better than remaining uncertain.

There is no universal answer.

A friendship can be worth preserving without testing every possible romantic feeling. You can decide that your interest isn’t strong enough to warrant changing the relationship.

On the other hand, if uncertainty is consuming your attention, a respectful conversation may be preferable to repeatedly interpreting small interactions.

The important distinction is between choosing not to ask and building an elaborate story without asking.

Choosing not to ask can be a thoughtful decision.

Assuming you know how someone feels because they remembered your birthday, replied with a heart emoji, or stayed late to talk is something else.

You cannot privately decide that a romantic relationship is developing when the other person hasn’t agreed that one exists.

If you guessed wrong

Suppose you tell a friend you’re interested, and they explain that they don’t feel the same way.

You may feel embarrassed. They may feel surprised, uncomfortable, or sad that you interpreted the friendship differently.

Or they may simply appreciate knowing what you meant.

In research involving people whose friendliness was mistaken for sexual interest, participants described reactions that included awkwardness, embarrassment, and indifference (Moran et al., 2024).

Those responses vary. A misunderstanding doesn’t produce one inevitable outcome.

You can acknowledge it without making the moment larger than necessary.

“I understand. I read things differently, and thanks for being clear.”

You don’t need to repeatedly apologize for having feelings.

You do need to stop treating the other person’s friendliness as evidence that they might secretly change their mind.

Sometimes a friendship continues with little disruption. Sometimes one or both people need space. Sometimes the friendship changes or ends.

There is no guarantee, and promising that everything will remain exactly the same would be unfair to both people.

The detailed work of dealing with rejection, disappointment, and friendship afterward belongs in Chapter 44.

For now, remember that misunderstanding a connection is different from refusing to respect the truth once it has been explained.

A mistaken interpretation is something you can correct.

Disregarding a clear answer is a choice you need to change.

A three-question reality check

When you find yourself wondering whether a friend likes you romantically, take a sheet of paper or open a note on your phone.

Write down three things.

1. What do I actually know?

Include only what the person has said or done, without interpreting what it means.

For example:

“Nadia invited me to a movie.”

“She messages me regularly.”

“She said she enjoys spending time with me.”

2. What am I assuming?

Write down the meanings you’ve added.

“She must want to date me.”

“She wouldn’t invite me if she didn’t find me attractive.”

“She probably knows that I like her.”

These might be true, but they aren’t facts.

3. What respectful choice can I make?

You have several possibilities. You can continue enjoying the friendship without turning it into romance. You can decide whether dating is something you genuinely want. If the circumstances are appropriate, you can ask once and accept the answer.

You can also recognize that the person has already answered and stop looking for reasons to reinterpret it.

You don’t need to solve every uncertainty. You need to distinguish what you know from what you’re imagining.

That distinction is useful whether you are 19, 39, or 69.

Two people, one friendship

Return to Nadia and Malik.

Malik has decided that he would genuinely like to date Nadia. Not because she’s kind to him, or because he wants proof that someone finds him attractive, but because he enjoys who she is and wants to explore a different kind of connection.

When they’re talking after a board-game night, he asks whether she’d be interested in a date.

Nadia is surprised.

She tells him that she cares about him but has only thought of them as friends.

Malik is disappointed. He thanks her for telling him and doesn’t argue.

Their next conversation may feel a little unusual. They may need to decide how much time together feels comfortable. Neither owes the other a particular outcome.

But at least they now understand the same thing.

In another version of the story, Nadia might say she’d been wondering about dating him too.

Or she might say she doesn’t know.

Those are three different answers, and all three must be allowed.

That is the central difference between friendship and romance when you’re unsure: you don’t discover mutual interest by assembling enough clues to make your preferred explanation seem certain. You discover it through mutual understanding.

A friend is not a failed romantic partner. A person who wants to date you is not automatically the right partner. And a friendship that becomes romantic isn’t more valuable than one that stays a friendship.

The connection is real even when its meaning isn’t what you hoped.

When feelings are mutual, you can begin exploring what comes next. When they aren’t, respecting that difference is part of treating each other well.

You don’t have to guess perfectly.

You have to leave room for the other person’s answer.
