Chapter 54: Conflict without harm

Two people can love each other, understand each other reasonably well, and still have a serious argument about a dishwasher.

One person thinks the dishes should be loaded immediately after dinner. The other prefers doing them before bed. Both consider their method obvious. By Thursday, the disagreement is no longer entirely about dishes.

Perhaps one person feels that the household work falls disproportionately on them. The other feels watched and corrected whenever they enter the kitchen. One wants the problem addressed immediately. The other needs time to think before answering.

Neither person has to be unreasonable for the disagreement to be real.

But what happens next matters.

A relationship doesn’t require you to agree about everything. It requires you to handle disagreement without making the other person afraid, humiliating them, or treating their needs as less important than your own.

That includes knowing when to speak, when to stop, and what to do when your brain or body can no longer manage the conversation.

Disagreement is not failure

Some people grew up in homes where arguments were frightening. Others grew up where nobody discussed anything difficult. A disagreement in an adult relationship may therefore feel unfamiliar, dangerous, or like proof that the relationship is failing.

But two people can care about each other and still want different things.

You might disagree about how much time to spend together, what counts as being on time, whether plans can change at the last minute, or how much quiet time you need after work. Chapter 53 discussed negotiating those everyday differences. Sometimes the negotiation goes smoothly. Sometimes it doesn’t.

A disagreement is information about something that needs attention. It is not automatically evidence that you chose the wrong partner.

John Gottman and colleagues studied how newlywed couples interacted during conflict. Their research found relationships between some communication patterns and later marital outcomes, but did not support the simple idea that anger itself is destructive (Gottman et al., 1998).

Being angry is not the same as being harmful.

Anger can tell you that something matters to you. You still have responsibility for what you do with it.

You can say, “I’m angry that you made this decision without talking to me.”

You cannot use that anger as permission to threaten, intimidate, or frighten your partner.

Likewise, your partner can be disappointed by a decision you made without treating you badly.

A useful distinction is between having a conflict and making the conflict unsafe. The National Domestic Violence Hotline’s guidance on fair disagreements is explicitly intended for relationships where both people can disagree safely. It is not a method for resolving violence or abuse (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

The goal isn’t a relationship without conflict. It’s a relationship where disagreement does not cost either person their dignity or safety.

A fair argument is not a competition

The phrase fair fight is common in relationship advice, but it can be misleading. The purpose of a disagreement is not to defeat your partner.

If you win an argument by exhausting, frightening, or embarrassing the other person, you haven’t necessarily solved the problem. You may simply have ended the conversation.

Imagine that Elena and Grace have been together for eighteen months.

Elena enjoys hosting friends. Grace needs quiet after working with people all day. They agreed to discuss invitations before making plans, but Elena invited four people over on Friday without checking.

Grace is upset.

“You never think about what I need,” she says.

Elena replies, “You always make everything difficult.”

Within seconds, both are defending themselves. The original problem has almost disappeared.

Now imagine a different beginning.

Grace says, “When I found out people were coming Friday, I felt stressed. We had agreed to check first, and I need a quiet evening.”

Elena answers, “I forgot about our agreement. I wanted to see everyone, but I should have checked. Can we work out what to do about Friday?”

They still disagree. Elena wants the gathering. Grace wants quiet. The difference is that they are discussing the problem rather than attacking each other.

Describe what happened before deciding what it says about the person.

“You’re inconsiderate” is a judgment about character.

“You invited people without checking with me” identifies an action that can be discussed.

This distinction is central to the Gottman Institute’s communication guidance (The Gottman Institute, 2026).

It is also worth checking what each person thinks the problem is. Elena might see Friday as an isolated scheduling mistake. Grace might see it as part of a repeated pattern of agreements being forgotten.

Those are different concerns. Solving one may not solve the other.

Being fair means giving both people room to explain their experience, without requiring either to pretend their needs don’t exist.

It does not mean splitting responsibility equally when responsibility isn’t equal.

If you broke an agreement, you can acknowledge that. If your partner did, they can acknowledge it. Being willing to understand the other person’s perspective doesn’t require you to accept an inaccurate account of what happened.

Four patterns worth recognizing

The Gottman Institute describes four communication patterns it calls the Four Horsemen:

  • Criticism: attacking someone’s character instead of describing a specific concern.
  • Contempt: communicating disgust, mockery, or superiority toward the other person.
  • Defensiveness: responding to a concern primarily by denying responsibility or redirecting blame.
  • Stonewalling: disengaging from an interaction rather than participating in its resolution.

The Institute recommends more specific complaints, respect, responsibility, and breaks when someone is too distressed to continue (The Gottman Institute, 2026).

These patterns can be useful things to notice in your own behavior. They are not a personality test for deciding whether your partner is good or bad.

They are not infallible predictions about whether your relationship will last, either.

Gottman’s original newlywed research reported that some observed patterns predicted later marital outcomes (Gottman et al., 1998). A subsequent study by Gottman and Robert Levenson linked different interaction patterns with earlier and later divorce during a fourteen-year follow-up (Gottman & Levenson, 2000).

But prediction is not the same as certainty, and the findings have not replicated consistently.

In a study of 85 married or cohabiting couples, Kim, Capaldi, and Crosby attempted to test important predictions from the earlier Gottman work. The major predictions they examined did not replicate (Kim et al., 2007).

The practical lesson is modest: character attacks, mockery, and reflexive blame can interfere with a useful conversation. You don’t need a divorce-prediction formula to recognize that.

There is also an important complication with stonewalling.

Someone who deliberately refuses to engage may look similar to someone who is overwhelmed, needs time to process, or has temporarily lost access to speech.

The silence alone doesn’t tell you what is happening.

That distinction becomes especially important when one or both partners experience overload or shutdown.

Sometimes the problem needs a direct answer

Being considerate doesn’t mean avoiding difficult subjects.

Suppose your partner has repeatedly spent money from a shared account without discussing it. You have raised the issue gently several times. Nothing has changed.

Another carefully worded hint may not be useful.

You can be direct:

“This keeps affecting our bills. I need us to make a clear agreement about shared spending. I can’t keep using this account the same way without one.”

That is different from insulting your partner or demanding obedience.

Research also complicates the idea that gentle, agreeable behavior is always better in every situation.

In two longitudinal studies of newlywed couples, McNulty and Russell found that the relationship between more directly negative problem-solving behavior and later satisfaction depended partly on the severity of the problem. Avoiding confrontation was not always associated with better outcomes, particularly when problems were serious (McNulty & Russell, 2010).

This finding does not justify being cruel. It suggests that an important problem sometimes requires a difficult conversation rather than an appearance of harmony.

Another study, using two longitudinal samples of different-gender couples, found that the relationship between one specific pattern of demanding change and withdrawing from discussion varied with socioeconomic circumstances. Withdrawal was associated with different outcomes when couples faced greater economic disadvantage (Ross et al., 2019).

That does not establish that withdrawal is a good solution. It shows why context matters.

A couple choosing between two vacation destinations faces a different problem from a couple struggling to pay rent. Some problems can be settled by preference. Others involve constraints that no amount of agreeable conversation can immediately remove.

Fair conflict means taking the problem seriously without treating your partner as the problem.

When you can no longer think clearly

You are trying to explain something important.

Your partner interrupts to clarify a detail. You lose your place. They ask another question. You hear frustration in their voice.

Now there are too many things to track.

Your heart may be racing. Your thoughts may be moving too quickly or disappearing entirely. Perhaps you feel an immediate urge to keep talking until everything is settled. Perhaps you need the conversation to stop but cannot work out how to say so.

This is a poor moment to insist on resolving a complicated disagreement.

The Gottman Institute describes the effects of high physiological arousal during arguments and recommends taking roughly twenty minutes to calm down before continuing (The Gottman Institute, 2013).

That duration is practitioner guidance, not a universal timer. Some people need less time; others need considerably more. There is no established twenty-minute rule specifically validated for autistic or ADHD couples.

The useful principle is simpler:

If you cannot listen, think, or respond safely, pause the discussion rather than forcing it forward.

You don’t have to wait until you are shouting.

You might notice that you’re repeating yourself, missing parts of sentences, struggling to find words, or becoming so upset that you want to say something hurtful.

Those are reasonable moments to stop.

You can tell your partner:

“I’m having trouble processing this. I want to talk about it, but I need a break.”

Or:

“I’m getting too angry to speak respectfully. I’m stopping now, and I’ll check back at seven.”

If speaking is difficult, you might use a previously agreed text message or signal.

Stopping before you hurt someone is a responsible choice. It is not an admission that your concern doesn’t matter.

A break needs to be understandable

A pause can help both people. But disappearing halfway through an argument without explanation may leave the other person confused about whether the conversation will ever resume.

Where both people feel safe, a useful arrangement is to communicate three things: that you need to stop, whether you intend to return to the discussion, and when you expect to check in.

For example:

“I need quiet. I can try again after dinner.”

Or:

“I can’t talk now. I’ll message you tomorrow morning to arrange a time.”

A check-in time is not necessarily a promise that the whole argument will be resolved then. You may discover that you need more time.

If that happens, communicate it when you can:

“I’m still not ready. I haven’t forgotten about this. Can we talk tomorrow evening?”

A partner may feel disappointed about waiting. That feeling matters too.

But feeling disappointed does not give someone permission to demand that you continue talking when you cannot do so safely.

At the same time, needing space doesn’t mean every important discussion can be postponed indefinitely.

If the pattern is always “not now,” with no later conversation, the underlying problem remains. When it is safe to do so, both people need a workable way to return.

It may help to agree on the process during a calm moment rather than inventing it during an argument.

Some couples use a spoken phrase. Others use a text, a written note, or a simple signal. The best option is one both people understand and can actually use.

A pause is not permission to punish

There is a difference between taking space to recover and using silence to make another person feel powerless.

But you cannot reliably determine someone’s intention from quiet behavior alone.

A person who suddenly stops speaking may be frightened, overloaded, exhausted, or unable to communicate. Someone else may be deliberately withdrawing contact in a harmful pattern.

Instead of deciding what a particular silence proves, pay attention to the wider circumstances.

Can both people take a break without being punished for it? Does the person who needed space make a reasonable effort to communicate afterward, when safe and able? Is silence repeatedly connected to demands for obedience or fear of consequences?

You can discuss a confusing pattern later, when both people can participate. You do not have to diagnose the reason in the moment.

And none of this requires someone to return to a frightening or abusive situation. A person leaving for safety does not owe the other person a promised return time.

When overload becomes a meltdown or shutdown

Sometimes an argument doesn’t merely make someone upset. It pushes them beyond what they can process.

Overload means there is more coming in than a person can manage at once. It might involve emotion, noise, questions, competing information, bright lights, physical discomfort, or several of these together.

A meltdown can involve intense distress and a temporary loss of the person’s usual ability to regulate their response. A shutdown is when the brain and body pull inward and slow down or stop, often after overload; speech may become very difficult.

These experiences are not identical, and neither happens to every autistic person.

In interviews with 32 autistic adults, Lewis and Stevens found that participants described meltdowns involving intense emotions, difficulty thinking or remembering, and attempts to regain control. Some deliberately sought solitude when a meltdown was approaching to protect themselves and their relationships (Lewis & Stevens, 2023).

In another qualitative study, 15 autistic adults described how sensory conditions, missed internal cues, misunderstandings, and feeling powerless could contribute to difficulty regulating emotions. Their experiences included shutdown and reduced access to speech or movement (Beck et al., 2026).

A scoping review of 18 studies also found that autistic distress can involve varied forms of spoken and nonspoken communication. The authors discussed environmental adjustments and alternative communication supports, although this research did not establish a proven approach to arguments between romantic partners (Oldenburg et al., 2026).

A smaller study involving eight autistic young people explored meltdowns and shutdowns as distinct but sometimes overlapping experiences. Its findings help describe the experiences, but cannot establish what works for adult couples (Phung et al., 2021).

What does this mean during a relationship disagreement?

Imagine that Niko and Jules are both autistic. Niko usually becomes more verbal under stress, explaining every detail in an attempt to resolve uncertainty. Jules processes information more slowly during overload and sometimes loses access to speech.

One evening, Niko wants to settle a disagreement about holiday plans. Jules has already had a difficult day in a noisy workplace.

Niko asks a question. Jules answers briefly.

Niko asks another, then explains why the answer seems inconsistent.

Jules becomes silent.

Niko, thinking the explanation hasn’t been understood, starts again from the beginning.

Neither is deliberately trying to hurt the other. Yet the interaction is becoming harder for both.

The solution in that moment is not for Niko to explain more clearly or for Jules to force an answer.

It is to stop adding demands.

They may later agree that, when speech becomes difficult, Jules can signal a need for quiet. Niko can acknowledge the signal and leave room for recovery. Once Jules is ready, they can discuss the holiday plans in writing if that works better.

The agreement doesn’t depend on both people having the same experience of overload. It depends on respecting what each person needs.

It also doesn’t require Niko to abandon the original concern forever.

A need for recovery and a need to resolve an important problem can both be legitimate. They may simply need to happen at different times.

If you're dating someone who suddenly can't answer

If your partner becomes unable to speak, answer questions, or follow what you’re saying during distress, consider whether they need less input rather than more explanation. Stop adding questions. Offer quiet, physical space, or a way to communicate later, according to what they’ve said helps them. Avoid assuming that silence means they don’t care.

When things are calm, ask how they would like you to respond if it happens again. Don’t insist on talking, touching, or comforting them in a particular way. Respect their preferences and your own safety. Someone’s distress deserves care, but it does not require you to tolerate threats, violence, or unwanted contact.

Support is not the same as accepting harm

A meltdown can explain why someone had difficulty controlling or communicating their distress. It does not erase the consequences of harmful behavior.

If your partner becomes distressed and needs to be alone, you can respect that.

If your partner frightens you, threatens you, becomes physically aggressive, or prevents you from leaving, your safety matters regardless of the explanation.

The same applies when you are the person experiencing overload.

You may not be able to choose exactly when your speech becomes difficult or how intensely you feel distress. You can still take responsibility for learning what helps you recognize an approaching crisis, creating safer conditions where possible, and addressing any harm after you recover.

That might mean asking for more breaks, reducing sensory demands, using written communication, or seeking professional support.

It never means expecting another person to remain somewhere unsafe.

When your emotions move faster than your words

For some people, a small disagreement can produce an unexpectedly intense emotional response.

You may know that your partner didn’t intend harm, yet feel furious, devastated, or desperate to resolve the situation immediately.

You may understand the reasonable response later but struggle to reach it in the moment.

ADHD can be one possible contributor to this experience.

A meta-analysis of thirteen studies involving 2,535 adults found substantially greater difficulties with emotion regulation among adults diagnosed with ADHD than among comparison participants (Beheshti et al., 2020).

That is a group-level finding. It doesn’t mean every person with ADHD becomes intensely upset during arguments, and it certainly doesn’t mean ADHD predicts abusive behavior.

It does mean that advice such as “just calm down” may be inadequate for someone who is struggling to regulate an intense emotion.

Instead of depending on willpower at the most difficult moment, you can make decisions beforehand.

For example, you might agree not to settle important disputes through rapid-fire messages, take space when you feel the urge to keep arguing, or write down the actual question you want answered before returning to the conversation.

You can also tell your partner what helps without making them responsible for your response:

“I sometimes need extra time before I can explain what I’m feeling. If I ask for a break, it means I need time to respond thoughtfully.”

If you speak sharply or hurt someone, you still need to address that afterward. An explanation helps another person understand you; it doesn’t cancel the impact of your actions.

Past frightening experiences may also affect how people respond to conflict.

A meta-analysis of 31 studies found associations between posttraumatic stress symptoms and difficulties in intimate relationships, including relationship discord and aggression-related measures. Much of that research involved military or veteran populations, and the findings were correlational (Taft et al., 2011).

This cannot tell you why a particular person becomes quiet, afraid, or intensely upset during an argument.

You do not need to decide whether your response comes from ADHD, autism, trauma, several of these, or none of them before asking for a safer way to communicate.

The practical starting point is what happens to you and what you need.

Chapter 57 examines how past experiences can affect closeness and conflict in greater depth.

The conversation after the conversation

A disagreement may end when someone leaves the room or the immediate problem is settled.

That does not necessarily mean the relationship has recovered.

Perhaps you have agreed to handle Friday’s plans differently, but your partner is still hurt by something you said.

Perhaps you apologized, but the same problem has happened five times.

This is where repair matters.

Chapter 24 introduced the basics of acknowledging and repairing misunderstandings. In a relationship, repair also means paying attention to what happens after the apology.

Suppose Marcus and Daniel argue about arriving late to a family dinner.

Daniel says that Marcus’s lateness made him feel unimportant. Marcus becomes defensive and snaps that Daniel is impossible to please.

Later, Marcus realizes he was responding to the feeling of being criticized rather than the actual concern.

He might say:

“I was frustrated, and I responded by insulting you. That was unfair. I’m sorry.”

Then he can address the original problem:

“I’ve been underestimating how long it takes me to get ready. I’m going to set an earlier reminder and start preparing before the departure time.”

Daniel doesn’t have to declare everything fine.

He might answer, “Thank you for acknowledging that. I still need some time, and I want to see whether the new plan works.”

That is a reasonable response.

An apology is not a purchase that obligates someone to forgive, feel safe, or move on immediately.

Likewise, an improvement plan matters only if it becomes a real change.

There are three useful questions to ask once both people are ready:

  1. What happened? Describe the event and each person’s experience without rewriting it to make yourself look better.
  2. What needs attention? Acknowledge harmful conduct separately from the original disagreement.
  3. What will be different? Agree on something observable, or identify the decision you still need to make.

These are practical prompts, not a scientifically validated three-step treatment.

You don’t have to settle every disagreement in one sitting. Sometimes the next honest step is admitting that you haven’t found a solution.

A couple might discover that they need different amounts of social time. They may need an arrangement that protects both people’s needs rather than trying to persuade one person to adopt the other’s preference.

Other disagreements may reveal a genuine incompatibility. Not every problem has a compromise that both people can accept.

Conflict skills can help you understand a disagreement. They cannot guarantee that every relationship should continue.

Don’t confuse an explanation with a repair

There is a difference between understanding why something happened and changing what happens next.

“I was overloaded” may be an accurate explanation for why you could not answer.

“I was frightened of being abandoned” may help explain why an argument felt so urgent.

“I struggle to regulate emotions” may explain why a reaction became intense.

These explanations can be important. They can make future conversations more informed and compassionate.

But if someone was hurt, frightened, or repeatedly ignored, the consequences still need attention.

A useful response recognizes both realities:

“This was difficult for me, and I recognize that it affected you.”

Neither person’s experience has to erase the other’s.

When this isn’t an ordinary argument

Not every conflict belongs in a discussion about communication techniques.

If someone threatens you, frightens you into compliance, controls where you can go, prevents you from leaving, pressures you sexually, or becomes physically violent, the problem is not simply that the two of you argue differently.

The CDC identifies intimate partner violence as including physical violence, sexual violence, stalking, and psychological aggression intended to harm or control someone. These behaviors can occur in dating relationships as well as marriages (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026).

The National Domestic Violence Hotline makes a critical distinction between respectful disagreements and abuse. Advice about fair arguments is not a substitute for safety planning when abuse is present (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

You do not need to become better at choosing your words to earn the right to be safe.

You also do not need to prove that your partner intended harm before taking your fear seriously.

If you are afraid of what may happen when you disagree, consider talking privately with a trusted person or a domestic violence support service. Chapters 59 and 60 explain where to find further help in the United States.

If you recognize yourself threatening, intimidating, restraining, controlling, or frightening a partner, stop that conduct. Respect their distance and their right not to engage with you. Take responsibility and seek specialized professional help. Do not make your partner responsible for teaching you how to stop harming them.

A diagnosis, emotional distress, difficult childhood, or fear of losing a relationship does not create an exception to another person’s safety or consent.

Make a plan before the next disagreement

You don’t have to wait for a serious argument to think about what makes disagreements difficult.

Choose a calm time and consider one ordinary disagreement you’ve had, or a situation you suspect might become difficult.

Maybe you become upset when plans change without warning. Maybe your partner struggles with conversations late at night. Maybe one person wants an immediate answer while the other needs an hour to think.

Talk about the conditions that make communication easier.

You could start with:

“I want us to be able to disagree without hurting each other. What makes an argument harder for you?”

Then consider the answer to the question yourself.

What are your early signs of overload? Do you need movement, silence, writing, time alone, or a slower conversation? What would make it easier to tell your partner you need a pause? How could they raise a concern without your feeling that you must resolve everything immediately?

You might agree on something as simple as this:

“Either person can ask to pause. If it’s safe to do so, we’ll check in about when to continue. We won’t demand an immediate answer when someone can’t process the conversation.”

That is not a contract requiring either person to endure an unsafe interaction. It is an agreement for handling ordinary disagreements with more care.

You can revise it as you learn more about each other.

If the first plan doesn’t work, that does not automatically mean either person failed. Perhaps the break wasn’t long enough. Perhaps writing would be easier than speaking. Perhaps the issue is more serious than you originally thought.

Talk about what happened when you’re able.

The point is not to perform perfect communication. It is to make a difficult experience a little less damaging the next time it occurs.

And if you repeatedly find that you cannot disagree safely, or that the same conflicts keep hurting both of you despite sincere efforts, you do not have to solve everything privately. Qualified outside support may help, although joint counseling is not a substitute for safety planning in an abusive relationship.

Two people can stand in different places, see the same situation differently, and both have something worth saying. The task is to understand those views without turning disagreement into a contest over whose experience counts.

A healthy relationship leaves room for anger, disappointment, mistakes, and difficult conversations.

It also leaves room to stop, recover, reconsider, and change.

You do not have to get every conversation right. You do have to care about what happens to the other person while you’re having it.
