Sam has just finished a first date with Jules. They spent an hour at a coffee shop, talked about their jobs, laughed about a terrible movie, and said goodbye outside.

Walking to the car, Sam starts reviewing everything that happened.

Did I talk too much? Was that joke stupid? Should I have offered to pay? Did Jules notice how nervous I was? What if I ruined my chance?

Sam remembers what Jules said, what Jules wore, and whether Jules smiled. Sam can practically reconstruct the entire conversation.

But one question hasn’t occurred to Sam:

Did I actually enjoy spending time with Jules?

Not whether Jules was attractive. Not whether Jules might agree to another date. Whether Sam felt comfortable, interested, respected, and curious enough to want another hour together.

That missing question changes the whole purpose of dating.

If dating is about convincing someone to choose you, every encounter becomes a performance. You try to say the right things, avoid mistakes, and earn a favorable decision. If the other person loses interest, you feel as though you failed.

But dating works better when you understand it as a process of finding out whether two people want something together.

You are not the only person being evaluated. You are also making a choice.

And neither person has to be wrong for the answer to be no.

Dating is not an audition

An audition is a situation where someone performs and another person decides whether the performance is good enough.

Dating can feel that way, especially when you desperately want a relationship or have little experience getting romantic attention.

You meet someone you find attractive. Suddenly, their opinion carries enormous weight. You want them to laugh at your jokes, appreciate your interests, and find your appearance appealing. You may spend so much energy trying to create a good impression that you stop noticing how they treat you.

Imagine that two people meet for dinner.

Taylor is interested in Morgan and wants another date. Morgan enjoys the conversation but doesn’t feel interested in continuing.

Taylor might conclude, I wasn’t good enough.

But Morgan hasn’t established anything about Taylor’s worth. Morgan has only decided that this particular connection isn’t one they want to pursue.

Taylor could be kind, interesting, emotionally available, and capable of being a wonderful partner. None of those qualities obligates Morgan to feel romantic interest.

The reverse is also true. Someone can be attractive, successful, funny, and genuinely decent without being someone you want to date.

Being a good person and being a good match for someone are different things.

Dating is not a competition to determine who deserves love. It is an encounter between people with different preferences, circumstances, histories, and hopes.

Both people can bring something valuable without wanting the same relationship.

Both people can do everything respectfully and still decide not to meet again.

This means you can stop treating every date as an opportunity to prove that you deserve to be chosen.

The useful question is not simply, How do I get this person to like me?

It is, Do we like each other, and would spending more time together be good for both of us?

What counts as dating?

At its simplest, dating means spending time with someone to explore whether you want a romantic relationship, a casual dating connection, or another kind of mutually desired involvement.

What that looks like depends on the people involved.

One person might be looking for a committed relationship. Another might want casual dates without a commitment. Two people might be getting to know each other slowly before deciding what kind of connection they want.

Chapter 7 covered the different relationships people might want. Dating is one way to discover whether another person’s wishes and yours can fit together.

A date doesn’t have to involve an expensive restaurant, physical affection, or elaborate planning. It might be coffee, a walk, a museum, a meal, or an afternoon doing something both people enjoy.

The important distinction is that both people understand that the meeting is a date.

If you ask a coworker to get lunch and they agree, that doesn’t automatically mean they’re romantically interested. They might believe they’re having lunch with a colleague.

If you’re unsure what kind of meeting someone has in mind, it’s better to clarify than to build expectations around a guess.

For example:

“Just so we’re on the same page, did you mean this as a date or as friends hanging out?”

That question might feel awkward. It also gives both people the chance to understand what they’re agreeing to.

Agreeing to one date doesn’t mean agreeing to physical affection, sex, exclusivity, a relationship, or another date.

Each of those is a separate choice.

And if someone doesn’t want a date, their answer is enough. You don’t need to discover the hidden reason or find a way to change their mind.

Not everyone who is single is looking

One common assumption is that single people must be searching for partners.

They aren’t.

In a Pew Research Center survey conducted in July 2022, 57% of single US adults said they were not currently looking for a committed relationship or casual dates. About 42% were looking for some form of dating or relationship connection (Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

That means being single does not automatically mean being romantically available.

Among those who were single and looking, people also wanted different things. About half were open to either committed relationships or casual dates. Others wanted only a committed relationship or only casual dating (Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

These are survey results, not rules about what any particular person wants. They also don’t capture every relationship structure or experience.

Still, they offer an important reality check.

Someone might be single because they’re enjoying their independence, recovering from a breakup, focusing on responsibilities, or simply uninterested in dating right now.

Someone else might enjoy meeting people but not want the kind of relationship you want.

Neither situation creates a problem for you to solve.

Suppose you’re interested in Alex, who says they aren’t looking for a relationship.

You might think:

Maybe Alex just hasn’t met the right person. Maybe spending time with me will change that.

It’s possible for people’s wishes to change over time. But you cannot build a healthy dating plan around the assumption that someone will eventually want something they’ve said they don’t want.

Take their current answer seriously.

Likewise, if someone wants a casual connection and you’re looking for a committed partnership, you don’t need to criticize their choice. You can recognize that your goals differ.

Finding out early that your wishes don’t align can prevent both people from investing in a connection that cannot meet their needs.

A mismatch in goals is useful information, even when it’s disappointing.

You cannot figure everything out before meeting

It would be convenient if finding a partner worked like buying a computer.

You could write down the specifications, compare the available options, and choose the one that meets your requirements.

Dating isn’t that predictable.

People can tell you useful things about themselves. They can describe their interests, values, daily habits, and relationship goals. You can decide whether those descriptions sound compatible with what you want.

But a profile or checklist cannot tell you everything about how two particular people will feel together.

In a 2017 study, researchers used information about personality, preferences, and other characteristics gathered before speed-dating events. The researchers could predict certain general tendencies, such as who was more likely to feel attraction or receive it. But they could not successfully predict which particular pairs would feel attracted to each other beyond those broad tendencies (Joel et al., 2017).

The study involved young US college students having brief, opposite-sex speed dates. It wasn’t a test of lasting relationships, and its results shouldn’t be treated as a universal law.

A larger international study published in 2025 found a more complicated picture. Across 43 countries, people tended to evaluate partners more positively when the partners matched some of their stated ideals. However, the distinctive effect of matching one person’s particular preferences was generally modest. The study did not test whether a dating app could successfully predict future relationships (Eastwick et al., 2025).

Taken together, these studies offer a sensible caution: what you think you want matters, but it doesn’t give you complete advance knowledge of whom you’ll connect with.

Consider two people who both enjoy science fiction, want children, and prefer quiet evenings at home.

Those similarities might give them something to talk about and suggest that some of their plans could fit together.

But they may discover that one prefers to discuss disagreements immediately while the other needs time before answering. Or they may enjoy exactly the same activities and feel little romantic interest.

Now consider two people with different hobbies who find that they listen carefully, respect each other’s preferences, and enjoy sharing ordinary time together.

A checklist might have favored the first pair. Meeting in person can reveal possibilities and problems that a checklist misses.

This doesn’t mean ignoring important differences.

If you know you don’t want children, you don’t need to date someone determined to have them just because chemistry might surprise you.

If someone’s behavior makes you feel unsafe or disrespected, you don’t need additional dates to test whether a better connection might eventually appear.

Use your preferences to guide your choices, not to replace the experience of getting to know someone.

The goal isn’t to abandon standards. It’s to understand which standards reflect genuine needs and which predictions require more information.

If you need the rules before you can play

Dating is unusually badly specified: no stated objective, no agreed signals, no point at which anyone tells you the result. That’s genuinely harder if you work from explicit rules rather than inference.

It helps to supply your own objective. “I’m finding out whether I enjoy this person’s company, and whether they enjoy mine” is concrete, and you can tell whether it happened.

The ambiguity isn’t a test you’re failing. Most people are also improvising; they’re just less bothered by not knowing.

What are you actually trying to find out?

A date doesn’t need to answer every question about your future together.

Especially on a first date, you are meeting someone who may still be unfamiliar to you. You have limited information, and both people may be nervous.

You are not deciding whether to spend the next fifty years together over one cup of coffee.

You are deciding whether you want to learn more.

Several kinds of information are especially useful.

Do I enjoy being around this person?

This sounds obvious, but it’s easy to overlook.

Someone might look exactly like your imagined partner. You might admire their accomplishments or feel excited that they agreed to meet you.

But while you’re together, do you actually enjoy the conversation?

Are you curious about their thoughts?

Do you feel interested in what they say, even when it isn’t about you?

Can you imagine enjoying ordinary time together, not just an exciting date?

You don’t have to feel instant certainty or constant excitement. A date can be pleasant without being spectacular.

You also don’t have to force yourself to continue merely because someone seems suitable on paper.

Can I be reasonably comfortable and myself?

First dates can involve nerves. A busy restaurant might create overload. You might need extra time to answer questions, struggle to follow conversation in a noisy room, or feel tired after socializing.

Those experiences don’t automatically tell you whether the person is a good match.

Try distinguishing the setting from the interaction.

Maybe the music was too loud, but your date happily moved to a quieter table.

Maybe you needed a moment to think, and your date gave you one.

Maybe your date was kind, but the activity was exhausting.

A different location or shorter meeting might reveal more.

On the other hand, if you feel that you must continuously hide your interests, ignore your needs, or pretend to be someone else to keep this person’s attention, that’s information too.

Earlier chapters discussed masking and energy. You don’t need to reveal every personal detail immediately. But dating someone should not require permanently performing a version of yourself that you cannot comfortably sustain.

How do we treat each other?

Notice what happens during ordinary moments.

If a plan changes, can you discuss another option?

If you disagree about something small, can both people speak respectfully?

If one person needs to leave earlier, does the other accept that?

These moments can tell you more about whether a connection feels workable.

Respect is not the same as agreeing about everything. Two people can have different preferences without making either person wrong.

A date might be enjoyable even when those differences become clear.

What matters is whether both people can make room for each other’s choices.

Do our lives and goals leave room for each other?

You already considered your own goals in Chapter 7.

Now you’re discovering what another person wants.

Someone might want a relationship but have little time available. Someone else might want frequent contact and shared activities.

Neither preference is automatically unreasonable.

You are trying to find out whether your needs and their needs could coexist without one person repeatedly giving up something important.

You don’t have to settle every question immediately. Some subjects require more trust and conversation.

But when a significant incompatibility becomes clear, you are allowed to believe it.

The answer has to be mutual

Imagine that you meet someone and think the date went wonderfully.

You laughed, shared interests, and felt unusually comfortable. You want another meeting.

The other person says they had a pleasant time but don’t want to continue.

It can feel confusing. You experienced the same hour. How could you reach different conclusions?

Because you didn’t experience it from the same perspective.

The sculpture-and-glasses image from the beginning of this book applies here. You’re both present at the same encounter, but you bring different preferences, experiences, and ways of interpreting what happened.

Neither person’s feelings cancel out the other’s.

You can genuinely have enjoyed the date.

They can genuinely have enjoyed parts of it without wanting another.

You do not need to prove that your interpretation is correct.

A relationship requires continuing agreement between the people involved. One person’s certainty cannot substitute for the other’s interest.

That also means you should not rely on friendliness as proof of attraction.

Someone laughing at your jokes, asking questions, or sharing personal information doesn’t automatically mean they want a romantic relationship.

People can be friendly without being interested in dating. They can enjoy one date without wanting a second.

If you’re unsure whether someone wants another meeting, ask clearly and give them room to answer.

If the answer is no, respect it.

If they stop responding, don’t keep sending invitations to obtain an explanation or change their mind.

And if you notice yourself repeatedly looking for ways to turn someone’s refusal into agreement, stop pursuing that person. You can work on how you handle disappointment without involving them further.

Dating only works as mutual discovery when both people remain free to choose.

Why so many conversations lead nowhere

Someone matches with you on a dating app but doesn’t respond.

Someone chats for a few days and then decides not to meet.

Someone enjoys a first date but doesn’t want a second.

Someone dates you several times before realizing the connection isn’t what they’re looking for.

These experiences can hurt. They also fit a process in which two people must independently want to continue.

Online dating research offers one window into how uncertain the early stages can be.

In a study of messaging on one dating website across four US cities in 2014, researchers found that replies were far from guaranteed. Reply rates varied, including with the difference between users’ positions in the site’s messaging network (Bruch & Newman, 2018).

That network measure was not a measurement of anyone’s human worth.

And the study doesn’t tell us what percentage of first dates become second dates or lasting relationships. The research available for this chapter doesn’t establish a reliable national percentage.

Still, it’s important to separate an interaction not progressing from a person failing.

Someone might not reply because they aren’t interested. They may be unavailable, uncertain, busy, or dealing with circumstances you know nothing about.

You usually cannot determine the reason from silence alone.

And you don’t need to.

You are allowed to feel disappointed without constructing a story about your defects or the other person’s character.

For example, consider the difference between these two conclusions:

Three people stopped replying to me. Nobody will ever want me.

Three conversations didn’t continue. That was disappointing, but it doesn’t tell me what every future person will think.

The second conclusion doesn’t promise success. It simply stays closer to what you actually know.

You can still examine your own behavior. If you repeatedly ignore other people’s preferences, arrive very late without explanation, or speak disrespectfully, those are choices worth changing.

But not every disappointing result is evidence that you did something wrong.

Sometimes two people simply don’t want the same connection.

Where you meet someone doesn’t decide whether they’re right for you

Dating apps are one possible place to meet people, but they aren’t the definition of dating.

A national Pew Research Center survey conducted in 2022 found that 30% of US adults had ever used an online dating site or app. Among adults ages 18 to 29, that figure was 53% (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

Online meeting has also become an important way relationships begin. One analysis of US couples found that meeting online had overtaken introductions through friends among heterosexual couples around 2013 (Rosenfeld et al., 2019).

But people also meet through friends, shared activities, communities, and other ordinary parts of life.

Different studies produce different estimates because they examine different groups and time periods. None establishes that one method is best for every person.

You don’t need to use dating apps because other people use them.

And using an app doesn’t make a relationship less genuine.

What matters is whether the setting gives you opportunities to meet people who are available, interested, and potentially compatible.

It also matters whether the process works for you.

Some people prefer the clarity of profiles that state dating intentions. Others prefer getting acquainted through shared interests before considering romance.

Both approaches involve uncertainty.

A dating profile can tell you some facts. A conversation can reveal more. Repeated interaction can reveal things you could not have known at the beginning.

No meeting method eliminates the need to discover mutual interest and compatibility.

Finding someone who works differently from you

Suppose you prefer direct questions and clear plans.

Your date prefers a conversation that moves freely between topics and doesn’t always decide things in advance.

You might feel confused by their indirect answers. They might think your questions sound unusually formal.

Neither of you is necessarily being unkind.

You may simply understand communication differently.

A systematic review of romantic relationships in autism found that many autistic people wanted romantic relationships, while some encountered greater difficulty initiating and maintaining them. The review included only 13 studies, so its findings cannot describe every autistic person’s experience (Yew et al., 2021).

In a small Australian study, autistic and non-autistic adults described several similar foundations for intimacy, including communication, shared interests, respect, safety, and effort. Autistic participants also described uncertainty about relationships and communication as a barrier (Sala et al., 2020).

The useful lesson isn’t that one kind of person needs to learn how to date like another kind.

It’s that two people need ways to understand each other.

Consider Priya and Daniel, who have been on two dates.

Priya likes to have plans agreed upon several days ahead. Daniel is comfortable deciding what to do on the day.

Priya asks, “Can we choose a place by Wednesday? I find it easier when I know what to expect.”

Daniel says, “Sure. I don’t usually plan that early, but Wednesday works.”

That exchange doesn’t require either person to change their personality. They have found an arrangement that respects both preferences.

Now imagine Daniel said that Priya was being ridiculous for needing a plan.

That response would tell Priya something different about whether this relationship might work.

Differences don’t automatically mean incompatibility. Refusing to respect another person’s needs may be a more serious problem.

The same principle applies when both people are autistic.

One might enjoy extended conversations about a favorite subject while the other needs quiet breaks. One might welcome frequent touch while the other prefers to ask first.

A small Australian study of autistic and non-autistic adults found considerable variety in what participants wanted from physical intimacy, including differences involving nonsexual touch and sensory preferences. The sample was mostly women or feminine-identifying adults, so it should not be treated as a rule for everyone (Sala et al., 2024).

These preferences are something to discover, not something to assume from a label.

Being compatible doesn’t require having identical brains, communication styles, interests, or sensory needs.

It requires a connection in which both people’s needs can be taken seriously.

If you're dating someone who treats dating like an exam

Someone who seems formal, over-prepared or anxious about doing it correctly may be managing a situation with no visible rules, not performing a personality.

Saying the obvious out loud helps more than reassurance does: “There’s nothing to get right here. I’m enjoying this.” Naming what’s actually happening gives them something solid to work from.

You’ll often see them relax considerably once the uncertainty drops. That version is worth waiting a couple of dates for.

A good date doesn’t guarantee a relationship

It’s natural to want signs that a connection is progressing.

You might be relieved when someone laughs at your jokes, shares an interest, or says they enjoyed the evening.

Those moments can be encouraging. Enjoy them.

But they aren’t promises.

Likewise, one awkward silence doesn’t mean the relationship is doomed. A nervous first meeting doesn’t tell you everything about how future conversations would feel.

You don’t need to turn every detail into evidence for or against a future together.

After a date, the immediate question can be much smaller:

Would another meeting be welcome and worthwhile for both of us?

Sometimes the answer is clearly yes.

Sometimes it’s clearly no.

Sometimes you’re uncertain because you enjoyed parts of the meeting but need more information.

Uncertainty is allowed.

For example, Rosa meets Leah for coffee. They get along, but the conversation feels slightly awkward. Rosa isn’t sure whether that’s because they’re unfamiliar with each other or because they don’t connect very well.

Rosa might decide she’d like another meeting to find out more.

Leah might agree.

Neither person needs to promise anything beyond that next date.

Alternatively, Rosa might decide she isn’t interested enough to continue. That’s also a valid conclusion.

There is no universally correct number of dates to give someone before making a decision. The research used here doesn’t establish one.

You can choose to continue when both people are interested, comfortable enough, and curious to learn more.

You can choose to stop when you don’t want to continue.

And you can take time to think rather than forcing an immediate answer.

The important thing is not to treat uncertainty as a reason to ignore a clear refusal, either yours or theirs.

When a date doesn’t go well

Not every disappointing date is a bad date.

Maybe your date was kind but your interests and goals didn’t align.

Maybe you had an enjoyable conversation without feeling romantic interest.

Maybe you discovered that you wanted different levels of commitment.

You learned something useful, even if the experience was disappointing.

But there’s a difference between a disappointing date and one where someone treats you badly.

If a person repeatedly ignores what you say you’re comfortable with, pressures you for affection, or behaves in a way that makes you feel unsafe, you don’t need to collect more evidence before deciding to leave or stop seeing them.

The purpose of dating isn’t to prove how much discomfort you can tolerate.

Nor does mutual discovery mean both people have to agree about why a date should end.

You are responsible for respecting the other person’s choices and boundaries. You are not responsible for persuading them that your decision is correct.

The same applies when someone ends things with you.

You can be disappointed, angry, confused, or sad. Those feelings don’t give you a claim on another person’s time, attention, or affection.

And if a date ends simply because the connection isn’t right, it doesn’t need to become a story about betrayal, inadequacy, or wasted effort.

You met. You learned something. You made a decision.

Sometimes that’s the complete outcome.

What success actually looks like

If you measure dating success only by whether a relationship begins, nearly everything before that relationship can feel like failure.

That makes it difficult to appreciate the information you’re gaining.

A more useful measure is whether you’re making informed, respectful choices.

You can have a successful dating experience when you notice that someone is incompatible with your goals and decide not to continue.

You can have one when you recognize that an otherwise appealing person doesn’t treat your needs with respect.

You can have one when you express your interest honestly, receive a no, and accept it.

You can also have one when someone treats you well, the connection feels mutual, and both of you choose another date.

These outcomes are different, but they share something important: you are paying attention to reality rather than trying to force a particular result.

Of course, this doesn’t mean rejection should feel wonderful or that you’re expected to celebrate every disappointing encounter.

You are allowed to want love and feel sad when you haven’t found it.

You’re allowed to become attached to someone and grieve the connection you hoped for.

You don’t need to pretend that finding out someone isn’t interested feels as good as finding out they are.

But disappointment does not make the process meaningless.

The question is whether you can learn from what actually happened without turning it into a verdict on yourself.

A small exercise after your next date

You don’t need a complicated rating system or a spreadsheet ranking potential partners.

In fact, treating people as collections of scores can distract you from your actual experience.

Instead, after your next date, take a few quiet minutes and answer four questions.

  1. Did I enjoy this person? Not just their appearance or attention, but the experience of being with them.
  2. Could I be reasonably myself? Did I feel able to communicate my needs, preferences, and boundaries?
  3. What did I learn? Consider what you discovered about their interests, goals, behavior, and what being together felt like.
  4. Do I want to know more? Is there a reason to continue that comes from your own interest, rather than fear of losing an opportunity?

You don’t have to give each answer a score.

You don’t have to decide whether the person could become your lifelong partner.

You are simply separating what happened from the hopes and worries you’ve attached to it.

If you want another date, you can express that clearly:

“I enjoyed spending time with you. Would you like to meet again?”

If you’re interested but uncertain, you can be honest about wanting to get to know them further:

“I had a good time. I’d be interested in meeting again and getting to know you better, if you’d like that.”

If you don’t want to continue, you can say so kindly:

“Thank you for meeting with me. I don’t feel the connection I’m looking for, so I don’t want another date. I wish you well.”

These are invitations and decisions, not methods for controlling another person’s response.

You cannot choose their answer for them.

But you can choose to communicate honestly, listen to their answer, and act respectfully.

Two people, two choices

Return to Sam, walking away from that coffee shop with Jules.

Sam has been replaying every sentence, trying to determine whether Jules was impressed.

But the encounter wasn’t an audition.

Sam was there too, with preferences, feelings, goals, and choices.

Maybe Sam genuinely enjoyed Jules’s company and wants another date.

Maybe Jules was charming but Sam felt uncomfortable with how often Jules interrupted.

Maybe both people had a pleasant afternoon and neither felt enough interest to continue.

Maybe something promising has begun.

None of those outcomes can be determined solely by whether Sam performed well enough.

Dating asks you to be interested in another person while remembering that your own experience matters too.

It asks you to notice compatibility rather than merely chase approval.

It asks you to accept that wanting someone doesn’t create an obligation for them, and being wanted doesn’t create an obligation for you.

Finding a relationship isn’t a matter of winning someone over until they finally agree to choose you.

It’s discovering whether two people, each free to say yes or no, want to keep choosing each other.

And before you can learn whether someone is right for you, you have to begin getting to know them.
