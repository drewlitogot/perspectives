The date went well. You laughed, talked easily, and agreed to see each other again.

The next morning, you send a message. By lunchtime, there’s no reply.

One person might think, They’re probably busy, and get on with their day.

Another might check their phone twenty times, replay the conversation, and wonder what they did wrong.

A third might feel irritated. If they can’t be bothered to answer, neither can I. They decide not to respond when the message finally arrives.

And someone else might barely think about the unanswered message at all, but feel intensely uncomfortable when the person suggests becoming more serious a few weeks later.

None of these reactions tells you everything about a person. But each raises a useful question: Where did you learn what closeness, uncertainty, and rejection are supposed to feel like?

You learned some things about relationships before you ever went on a date.

You watched adults disagree. You noticed whether apologies happened. You learned what happened when you said no, needed help, made a mistake, or showed feelings other people didn’t like.

Perhaps your home was affectionate and predictable. Perhaps it was loving but chaotic. Perhaps everyone got along as long as nobody brought up a problem.

Maybe the adults around you were struggling themselves. Maybe the hardest lessons came from classmates, friends, or an earlier relationship rather than your family.

Those experiences don’t dictate the relationships you’ll have. They can, however, influence what you expect from other people and what you do when something feels uncertain.

Understanding those expectations gives you more choices.

The relationship rules you didn’t know you learned

Imagine two children growing up in different households.

In one, a disagreement might sound like this:

“I’m upset about what happened.”

“Okay. Tell me.”

The adults sometimes argue. They sometimes get things wrong. But disagreement doesn’t automatically mean the relationship is in danger.

In another household, an ordinary disagreement might lead to days of silence, shouting, ridicule, or a sudden change in how affection is given.

A child in that home may learn to pay very close attention to other people’s moods.

The lesson might be: Keep everyone happy, or something bad happens.

Years later, these two people could go on equally pleasant dates but experience the same moment differently.

When a potential partner says, “Actually, I’d rather do something else Saturday,” one person hears a change of plans. The other hears the beginning of rejection.

The second person’s reaction may be understandable given their history. It isn’t necessarily an accurate reading of the present situation.

That’s the distinction worth learning: a feeling can make sense without correctly predicting what someone else will do.

Childhood adversity has been associated with a wide range of later health and interpersonal difficulties. A systematic review and meta-analysis covering 37 studies and more than 250,000 participants found that people reporting multiple adverse childhood experiences faced higher risks across the outcomes examined, including mental-health difficulties and violence-related outcomes (Hughes et al., 2017).

Researchers call these experiences adverse childhood experiences, or ACEs. They can include abuse, neglect, and certain forms of serious household instability.

But the studies don’t establish that a particular childhood experience makes you choose a particular kind of romantic partner.

They also don’t mean that everyone who grew up in difficult circumstances will struggle with relationships. An ACE score isn’t a prediction of your romantic future.

Your history is part of your explanation, not a verdict.

What felt normal?

Consider what you learned about these ordinary situations:

  • Disagreement: Could someone be unhappy with you and still care about you?
  • Affection: Was kindness given freely, or did it seem to depend on pleasing someone?
  • Independence: Could you have different interests, opinions, and friends without getting into trouble?
  • Mistakes: Were mistakes discussed, ignored, mocked, or punished?
  • Help: When you needed support, did you expect someone to respond?

You don’t need a dramatic childhood story to recognize a pattern.

Perhaps your family never discussed feelings. Nobody was deliberately cruel, but you grew up without much practice telling someone you were hurt.

Perhaps you were praised for being the person who never needed anything.

Perhaps you learned that admitting interest in someone invited ridicule.

Those experiences may leave you with habits that once made sense but no longer help you.

A useful question isn’t simply, “What happened to me?”

It’s also, “What did I learn to do because of it, and is that still helping me?”

Attachment: how you respond to closeness

Attachment describes patterns in how people experience emotional closeness, dependence, reassurance, and possible separation.

You may have encountered online explanations that divide everyone into four attachment styles: secure, anxious, avoidant, and fearful-avoidant.

Those labels can provide a starting vocabulary. But they can also make people sound like four completely different species.

The research is more nuanced.

A study examining whether adult attachment falls into distinct categories found stronger support for continuous dimensions rather than separate types (Fraley et al., 2015).

In plain language, attachment is better understood through two questions:

How much do you worry that someone you care about will reject or abandon you?

This is called attachment anxiety.

How uncomfortable do you feel depending on someone or allowing emotional closeness?

This is called attachment avoidance.

You can experience more or less of either. They aren’t mutually exclusive.

Attachment anxiety

Someone with greater attachment anxiety may find uncertainty particularly difficult.

For example, Luis, 32, has been seeing another man for a month. They enjoy spending time together, but when his date has a busy weekend, Luis starts wondering whether the relationship is ending.

He wants reassurance, but he’s afraid that asking will make him seem demanding.

He sends several messages, then regrets them. When his date replies warmly, he feels better.

The problem isn’t that Luis wants reassurance. Wanting reassurance is ordinary.

The difficulty comes when uncertainty repeatedly becomes evidence of rejection, even when there isn’t enough information to reach that conclusion.

Luis can practice separating three things:

  • Fact: His date hasn’t replied since yesterday.
  • Interpretation: His date is losing interest.
  • Need: He would appreciate clearer communication about when they’ll talk.

Instead of treating the interpretation as fact, he could say:

“I like talking with you, and I do better when I have an idea of when we’ll catch up. Would you be comfortable making a plan for our next call?”

His date is free to agree, suggest something different, or decline.

The request is more useful than trying to make his date prove that everything is okay.

Attachment avoidance

Someone with greater attachment avoidance may feel uncomfortable when emotional closeness or dependence increases.

For example, Evelyn, 41, genuinely enjoys dating Tessa. They have good conversations, similar values, and plenty of affection.

But when Tessa begins talking about spending more weekends together, Evelyn feels a strong urge to withdraw.

She suddenly starts noticing minor things that irritate her. She wonders whether she was ever interested.

The question isn’t whether Evelyn must continue the relationship. She doesn’t.

The question is whether something about Tessa is genuinely incompatible with her needs, or whether closeness itself has started to feel uncomfortable.

Those aren’t the same thing.

Evelyn might choose to slow the pace rather than end a relationship she otherwise values.

She could say, “I like spending time with you. I also need plenty of time to myself. Could we keep one weekend day separate for now?”

Again, Tessa has choices. She might be comfortable with that arrangement, or she might want a relationship with more shared time.

Neither person has to be wrong for their needs to differ.

What secure attachment actually means

Secure attachment generally describes relative comfort with both closeness and independence, without intense anxiety about rejection or a strong need to avoid dependence.

It doesn’t mean never feeling jealous, never needing reassurance, or always communicating perfectly.

Someone can be generally comfortable with closeness and still become worried when a partner behaves inconsistently.

Someone who values independence can also build a close, caring relationship.

Attachment is not a ranking of good and bad people.

Nor is a label a diagnosis.

Calling a date “avoidant” doesn’t establish why they didn’t call. Calling yourself “anxious” doesn’t make repeated unwanted messages acceptable.

You still have to look at what happened, what each person meant, and whether both people are willing to communicate respectfully.

Can attachment patterns change?

Yes, although change isn’t automatic.

A longitudinal study following more than 4,000 adults found that attachment sometimes shifted around important life events. Some changes lasted, while others were temporary. People differed considerably in how they responded (Fraley et al., 2021).

That means two reassuring things can be true at once.

You aren’t permanently assigned an attachment pattern in childhood.

And one wonderful date probably won’t erase years of learned expectations.

You can practice responding differently without demanding that your feelings change immediately.

For example, if you tend to assume rejection when someone needs time alone, your first goal may simply be to recognize that assumption before acting on it.

You may still feel afraid.

You can choose not to turn that fear into an accusation.

Not every need for distance is attachment avoidance

This distinction is especially important when you or your partner processes the world differently.

Imagine that Noor and May are both autistic.

Noor enjoys affectionate messages and likes knowing when she and May will see each other next.

May loves Noor’s company but finds continuous conversation tiring. After an afternoon together, she sometimes prefers to spend the evening alone, without messaging.

An outsider might call May avoidant.

That conclusion would skip an important question: Why does she need the time alone?

May might be perfectly comfortable with emotional closeness while needing time with fewer demands.

She may also have concerns about dependence. Or both might be true.

Neither Noor nor May can determine the answer from a label.

A 2025 systematic review examined 12 studies of attachment in autistic adults or people with autistic traits. The review reported more insecure attachment patterns in the samples examined, but its evidence had substantial limitations. The aggregated participants were overwhelmingly women, many studies used measures of autistic traits rather than confirmed diagnoses, and some of the directly relevant studies were very small.

One small study discussed in the review raised the concern that autistic ways of speaking or recalling experiences could be interpreted as signs of attachment insecurity by an interview measure (Sonfelianu et al., 2025).

That doesn’t mean attachment difficulties are impossible for autistic people. It means that communication differences shouldn’t automatically be treated as evidence of an emotional problem.

If closeness feels good but frequent contact is tiring

You might genuinely enjoy someone and still need long periods without conversation, messages, or social expectations. That preference does not, by itself, mean you’re afraid of intimacy.

It can help to make the distinction visible. For example: “I had a really good time with you. I usually spend the following evening by myself. I’d like to see you again Saturday.”

The other person still gets to decide whether that amount of contact works for them. Explaining your needs makes compatibility easier to discuss without requiring either person to guess.

If you're dating someone who needs time alone

Try asking what the time alone means to them instead of deciding that it means rejection. A preference for less frequent contact isn’t necessarily a preference for less emotional closeness.

You can also explain your own needs: “I respect that you need quiet time. It helps me to know when we’ll talk again. Could we agree on a rough time?”

You don’t have to give up your need for connection, and your partner doesn’t have to give up their need for space. Sometimes a workable arrangement exists. Sometimes the difference is too large. Either result is useful information.

When attention and expectations don’t line up

ADHD adds another reason to be cautious about simple explanations.

A meta-analysis of 70 observational studies found an association between adverse childhood experiences and ADHD indicators, but the studies varied substantially and couldn’t establish a straightforward causal relationship (Zhang et al., 2022).

A separate longitudinal study of Puerto Rican children found that ADHD at the start of the study predicted greater exposure to subsequent adversity (Lugo-Candelas et al., 2021).

These findings don’t establish an adult ADHD attachment style, and they don’t tell you what a particular person’s delayed reply means.

If attention, time management, or changing demands affect communication, the useful conversation is about what actually happens.

A person may care deeply and still struggle to maintain a messaging routine. Their partner may understand that difficulty and still need more consistent contact.

Understanding a reason doesn’t automatically solve the problem, but it can help two people decide what would be workable.

When your history includes trauma

Some childhood experiences are difficult. Others involve sustained fear, abuse, neglect, or serious violations of safety.

These distinctions matter. Not every unpleasant memory is trauma, and not everyone who experiences trauma develops a trauma-related disorder.

But when someone has repeatedly experienced danger or mistreatment, ordinary dating situations may carry meanings that aren’t obvious to a new partner.

A raised voice might be particularly frightening. A sudden change of plans might create an expectation that something bad is about to happen. Receiving kindness might feel unfamiliar or difficult to trust.

These are examples of possible experiences, not a checklist for diagnosing yourself.

It’s possible to understand why you react strongly without assuming every situation is dangerous.

It’s equally important not to explain away genuine mistreatment as something you’re imagining because of your history.

Your past can affect how you read a situation. Your present circumstances still deserve to be evaluated on their own.

PTSD and complex PTSD

Post-traumatic stress disorder, or PTSD, is a diagnosis associated with particular difficulties following trauma. These include reliving traumatic experiences, avoiding reminders, and experiencing a continuing sense of threat.

Complex post-traumatic stress disorder, or CPTSD, is a related diagnosis that also includes persistent difficulties with emotional regulation, negative beliefs about oneself, and relationships.

The World Health Organization’s ICD-11 diagnostic system recognizes CPTSD separately from PTSD. The DSM-5 system used in the United States does not include a separate CPTSD diagnosis (Larsen, 2026).

CPTSD is often associated with repeated interpersonal trauma, including experiences beginning in childhood. But a particular childhood history isn’t required for the diagnosis.

Likewise, experiencing repeated trauma doesn’t automatically mean someone has CPTSD.

A US population-based survey of 1,839 adults estimated that 3.4% met questionnaire criteria for ICD-11 PTSD and 3.8% for CPTSD. Cumulative childhood trauma was more strongly associated with CPTSD, and childhood abuse by caregivers was among the associated experiences (Cloitre et al., 2019).

These are population estimates, not a way to diagnose yourself from your dating experiences.

If trauma-related difficulties are affecting your daily life or relationships, a qualified mental-health professional can help you understand what you’re experiencing and explore appropriate support.

You don’t have to establish a diagnosis before deciding that an experience deserves attention.

And you don’t have to tell a new romantic partner the details of what happened to you.

You can explain a present need without recounting its entire history.

For example:

“I sometimes need a little time before discussing something upsetting. I’m willing to talk about it, but I don’t want to do that while we’re both upset.”

That gives your partner information they can use without requiring you to disclose anything you’re not ready to share.

What if trauma has been missed?

Research into autistic adults illustrates why it is important to separate trauma experiences, symptoms, and formal diagnoses.

A 2025 meta-analysis combining 13 adult studies involving more than 142,000 autistic adults reported an estimated PTSD point prevalence of 2.06%, meaning the proportion meeting the study definitions at a particular time. The uncertainty around that estimate was very wide (Mansour et al., 2025).

By contrast, a much smaller UK study of 59 self-selected, trauma-exposed autistic adults found that more than 40% screened positive for probable PTSD symptoms in the previous month. Some participants described traumatic experiences that would not satisfy the usual DSM-5 event requirement for PTSD (Rumball et al., 2020).

Those figures seem contradictory until you consider who was counted.

The larger analysis concerned identified or criterion-based PTSD across studies. The smaller study used screening questionnaires in a selected group of people who had already experienced events they considered traumatic.

A positive screening result is not a clinical diagnosis, and the smaller group’s results cannot be generalized to all autistic adults.

The authors of the larger review raised concerns that existing criteria may miss some autistic trauma experiences. That is an important possibility, not proof of a particular underdiagnosis rate (Mansour et al., 2025).

For dating, the practical lesson is simpler.

You don’t need to determine whether a partner’s experience qualifies for a diagnosis before taking their discomfort seriously.

You also shouldn’t assume that every difficulty is caused by trauma.

Listen to what the person tells you. Ask what helps. Respect what they’re willing and unwilling to discuss.

When saying yes feels safer than saying no

You’re invited to spend the evening with someone you’ve been dating.

You’re exhausted and want to stay home.

You open your mouth to decline, but instead you hear yourself saying, “Sure.”

You spend the evening wishing you hadn’t agreed. You feel frustrated with the other person, even though they may have no idea you wanted something different.

Why did you say yes?

There are several possible answers.

Maybe you genuinely wanted to go but regretted it later.

Maybe you have trouble making decisions quickly.

Maybe you wanted to be considerate.

Or maybe saying no felt dangerous because you expected disappointment, anger, or rejection.

The last possibility is often discussed using the word fawning.

In clinical and community discussions, fawning describes appeasing someone, agreeing with them, or suppressing your own needs when you feel threatened.

It’s a recognizable description of behavior. But the sources reviewed for this chapter don’t establish fawning as a separate, validated trauma-response category with its own reliable diagnostic test.

You don’t need to decide whether you “are a fawner” to notice that you’re agreeing to things you don’t want.

The more useful questions are:

Am I choosing this freely?

Do I feel able to decline?

What do I expect will happen if I say no?

There’s an important distinction between compromise and agreement driven by fear.

Compromise means you consider another person’s wishes and willingly choose an arrangement that works well enough for you.

Agreement driven by fear means you’re trying to prevent a reaction you believe you can’t safely handle.

Only you can explore what was happening inside your decision. But the other person’s behavior also matters.

A partner who respects you should be able to hear an ordinary refusal without threatening, punishing, or pressuring you.

Practice buying yourself time

If you often agree before you know what you want, try giving yourself permission to answer later.

You might say:

“Can I think about that and get back to you tomorrow?”

Or:

“I don’t know whether I have the energy for that tonight. I’ll let you know.”

Or simply:

“No, thank you.”

You don’t need to provide an elaborate justification for an ordinary boundary.

A boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept. A request asks someone else to do something, and they can decline.

“I won’t make plans when I’m too tired to enjoy them” is a boundary.

“Would you be willing to plan dates a few days ahead?” is a request.

Both can be useful.

The person you’re dating has the same rights. They can change their mind, say no to affection, decline an invitation, or decide they don’t want to continue dating.

Even if rejection brings up difficult feelings, those feelings don’t create an obligation for the other person to change their answer.

If you recognize yourself responding to someone’s refusal with pressure, repeated unwanted contact, threats, or punishment, stop that behavior. Respect the refusal and seek individual professional help if you’re struggling to do so.

Your discomfort deserves attention. It doesn’t justify controlling someone else.

If you agree before you know what you want

Sometimes the first challenge isn’t saying no. It’s noticing that you have a choice.

You can practice with low-stakes decisions: which restaurant you prefer, whether you want to watch a movie, or whether you’re available Saturday. Try stating a genuine preference without immediately offering to give it up.

If saying no to someone creates a credible fear of retaliation, this isn’t just a communication exercise. Your safety matters more than practicing assertiveness with that person. Seek support from someone safe or a relevant professional service.

When chaos feels like chemistry

There is a difference between an exciting relationship and an unpredictable one.

An exciting relationship might involve strong attraction, enjoyable surprises, unfamiliar experiences, and discovering things about another person.

An unpredictable relationship might involve constantly wondering where you stand, whether a boundary will be respected, or whether the other person will treat you kindly tomorrow.

Sometimes those experiences get confused.

You might feel an intense pull toward someone who gives you very little clarity.

You might mistake relief after a period of uncertainty for evidence of closeness.

Or you might meet someone considerate and consistent and think, Why don’t I feel more excited?

People sometimes describe these experiences by saying that “chaos feels like chemistry.”

It’s a memorable phrase, but it isn’t a proven psychological law.

The research available for this chapter does not establish that childhood trauma causes people to find chaotic dating partners more attractive.

There is, however, some evidence about emotional attachment within already abusive relationships.

In an older study of 75 women who had recently left abusive relationships, researchers found associations between intermittent maltreatment, differences in power, and continuing emotional attachment after separation (Dutton & Painter, 1993).

The study helps explain why emotional attachment can persist under harmful circumstances. It doesn’t establish that survivors seek out harmful partners, or that strong attraction indicates trauma.

A broader meta-analysis has also documented subsequent sexual victimization among people who experienced childhood sexual abuse (Walker et al., 2019).

That association must never be turned into an accusation that survivors chose, invited, or caused later mistreatment.

Responsibility for abuse belongs to the person committing it.

Attraction isn’t a safety assessment

Suppose you’re dating someone who makes you feel intensely excited.

You think about them all day. You can’t wait to see them.

That’s information about your attraction.

It isn’t yet enough information about their character, compatibility, or willingness to respect you.

You learn those things over time.

Likewise, someone who treats you well isn’t automatically the right romantic partner.

You don’t owe someone a relationship because they’re kind. You need enough attraction, shared interest, and compatibility to want the relationship yourself.

The goal isn’t to choose someone who feels boring.

It’s to stop confusing emotional intensity with evidence that a relationship is right for you.

Consider two situations.

In the first, someone takes a little time to answer messages but communicates clearly about their interest. They respect your decisions. Their actions generally match what they tell you.

In the second, someone repeatedly leaves you uncertain about basic expectations, disregards your limits, and makes you afraid of their reactions.

You may feel strong attraction in either situation.

But the second contains problems that attraction cannot solve.

You can want someone and still decide that dating them is not good for you.

Look at behavior when things don’t go their way

Early dates are often pleasant. The more revealing information comes when two people want different things.

Notice what happens when:

  • You decline an invitation.
  • You ask to slow the pace of physical affection.
  • You express a preference that differs from theirs.
  • A plan changes.
  • You raise a small concern.

You aren’t looking for a person who never feels disappointed or frustrated.

You’re looking for someone who can experience those feelings without taking away your freedom to make decisions.

The US Office on Women’s Health identifies controlling someone’s activities or relationships, monitoring communications without permission, threats, sexual coercion, and isolation as warning signs of intimate partner abuse. These concerns apply to dating relationships, including same-sex relationships (Office on Women’s Health, 2026).

An isolated awkward moment isn’t automatically abuse. But repeated violations of your boundaries, threats, or coercion deserve serious attention.

If you’re worried about abuse, seeking confidential outside support can be safer than trying to resolve everything privately with the person involved.

In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at 800-799-SAFE (7233). For immediate danger, contact emergency services. Additional support and crisis resources are covered in Chapters 59–60.

You do not need to prove that your childhood made you vulnerable before seeking help.

And you don’t need to settle the question of whether you love someone before deciding that harmful behavior is unacceptable.

Your partner has a history, too

It can be tempting to use everything you’ve learned in this chapter to analyze the person you’re dating.

They dislike texting? Avoidant.

They want reassurance? Anxious.

They don’t want to discuss an upsetting subject immediately? Trauma.

They change their plans? ADHD.

These interpretations can create misunderstandings instead of resolving them.

A behavior may have several possible explanations.

Someone might want reassurance because of earlier experiences, because the relationship is currently uncertain, or simply because they appreciate clear affection.

Someone might avoid a difficult conversation because they’re afraid of closeness, because they need time to think, or because the discussion is happening at a terrible moment.

You aren’t required to figure out the hidden psychological explanation.

You can ask about what matters to the relationship.

Consider Jackson and Eli, both in their late twenties.

Jackson likes talking through disagreements immediately. Eli prefers to think before answering. After their first serious disagreement, Jackson interprets Eli’s silence as withdrawal.

Eli interprets Jackson’s repeated attempts to continue talking as pressure.

Both have information the other person needs.

Jackson says, “When you stop responding, I start wondering whether we’re going to resolve things.”

Eli answers, “I do want to resolve it. I just need time to work out what I want to say.”

They agree to return to the discussion the following evening.

That agreement doesn’t require either person to accept the other’s interpretation as the only correct one.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book. Your position and experiences affect what you notice. So do the other person’s.

Describing what each of you sees is more useful than declaring whose perspective is psychologically defective.

Understanding doesn’t require tolerating everything

You can be compassionate toward a person’s history and still decide that a relationship doesn’t work for you.

Perhaps someone has good reasons for needing long periods without contact, but you want a relationship with frequent communication.

Perhaps someone has experienced repeated rejection, but their requests for reassurance exceed what you’re willing to provide.

Perhaps you care about someone who has survived trauma, but they’re behaving in ways that violate your boundaries.

You can acknowledge the circumstances without surrendering your own needs.

The same applies in reverse.

A partner doesn’t have to accept everything you do because you have a particular history or diagnosis.

Explanations can guide understanding. They don’t erase the effects of behavior on another person.

Separating yesterday from today

Understanding your history doesn’t require spending every date searching for childhood patterns.

A more manageable approach is to notice situations in which your reaction feels unusually strong, automatic, or difficult to explain.

Try this short exercise.

Three columns: learned rule, present need, next choice

Choose one situation that comes up in your relationships or friendships.

Write down three things:

What I may have learned What I need now One choice I can practice
“Disagreement means someone will leave.” To know that differences can be discussed respectfully. Ask one clear question instead of immediately apologizing or withdrawing.
“If someone needs space, they don’t care.” Some clarity about when contact will resume. Request a rough plan rather than assuming rejection.
“My preferences are inconvenient.” To have an equal say in ordinary plans. Name a genuine preference and allow the other person to respond.
“Strong feelings mean this person must be right for me.” Time to discover compatibility. Observe how the person behaves across several situations before making major commitments.

These are examples, not categories you have to fit into.

Your own learned rule might be entirely different.

Perhaps you learned that asking for help was embarrassing. Your next choice could be making one small, direct request.

Perhaps you learned that people only appreciate you when you’re useful. Your next choice could be noticing whether someone enjoys spending time with you when you aren’t doing anything for them.

Perhaps you learned to leave as soon as a relationship becomes emotionally important. Your next choice could be identifying what specifically worries you before deciding whether to continue.

You don’t have to challenge every old expectation at once.

Pick one that affects how you treat yourself or others today.

A four-question check after a difficult moment

The next time a dating interaction brings up an unexpectedly strong reaction, try asking:

1. What actually happened?

Describe observable events without adding conclusions.

“They canceled Thursday’s dinner and suggested Sunday.”

2. What did I assume it meant?

“They’re losing interest.”

3. What else could explain it?

“They may genuinely be unavailable Thursday. I don’t know yet.”

4. What can I ask or decide without controlling them?

“I can ask whether Sunday still works and observe whether they follow through.”

This exercise isn’t meant to convince you that everything is fine.

Sometimes the facts do show a pattern of unreliability, incompatibility, or mistreatment.

The purpose is to give facts, interpretations, feelings, and decisions separate places in the conversation.

That makes it easier to respond to what is happening rather than only to what you fear might happen.

What if you don’t remember much about childhood?

You don’t need a detailed autobiography to recognize your current relationship habits.

Some people remember childhood clearly. Others have only scattered memories, don’t know what to make of those memories, or simply don’t want to revisit them.

You can begin with what you notice today.

Do you apologize when you haven’t done anything wrong?

Do you become frightened when someone expresses disappointment?

Do you feel compelled to prove your value through constant helpfulness?

Do you pull away from people you like without knowing why?

Do you struggle to identify what you want until after you’ve agreed to something else?

These questions are invitations to reflect, not signs that you have a disorder.

You may find a connection to your history. You may discover a practical communication problem. You may decide that professional support would help.

And sometimes you’ll find that a particular person simply isn’t a good match.

Not every uncomfortable dating experience needs a childhood explanation.

You can learn a different relationship

There is no single correct amount of closeness, independence, affection, or reassurance.

People differ in how much time they want together, how quickly they become comfortable, and how they prefer to communicate.

The point is not to become so independent that you never need anybody.

It isn’t to become so trusting that you ignore warning signs.

And it isn’t to become so understanding of other people’s histories that you forget your own limits.

The aim is to recognize the difference between what you learned to expect and what is actually happening with the person in front of you.

Maybe you grew up believing disagreement meant danger. You can practice having disagreements with people who respect you.

Maybe you learned that affection had to be earned. You can practice noticing whether someone values you without requiring constant proof.

Maybe you learned to keep everyone happy. You can practice making an ordinary choice that someone else might not prefer.

None of those changes requires rewriting your past or pretending your feelings aren’t real.

You can feel uncertain and still ask a clear question.

You can want affection and still respect someone’s no.

You can care about another person’s history without accepting harmful behavior.

And you can discover that a relationship isn’t right for you without deciding that either person is defective.

Before your next date, consider one question:

“Am I responding to what this person is showing me, or to what I’ve learned to expect from other people?”

You might not know the answer immediately. That’s fine.

Notice what happens. Ask when something is unclear. Let the other person tell you about themselves rather than filling every gap with an assumption.

Your history influences the glasses you wear. It doesn’t have to decide everything you see.
