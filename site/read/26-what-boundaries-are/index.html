You’re enjoying getting to know someone. You like their company, you look forward to their messages, and you’re beginning to wonder where things might go.

Then, one Tuesday evening, your phone rings.

You’ve had a long day. You were planning to make dinner, watch something familiar, and go to bed early. But the person you’re dating wants to talk.

You answer.

The next evening, they call again. You answer again, even though you’re tired. By Friday, you’re beginning to dread the sound of your phone.

Nothing terrible has happened. The other person hasn’t threatened you or demanded anything. They enjoy talking to you. You enjoy talking to them, too.

But you don’t want to talk every evening.

What are you supposed to do?

If you learned that caring about someone means always being available, you might keep answering. If you’ve had little dating experience, you might wonder whether daily phone calls are simply what couples are expected to do. If you’ve previously upset people by saying no, you might be afraid that declining a call will hurt the relationship.

You could even become irritated with the other person for repeatedly doing something you’ve never told them bothers you.

There is another option.

You can like someone and still have limits.

Those limits are called boundaries.

A boundary is about what you will do or accept

A boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept.

That’s the working definition used in this book. It’s a practical way of understanding relationships, not a single scientific definition that everyone has agreed upon. Professional guidance describes boundaries in terms of your own comfort, choices, time, and behavior rather than your authority over somebody else’s life (Cleveland Clinic, 2026).

Consider a few examples:

  • You enjoy spending time with someone, but you need evenings alone.
  • You like talking about personal experiences, but you’re not ready to discuss your childhood.
  • You’re happy to disagree about politics, but you won’t continue a conversation where someone insults you.
  • You want a romantic relationship, but you don’t want to share your phone password.
  • You care about your partner, but you cannot be their only source of emotional support.

These are different situations, but the underlying idea is the same.

Something matters to you, and you get to decide what participation you’re comfortable with.

A boundary does not have to mean that the other person has done something wrong. They might simply want something different.

You may love spontaneous plans. Someone else may need to know their schedule several days ahead. Neither preference makes a person defective.

You might enjoy hours of conversation. Your date might reach a point where talking becomes exhausting.

Someone might be comfortable discussing sex early in dating. You might want to wait until you know each other better.

Boundaries give people a way to remain separate individuals while exploring whether a relationship works for both of them.

They are not a substitute for kindness, and they don’t eliminate the need for compromise. They make it possible to discuss what each person can willingly offer.

You are still a separate person when you’re dating

Dating someone does not mean combining two lives into one person.

You still have your own body, thoughts, friendships, responsibilities, possessions, time, and decisions. The other person has theirs.

Getting closer might mean sharing more of those things. But closeness is not the same as unlimited access.

You can love somebody without being available every minute. You can trust somebody without giving them your passwords. You can care about their feelings without agreeing with every opinion they express.

And you can want a relationship without accepting every condition somebody attaches to it.

One useful question is:

“What am I comfortable participating in, and what will I do if that limit isn’t respected?”

Notice that the question concerns your own choices.

That distinction matters.

A boundary is not the same as a request, agreement, or control

People often use the word boundary to describe several different things.

Someone might call a preference a boundary. Someone else might call an ordinary request a boundary. Occasionally, a person uses the word to make controlling behavior sound reasonable.

The words can become confusing because all these situations involve one person wanting something.

A practical distinction is to look at who has the power to choose.

Term What it means Example
Boundary A limit on what you will do or accept “I’m going home at nine because I need to sleep.”
Request Something you ask another person to do; they can decline “Could you call me before coming over?”
Agreement Something both people freely decide to do “We’ve agreed to ask before inviting guests into our shared home.”
Control Trying to take away someone else’s freedom to decide Monitoring a partner’s messages or preventing them from seeing friends

This is a teaching distinction, not a set of categories established by a scientific experiment. It is consistent with professional guidance about boundaries and with US descriptions of controlling behavior (Cleveland Clinic, 2025; U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

Let’s look at the differences.

A request gives the other person a choice

Suppose you’re dating someone who enjoys sending messages late at night.

You could ask:

“Would you mind sending nonurgent messages before ten? I’m usually asleep after that.”

That’s a request.

The other person might agree. They might explain that they work evenings and cannot always message earlier. They might prefer a different arrangement.

You can discuss the possibilities.

You could also decide that you won’t answer messages after ten and will respond the following day.

That’s a boundary about your availability.

You don’t need to prevent the other person from sending a message to decide when you will answer it.

A request and a boundary can exist together. You can ask for a change while still being responsible for your own choices.

An agreement requires two willing people

Some relationship expectations cannot be decided by one person alone.

Consider exclusivity.

You may want to date only one person at a time, and you may only want to continue a relationship with someone who wants the same thing.

That’s a legitimate relationship requirement.

But you cannot simply decide that another person is exclusive with you because you’ve gone on several dates.

You have to discuss it and both agree.

If the other person doesn’t want exclusivity, you can decide that the relationship isn’t right for you. You cannot make their decision for them.

The same principle applies to shared plans, living arrangements, finances, and commitments.

An agreement needs both people’s participation. It can be revisited when circumstances change.

Control takes choices away

Control is different.

The issue is no longer that two people have different preferences. One person is attempting to restrict the other’s freedom through pressure, intimidation, monitoring, isolation, or other coercive behavior.

The US Department of Justice describes domestic violence as a pattern of abusive behavior used to gain or maintain power and control. That pattern can involve nonphysical behavior, including emotional, financial, and technological abuse (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

Someone might claim that their controlling behavior is necessary because of their personal boundaries.

The label doesn’t make the behavior acceptable.

Calling something a boundary does not give you authority over another person’s life.

For example, you may decide that you cannot continue dating someone whose relationship expectations differ fundamentally from yours.

You may not force them to change their friendships, beliefs, or private choices to prevent you from becoming upset.

You can express what you need. You can decide whether to stay. You cannot remove their right to choose.

There is an important caution here: the sentence structure alone does not tell you whether something is healthy.

A statement beginning with “I will” can still be a threat. A sentence beginning with “Could you” can be a respectful request.

Pay attention to what the person is actually doing, whether the other person is free to disagree, and whether pressure or fear is involved.

Different people can have different boundaries

Imagine two people looking at the same sculpture from different positions. Each sees something the other doesn’t.

You’ve already encountered this image earlier in the book.

Boundaries add another difference: people may stand in different places and also want different distances from what they’re looking at.

One person enjoys frequent contact. Another enjoys independence.

One is comfortable discussing private subjects quickly. Another needs months to develop that trust.

One likes affectionate gestures in public. Another prefers to keep affection private.

Neither person can discover the other’s limits simply by asking what most people do.

In a qualitative study of 16 gender- and sexually diverse autistic adults in the UK, participants described creating relationships that suited their individual needs rather than automatically following conventional dating rules. Explicit conversations about boundaries and expectations were important to some participants (Ciric et al., 2026).

This was a small, mostly White group of adults aged 20 to 46. Their experiences cannot tell us what every autistic person, LGBTQ+ person, or couple prefers. They do illustrate something useful: people can choose to discuss their expectations instead of relying on unwritten rules.

“But isn’t that just what couples do?”

You might have heard that people in relationships are supposed to share everything, text every day, spend weekends together, or tell each other every secret.

These are possible relationship arrangements. They aren’t obligations created by the word dating.

Even if a behavior is common among people you know, that doesn’t mean you must participate in it.

Likewise, your comfort with something doesn’t establish that your partner should be comfortable with it.

Suppose you’re happy to spend every weekend together, but your partner wants one weekend a month for hobbies and friends.

You might feel disappointed. That’s understandable.

But the disappointment doesn’t automatically mean your partner is failing at the relationship.

It means you have different expectations to discuss.

Perhaps you’ll find an arrangement both of you like. Perhaps you’ll discover that you want fundamentally different kinds of relationships.

Sometimes a boundary reveals incompatibility rather than a problem somebody needs to fix.

That discovery can be painful, especially when you’re attracted to someone. But compatibility involves more than attraction. It includes whether you can build a life that both people willingly want.

Why boundaries can be difficult to recognize

Some people have never been taught that they are allowed to have limits.

Others know what boundaries are in theory but struggle to notice when one is being crossed.

You might recognize the feeling only afterward.

During a date, you agree to stay another hour even though you’re exhausted. You smile through a conversation that makes you uncomfortable. You tell somebody you’re fine because explaining the problem seems harder than enduring it.

Later, at home, you become upset.

You wonder why you agreed.

Maybe you didn’t realize what you wanted until you were alone. Maybe you were afraid to disappoint someone. Maybe you’ve been taught that refusing is rude.

There isn’t one explanation that fits everyone.

But research involving autistic adults who have experienced interpersonal mistreatment offers important examples.

When you’re unsure what is reasonable

In a qualitative study, Pearson, Rees, and Forster collected written accounts from 43 autistic adults who had experienced interpersonal victimization by people they knew.

Some described uncertainty about what ordinary friendship boundaries should look like. They had accepted unreasonable requests or treatment partly because they wanted to maintain relationships, please others, or avoid confrontation (Pearson et al., 2022).

One difficulty was knowing whether something uncomfortable was simply an unfamiliar social expectation.

Consider someone who is new to dating.

Their date repeatedly asks them to change plans at short notice. The person feels stressed but thinks, “Maybe relationships are supposed to require this much flexibility.”

They don’t know whether they’re being unreasonable.

A boundary can help them consider a different question.

Instead of asking only, “Would most people tolerate this?”, they can ask, “Can I comfortably continue participating in this arrangement?”

The two questions are not identical.

Social expectations can provide context, but they don’t replace your own needs.

The Pearson study cannot tell us how common this difficulty is among autistic adults generally. Participants volunteered accounts of experiences involving mistreatment. People without those experiences may describe something very different.

When you’ve learned to agree to avoid conflict

In another study, Gibbs and Pellicano interviewed 22 autistic adults in Australia who had experienced interpersonal violence.

Some participants described being repeatedly told that their perceptions were wrong or that they needed to change themselves to fit in. They connected those experiences to second-guessing their judgment and going along with other people to avoid conflict (Gibbs & Pellicano, 2023).

The researchers reported participants’ explanations of their own experiences. The study did not establish that particular childhood experiences or compliance-focused teaching caused later victimization.

Still, the accounts identify a difficulty worth recognizing.

If disagreement has often brought punishment, criticism, or rejection, agreeing may feel easier than expressing what you want.

That habit can continue even when you have more freedom to make your own decisions.

You might find yourself saying yes before considering whether you actually want to.

Or you might believe that somebody else’s disappointment proves you have behaved badly.

Neither feeling is reliable evidence that your limit is unreasonable.

Someone can be disappointed by your decision without your decision being wrong.

That applies in reverse, too. You can be disappointed by someone else’s boundary without their decision being wrong.

When nobody explained that you could refuse

Bargiela, Steward, and Mandy interviewed 14 late-diagnosed autistic women in the UK, aged 22 to 30.

Some participants described difficulty recognizing when they could refuse unwanted sexual or romantic advances. Others understood that refusal was possible but didn’t know how to act on their discomfort in the moment (Bargiela et al., 2016).

These were experiences from a small, selected group of women, not a description of autistic women generally.

They nevertheless raise an important question for anyone who missed explicit lessons about relationships:

Have you been taught what you’re allowed to decline, or have you mostly been taught what other people expect?

There is a difference.

Knowing how to make someone happy is not the same skill as knowing when you don’t want to participate.

If you recognize yourself in these accounts, you don’t need to decide whether autism, ADHD, past experiences, or something else explains the difficulty before taking your own discomfort seriously.

And if someone has mistreated you, responsibility for that behavior belongs to the person who chose it. Your difficulty recognizing or expressing a boundary does not excuse their actions.

Being different does not mean having weaker boundaries

It’s important not to turn the preceding studies into a stereotype.

Autistic people aren’t universally unable to recognize danger or express limits. People with ADHD aren’t universally impulsive about relationships. People with difficult childhoods aren’t destined to accept mistreatment.

The evidence is more varied.

A systematic review by Gibbs and colleagues examined 16 studies of interpersonal violence reported by autistic adults. Estimates differed substantially across studies, partly because researchers used different definitions, measurements, and populations.

Several studies found higher reported victimization in autistic groups on at least one measure, while two comparisons found no statistically significant difference on their respective measures. The researchers also emphasized that evidence about possible causes and risk factors remained limited (Gibbs et al., 2024).

That means no single risk figure describes every autistic person, and the review cannot establish that difficulty setting boundaries causes victimization.

Two studies included in the review found associations between co-occurring ADHD and reported victimization. That is not evidence that ADHD causes poor boundaries or that forgetting to answer a message makes somebody incapable of respecting one.

The research also leaves many people underrepresented, including autistic adults with intellectual disabilities, people from lower-income backgrounds, and people outside predominantly Western research settings (Gibbs et al., 2024).

Meanwhile, participants in Ciric and colleagues’ study described actively establishing boundaries, challenging conventional relationship assumptions, and seeking partners who respected their preferences (Ciric et al., 2026).

These experiences can coexist.

Some people struggle to identify limits. Others are quite confident about theirs. The same person may be confident in one situation and uncertain in another.

What matters for this book is not which label describes you.

It’s whether you can recognize what you need, express it when appropriate, and allow other people the same freedom.

If agreeing has always felt safer than refusing

If disagreement used to bring consequences, going along with things may have stopped feeling like a choice. You may notice you’ve agreed before you’ve decided anything.

Buy yourself the gap: “Let me think about that and tell you tonight.” You don’t owe a reason, and a delay is not a refusal. Practice it on small things, where being wrong costs nothing, until it’s available on large ones.

Agreeing quickly kept you safe once. That doesn’t make it a character flaw, and it doesn’t mean it still serves you with someone who would have accepted a no.

Boundaries work in both directions

Suppose Marcus, 38, enjoys long phone conversations. He’s dating Daniel, 35, who finds extended calls exhausting after work.

Daniel explains that he prefers shorter calls during the week and longer conversations on weekends.

Marcus feels hurt at first. He had interpreted long conversations as a sign of growing closeness.

Daniel hadn’t intended to suggest that he cared less.

Neither person’s initial interpretation tells the whole story.

Marcus can explain why the calls matter to him. Daniel can explain why he needs a different arrangement. They can see whether there’s enough room for both needs.

Perhaps Marcus enjoys connecting through messages during the week, while Daniel is happy to plan a longer Sunday call.

Perhaps they discover that their preferences are too different.

Either outcome is possible.

The important point is that Daniel doesn’t need to pretend he’s comfortable, and Marcus doesn’t need to pretend his wish for frequent contact has disappeared.

Understanding is not the same as agreeing

You can understand why someone wants something and still decide you cannot provide it.

A partner may need frequent reassurance. You can recognize that need without promising to answer immediately every time they feel anxious.

You may need quiet after a busy day. Your partner can respect that without personally enjoying quiet evenings.

Understanding another person’s limits doesn’t require giving up your own.

It also doesn’t require guessing correctly every time.

The earlier chapters on communication introduced asking questions, explaining what you mean, and repairing misunderstandings. Those skills become particularly useful when a conversation concerns something important to either person.

For some people, directly naming expectations feels more natural than relying on hints. In Ciric and colleagues’ study, several autistic participants described appreciating clear, individually negotiated relationship expectations, including in relationships with people whose brains worked differently (Ciric et al., 2026).

That does not mean all autistic people communicate alike, or that non-autistic partners must become experts in interpreting another person’s behavior.

Both people can participate in making things understandable.

If your partner takes time to answer a personal question, you can allow that time. If your partner asks you to explain an expectation directly, you can explain it rather than treating the question as a challenge.

And if you need something that your partner genuinely cannot provide, that remains an issue to discuss. Neither person’s neurotype automatically decides whose needs matter more.

If you're dating someone who agrees to everything

Instant agreement isn’t always enthusiasm. Someone who learned that refusing was unsafe may say yes long before they’ve worked out what they want.

Make the no easy and visibly cost-free. Offer it yourself — “it’s completely fine if you’d rather not” — and when it’s taken, respond well. One well-received no teaches more than any amount of reassurance.

Avoid stacking questions or pressing for a decision in the moment. “Tell me tomorrow” gives them room to find an answer that’s actually theirs.

A boundary isn’t a punishment

Imagine that Renee, 42, and her girlfriend have an argument.

Renee says she’s too upset to continue talking and needs some time alone.

That can be a boundary. She is deciding that she cannot participate constructively in the conversation right now.

But what happens next matters.

Taking time alone doesn’t erase the original disagreement or mean the other person’s concerns no longer deserve attention.

Renee can still acknowledge that the problem exists and, when appropriate and safe, return to the discussion.

A boundary should not be used as a weapon to punish somebody for disagreeing.

Similarly, declining an activity is different from deliberately making somebody suffer because they didn’t give you what you wanted.

Professional guidance cautions against using therapeutic vocabulary to justify mistreating or controlling others (Cleveland Clinic, 2025).

The phrase “That’s my boundary” doesn’t settle every argument.

You are responsible for the limits you choose and for how you act on them. Your partner is responsible for their own choices and conduct.

Neither person’s feelings automatically cancel the other’s freedom.

You can reconsider a boundary without giving it up under pressure

Your boundaries may change.

You might be uncomfortable sharing personal stories on a first date but happy to discuss them after several months.

You might prefer one evening alone each week, then temporarily need more time because your circumstances change.

You might discover that a situation you expected to enjoy actually makes you uncomfortable.

Changing your mind is allowed.

But deciding freely to reconsider something is different from feeling worn down until you finally agree.

You don’t owe another person permanent access to something just because you were comfortable with it before.

Likewise, changing a boundary doesn’t automatically mean you were wrong to have it.

A limit is information about what you can willingly accept. You can learn more about yourself without treating your earlier needs as mistakes.

A boundary isn’t the same as consent

Boundaries and consent are connected, but they aren’t identical.

A boundary describes a limit on what you will do or accept.

Consent means freely agreeing to a specific activity. It must be ongoing and can be withdrawn.

For example, someone may generally be comfortable with kissing. That does not mean they have agreed to a kiss at this moment.

Someone may agree to a particular activity on one occasion and not want it the next time.

Someone may have expressed a broad boundary but still need to be asked about a particular activity.

RAINN, a US sexual-assault prevention organization, emphasizes that consent is specific, voluntary, and ongoing. Silence and lack of resistance are not permission, and agreeing in the past does not establish agreement now (RAINN, 2026).

This distinction matters beyond sexual situations.

If somebody has invited you to visit their apartment before, you still shouldn’t assume you’re welcome to arrive without asking.

If your partner enjoys discussing personal matters, that doesn’t mean every subject is available for conversation whenever you choose.

When you’re uncertain whether someone wants to participate, ask or wait.

A boundary is not a substitute for getting consent.

Later chapters will cover consent, touch, and sexual safety in much greater detail.

For now, remember that another person’s willingness cannot be assumed from your own comfort, from a previous experience, or from being in a relationship.

When someone’s boundary disappoints you

Sometimes you’ll be the person who wants more.

You might want another date when someone doesn’t. You might want to spend the night together when your partner prefers to go home. You might want a longer conversation when the other person has reached their limit.

You may feel sad, frustrated, embarrassed, or rejected.

Those feelings deserve attention, but they don’t give you permission to override someone else’s decision.

You can be disappointed without turning the disappointment into pressure.

For example, suppose the person you’re dating tells you they need Saturday afternoon alone.

You were hoping to spend the entire weekend together.

You could ask whether they’d like to make plans for another day. You could also decide whether the amount of time they’re available is enough for the relationship you want.

What you cannot reasonably expect is for their free time to become yours simply because you were looking forward to it.

The same principle protects you when someone wants more than you can offer.

Nobody is entitled to unlimited access to a partner’s time, attention, personal information, body, or relationships.

Later, when the book covers respecting other people’s boundaries, you’ll practice what to do when you hear no, not yet, or no answer at all.

When a boundary isn’t respected

Not every misunderstanding is evidence of abuse.

Some people need an expectation explained because it wasn’t obvious to them. Others may forget an arrangement or misunderstand what was agreed.

If the situation is safe, clarification may be enough.

Suppose you told someone you don’t usually answer calls after ten, but they call at 10:15.

They may not understand whether you meant you won’t answer, whether you were making a request, or whether something had changed.

You can clarify, and they can adjust their expectations.

But a misunderstanding is different from repeatedly disregarding someone’s choices, intimidating them, or pressuring them to participate against their wishes.

The CDC includes psychological aggression intended to harm or exert control within its description of intimate partner violence. The Department of Justice also recognizes patterns of nonphysical abusive behavior, including isolation and intimidation (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026; U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

You don’t need to decide whether conduct meets a particular legal definition before taking a safety concern seriously. Laws and available remedies vary by state, and this book is not a substitute for legal advice.

You do not need to keep explaining yourself to someone who is unsafe

Ordinary relationship disagreements may benefit from more communication.

Threatening, intimidating, or abusive behavior is different.

If someone repeatedly disregards your safety, another carefully worded explanation may not help. You are not required to confront them again to prove that your boundary is real.

If you’re afraid of someone, consider reaching out privately to a trusted person or a qualified domestic violence advocate for help thinking through your options.

Your safety matters more than making sure the other person agrees that your decision is reasonable.

And if you recognize that you’ve been intimidating, monitoring, or pressuring a partner, stop that behavior. Take responsibility rather than describing the other person’s refusal as unfair. Seek qualified help to change the behavior without placing responsibility for that change on the person being harmed.

A boundary cannot guarantee that another person will behave safely.

If someone chooses to violate your limits, that is not proof that you failed to explain them correctly.

Detailed information about harassment, abuse, and finding support appears in later chapters.

A small way to start

You don’t have to begin with the most difficult conversation in your life.

Start with an ordinary situation where you have a choice.

Imagine three invitations.

Situation one: Someone asks you to join a video call tonight. You’re tired, and you want to spend the evening alone.

Situation two: The person you’re dating wants to spend every Saturday together. You enjoy seeing them, but you also want time for friends.

Situation three: A date asks a personal question that you aren’t ready to answer.

For each situation, consider these questions:

  1. What would you actually prefer, before thinking about whether somebody might be disappointed?
  2. Is there something you want to ask the other person to change, or a limit on your own participation?
  3. What would you choose if the other person couldn’t offer the arrangement you wanted?

You don’t have to produce the perfect sentence.

For the first situation, the answer might simply be:

“Not tonight. I need a quiet evening.”

That’s enough.

For the second, you may need a conversation about how much time each person wants to spend together.

For the third, you might say:

“I’m not ready to talk about that.”

You don’t owe someone an extensive explanation of a private experience just because they asked.

If you find yourself automatically saying yes to things you don’t want, try giving yourself time to think before answering, when the situation allows it.

You can begin with a small question:

“Do I actually want to do this?”

Then consider what you are willing to offer.

The purpose isn’t to become rigid or to refuse anything inconvenient. Relationships require generosity, patience, flexibility, and consideration.

The purpose is to make those choices willingly rather than feeling that a relationship requires you to abandon your own judgment.

What boundaries can and cannot do

Return to the person whose phone keeps ringing every evening.

They eventually explain that they enjoy talking but need quiet evenings during the week.

The person they’re dating is surprised. They hadn’t realized the calls were becoming exhausting.

Maybe they find a new schedule. Maybe they discover different expectations about closeness. Maybe one person realizes that the arrangement doesn’t meet their needs.

The boundary doesn’t guarantee a particular result.

It gives both people information they didn’t have before.

That is one of the most useful things boundaries can offer: a clearer understanding of what each person is willing and able to bring to a relationship.

They don’t guarantee that someone will like your decision. They don’t make every disagreement disappear. They don’t transform an unsafe person into a safe one.

And they aren’t a way to make another person behave exactly as you wish.

They help you distinguish between what you can choose, what you can ask for, what requires mutual agreement, and what you cannot decide for someone else.

You are allowed to have limits. So is the person you’re dating.

The next question is how to discover what your own limits are, especially when you’ve spent a long time following other people’s expectations.

That’s where the next chapter begins.
