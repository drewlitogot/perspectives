Content note: This chapter discusses emotional abuse, controlling relationships, sexual violence, and situations where someone may feel unsafe with a partner. It does not include graphic descriptions. If you recognize your situation here, you can skip directly to “Getting help without increasing the danger.”

You can be in a relationship where nobody hits you, nobody openly threatens you, and you still don’t feel free.

Perhaps you used to see your friends regularly. Now you rarely do. You still have their numbers, but spending time with them has become difficult.

Perhaps you used to make ordinary decisions without thinking twice. Now you rehearse how your partner might react before buying something, answering a message, or saying you need time alone.

Perhaps you keep trying to explain that something hurt you. Somehow, the conversation ends with you apologizing. You leave less certain of what happened than when you started.

You may love your partner. They may be affectionate, generous, funny, and thoughtful at other times. You may have wonderful memories together.

None of that automatically makes the harmful parts harmless.

A relationship can contain genuine affection and still contain abuse.

The important question is not whether your partner has good qualities. It is whether you can make choices, maintain relationships, express disagreement, and protect your own boundaries without fear of what will happen afterward.

When a relationship begins to take away your choices

Manipulation means influencing someone through deception, emotional pressure, or other unfair means rather than allowing them to make an informed, free choice.

Abuse involves behavior that harms, intimidates, exploits, or controls another person. In relationships, it can be physical, sexual, emotional, economic, psychological, or technological.

The US Department of Justice describes domestic violence as a pattern of abusive behavior used to gain or maintain power and control over a partner. The CDC also recognizes psychological aggression, including communication intended to cause emotional harm or control someone, as a form of intimate partner violence (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026; U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

You may hear the term coercive control. It describes a pattern in which someone increasingly restricts another person’s choices and independence, using pressure, intimidation, restrictions, or other abusive behavior.

Researchers do not share one precise definition or measurement of coercive control. Some include physical violence; others emphasize nonphysical restrictions. But the central concern is recognizable: someone’s freedom is being reduced by another person’s behavior (Hilton et al., 2024).

Consider the difference between these two situations.

Situation A: Taylor enjoys frequent contact with their boyfriend, Marcus. Marcus prefers more time alone. They disagree about how often to see each other, discuss what they each need, and decide whether a compromise works.

Situation B: Taylor is increasingly afraid to spend time alone because Marcus reacts badly whenever they make independent plans. Taylor stops arranging activities they previously enjoyed. Even when Marcus is not present, Taylor makes decisions around avoiding his reaction.

Both situations involve different preferences about time together.

Only the second describes a loss of freedom driven by fear.

The same distinction applies to money, friendships, privacy, clothing, medical decisions, sex, and countless ordinary choices.

Having a preference is not automatically controlling. Asking for something is not automatically abusive. Feeling disappointed when a partner says no is not automatically abusive.

The concern grows when saying no, disagreeing, or acting independently brings consequences that make you afraid to exercise those choices again.

Not every unhealthy interaction meets a legal definition of abuse. And one serious incident can be harmful even without a repeated pattern. You do not need to decide which legal category applies before taking your safety seriously.

It is more common than many people realize

According to the CDC’s August 2026 overview, more than one in three US women and more than one in six US men have experienced contact sexual violence, physical violence, and/or stalking by an intimate partner during their lifetimes (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026).

These figures combine several forms of violence. They are not estimates of coercive control alone. They also do not provide a separate prevalence estimate for non-binary people.

An intimate partner can be someone you are dating, living with, married to, separated from, or previously involved with. Abuse is not limited to marriage or long relationships (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026).

The consequences can be significant even when there are no visible injuries.

A systematic review and meta-analysis covering 68 studies found that coercive control was moderately associated with post-traumatic stress disorder and depression. The results cannot establish that coercive control caused those outcomes in every case, but they show that controlling abuse is associated with substantial psychological harm (Lohmann et al., 2024).

You do not need to develop a mental health condition to have a legitimate reason to seek help.

You also do not need to prove that your experience is worse than someone else’s.

Love bombing: when affection moves faster than your freedom

Imagine that you meet someone and immediately enjoy their company.

They send thoughtful messages. They remember details from your conversations. They want to spend time together. You feel wanted, perhaps for the first time in years.

None of this is automatically suspicious.

Some people fall in love quickly. Some enjoy frequent communication. Some are naturally expressive, and some autistic people communicate intense enthusiasm very directly.

Intensity is not the same thing as abuse.

The popular term love bombing usually refers to intense attention, affection, or generosity early in a relationship, particularly when it creates pressure for rapid commitment.

The research behind this term is much weaker than its popularity suggests. One 2017 study of university students examined excessive early communication and its associations with personality and relationship measures. It did not establish that intense early affection reliably predicts later abuse (Strutzenberg et al., 2017).

That distinction matters. You cannot diagnose someone’s intentions by counting messages, measuring enthusiasm, or noticing that they want to see you often.

What matters is whether the affection leaves room for your choices.

Suppose Priya, 29, is dating Elena, 33. Both women are excited about the relationship. Elena wants to spend nearly every evening together, while Priya also wants to maintain her weekly activities with friends.

If Elena accepts that preference, the two can decide whether their desired levels of closeness are compatible.

If Priya increasingly feels that maintaining her friendships will cost her affection, security, or peace, something more concerning may be happening.

The issue is not how strongly Elena feels.

It is whether Priya remains free to choose.

A useful question is:

“Can this relationship move at a pace that also feels right for me?”

You should not have to accept a level of commitment you do not want in order to keep someone’s kindness.

Nor do you need to deliberately refuse something to see how a partner reacts. If you already feel unsafe saying no, that feeling deserves attention without creating another potentially dangerous situation.

Isolation: when the rest of your life starts disappearing

A relationship can take up a lot of time, especially at the beginning.

People sometimes spend less time with friends during a new romance because they are excited about being together.

Isolation is different.

Isolation becomes concerning when your ability to maintain other relationships is restricted or made emotionally costly.

You might notice that:

  • You rarely see people you previously trusted, and the change is connected to your partner’s reactions.
  • You feel anxious about answering calls or messages from family and friends.
  • Your partner’s approval seems necessary for ordinary social plans.
  • You no longer have someone outside the relationship with whom you can speak freely.
  • You have stopped pursuing activities important to you because doing so creates conflict or fear.

The Department of Justice includes isolation and restrictions on a person’s independence among patterns of abusive control (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

For someone who already has a small social circle, these changes can be especially difficult to recognize.

Perhaps your partner is your closest friend. Perhaps meeting new people is exhausting, or friendships have been difficult to maintain. Perhaps this is your first serious relationship, and the attention feels extraordinary.

Having one especially important relationship is not itself a problem.

The concern is whether that relationship leaves you free to have others.

In qualitative research involving autistic adults who had experienced abuse, participants described isolation, difficulty recognizing abusive patterns, and circumstances in which their partner became an important source of support while also causing harm (Douglas & Sedgewick, 2024; Gibbs & Pellicano, 2023).

That is not a reason to avoid closeness.

It is a reason to remember that being loved should not require losing access to everyone else.

When someone keeps undermining your sense of reality

Two people can remember the same conversation differently.

The sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of this book is useful here. Different positions and experiences can produce genuinely different interpretations.

In a healthy disagreement, both people remain entitled to describe their experiences.

A different situation arises when one person consistently treats the other’s memory, feelings, or judgment as untrustworthy.

You might begin to notice that your recollection of events is repeatedly dismissed. Your concerns are treated as proof that you are unreasonable. When something hurts you, the conversation leaves you doubting whether you are entitled to feel hurt at all.

Eventually, you may start asking your partner to tell you what you think or feel.

This kind of patterned undermining is often called gaslighting.

Sociologist Paige Sweet examined gaslighting as a form of reality-undermining connected to power and social inequality. The concept is more specific than ordinary disagreement, misunderstanding, or someone remembering something incorrectly (Sweet, 2019).

Not every lie is gaslighting. Not every correction is gaslighting. And two people genuinely disagreeing about an event does not establish that either is abusing the other.

The question is whether there is a continuing pattern in which one person’s account dominates and the other loses confidence in their ability to understand their own experiences.

For example, imagine Devon, 41, tells his partner that a recent interaction made him uncomfortable.

His partner may reasonably remember the interaction differently. Both could ask questions, compare what they remember, and acknowledge the other’s feelings.

But if Devon’s concerns are routinely dismissed and he increasingly believes he cannot trust himself about anything, the pattern deserves attention.

You do not need a perfect transcript of every conversation to recognize that a relationship is making you afraid to rely on your own judgment.

When your brain differences become a reason to dismiss you

Some readers have spent years hearing that they are too literal, too sensitive, too emotional, or too difficult to understand.

Others have been encouraged to suppress their natural responses through masking, or to accept other people’s interpretations of social situations without question.

Those experiences can make it harder to trust your judgment when something feels wrong.

In a UK qualitative study of 43 autistic adults, participants described uncertainty about boundaries, compliance with expectations, and difficulty knowing whether troubling treatment in friendships and other relationships was acceptable (Pearson et al., 2022).

Another study interviewed 24 autistic survivors of interpersonal abuse, including men, women, and non-binary people. Some described having their autism used against them to discredit their experiences, as well as abusive behavior involving isolation and deliberately provoked distress (Douglas & Sedgewick, 2024).

These are survivors’ accounts, not a claim that every autistic person experiences the same pattern.

They also illustrate an important distinction.

A difference in how you communicate is not permission for someone else to control you.

If you need extra time to answer, you are entitled to that time.

If you find indirect language confusing, you can ask for direct communication.

If you experience overload in a loud environment, you can ask to leave or take a break.

If you enter shutdown and cannot speak easily, that does not mean another person can decide your wishes for you.

And if you misunderstand something, that does not mean your preferences cease to matter.

If you tend to take people at their word

You may expect that other people mean what they say because you make an effort to mean what you say. When someone’s words and actions conflict, you may keep searching for an innocent explanation.

You can take the contradiction seriously without deciding immediately whether the person intended to deceive you. If you feel safe doing so, ask someone outside the relationship for an independent perspective. You can describe what happened in concrete terms instead of trying to identify someone’s hidden motives.

Being straightforward is not a flaw that needs correcting. The responsibility for dishonest or abusive behavior belongs to the person choosing it.

Why the research on autistic victimization needs care

Several studies report higher rates of victimization among autistic people, but the findings are not uniform.

A systematic review of 16 studies of autistic adults found that victimization rates varied widely. Some comparisons suggested greater risk than in the general population, while others did not find statistically significant differences (Gibbs et al., 2024).

Another review combined 34 studies involving children and adults and estimated a pooled victimization prevalence of 44%. However, the studies differed enormously in what they measured, including bullying, sexual violence, and other experiences. The statistical variation was so large that this percentage should not be treated as the rate of partner abuse among autistic adults (Trundle et al., 2023).

A smaller comparison study of 45 autistic and 42 non-autistic adults found differences in some victimization experiences but not in every adult victimization measure. The researchers’ measures of social-communication ability and emotion regulation did not explain the observed differences (Weiss & Fardella, 2018).

These limits are important.

It would be misleading to say that autistic people are abused simply because they take words literally or have difficulty interpreting intentions.

The research also points to social exclusion, invalidation, inaccessible support, and other people’s harmful actions (Douglas & Sedgewick, 2024; Gibbs & Pellicano, 2023; Pearson et al., 2022).

An autistic person can have excellent boundaries and still encounter an abusive partner.

An autistic person can recognize abuse immediately, years later, or somewhere in between.

And an autistic person can also behave abusively. Neurotype does not determine character or remove responsibility.

The goal is not to teach autistic people to distrust everyone. It is to help everyone recognize when a relationship is taking away their choices.

When helping becomes deciding for you

Sometimes one partner genuinely needs more practical support than the other.

Perhaps one person finds managing bills difficult. Perhaps the other handles scheduling, driving, or complicated phone calls.

Support can be a welcome part of a relationship.

But there is a difference between receiving help and surrendering control.

Consider two autistic partners, Sam and Jules.

Sam finds appointment scheduling exhausting. Jules enjoys organizing calendars and offers to help. Sam decides which appointments to make, and Jules handles some of the calls.

This arrangement works because Sam retains the ability to make decisions and change the arrangement.

Now imagine that Jules begins making decisions Sam has not agreed to, refuses to share important information, or treats Sam’s need for assistance as proof that Sam cannot make personal choices.

The same arrangement would no longer be freely shared support.

It would be restricting Sam’s independence.

A person’s disability, diagnosis, communication differences, or need for assistance does not automatically authorize a romantic partner to take control of their life.

If you're dating someone who needs help with decisions or communication

Ask what assistance your partner actually wants. Some people appreciate written choices, extra time to respond, help with appointments, or support during unfamiliar situations. Others prefer to handle those tasks themselves.

Do not mistake needing support with one task for lacking the ability to decide about other parts of life. A person may need help making a phone call and still be perfectly capable of deciding whom to date, where to live, or how to spend their money.

Useful support makes choices more accessible. It does not quietly transfer ownership of those choices to you.

Money, privacy, and dependence

Control is not always expressed through arguments.

Sometimes you notice it in the practical details of daily life.

You may be unable to access money you need. You may feel that seeking work or continuing education is no longer an option. You may be expected to surrender privacy that you previously had.

You may have difficulty making personal decisions because another person controls information, resources, or access to ordinary activities.

Economic and technological abuse are among the forms of domestic violence recognized in US Department of Justice guidance (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

Shared finances are not inherently abusive. Neither is sharing a phone, a password by choice, or deciding together that one partner will earn income while another handles responsibilities at home.

What matters is whether the arrangement is genuinely agreed upon and whether each person’s autonomy and safety are respected.

A relationship does not become controlling simply because partners divide responsibilities unequally.

It becomes concerning when dependence is used to restrict choices or when someone cannot safely question or change the arrangement.

The distinction matters especially when disability, caregiving, housing, or financial hardship makes one partner more dependent on the other.

Depending on someone is not consent to be controlled.

Different experiences, the same right to safety

Abuse can happen to people of every gender and sexual orientation, including in relationships between two men, two women, two non-binary people, or partners with different gender identities.

It also happens in relationships between two neurodivergent people, two neurotypical people, and partners whose brains work differently.

No combination of identities guarantees safety or danger.

But different social experiences can affect whether abuse is recognized and what support is available.

Perspective: Men

If you are a man, you may have encountered the expectation that you should always be able to protect yourself or manage relationship problems without outside help.

That expectation does not determine whether an experience is abusive. The CDC’s national data include substantial numbers of male survivors of intimate partner violence (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026).

You can seek support even if the person harming you is physically smaller, even if you have never been physically attacked, or even if you feel embarrassed about what happened. You do not have to prove physical helplessness to deserve help.

Perspective: Women

The CDC reports a higher lifetime prevalence of the specified combined forms of intimate partner violence among women than men in its national summary (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026).

That difference does not mean a harmful relationship is inevitable, or that preventing abuse is your personal responsibility. Being cautious cannot guarantee another person’s behavior.

If you have experienced abuse, the responsibility rests with the person who chose to harm you. You are entitled to support regardless of whether you noticed warning signs early, stayed in the relationship, or returned to it.

Perspective: Non-binary

You may not see your experiences reflected in advice that assumes every relationship involves a man and a woman. A review of coercive control in sexual and gender minority relationships found important gaps in research, particularly for non-binary, asexual, pansexual, intersex, and other underrepresented groups (Hilton et al., 2024).

Abuse does not become less serious because available statistics fail to describe your identity accurately. If someone uses your identity, relationships, or access to support to restrict your choices, those experiences deserve attention.

You can ask a support service whether it can provide care that respects your name, pronouns, identity, and relationship circumstances.

If attention, memory, or past experiences make the pattern difficult to see

Sometimes you recognize that a relationship feels wrong but cannot explain exactly why.

Perhaps conversations move too quickly. Perhaps you lose track of details during conflict. Perhaps you need several hours to understand how an interaction affected you.

These experiences do not establish that abuse is occurring. But they also do not make your concerns less legitimate.

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis found associations between ADHD and experiences of intimate partner violence and sexual violence. The studies were observational and varied considerably in method and population. They do not establish that ADHD causes victimization or determine what will happen to an individual (Arrondo et al., 2023).

If you have trouble remembering what happened during conflict

You might remember the emotional effect of a conversation more clearly than its exact sequence. That can make it difficult to explain your concerns afterward.

You do not have to reconstruct every sentence to ask for support. If it is safe, you can explain what you remember and what you are uncertain about. A trusted person or professional can help you think through the situation without demanding perfect recall.

Do not use a disagreement about memory as proof that you or your partner must be lying. Look at the wider pattern of how disagreements are handled.

Past trauma can also make some relationship experiences especially difficult.

You may notice that you become anxious when someone is displeased, or that you feel compelled to resolve a conflict immediately. You may have trouble recognizing what treatment you want to accept.

The research on coercive control and mental health does not establish that any particular childhood experience causes someone to enter or remain in an abusive relationship (Lohmann et al., 2024).

You do not need to find an explanation in your childhood before responding to an unsafe situation.

And you do not have to earn the right to leave by understanding exactly why the relationship developed as it did.

Is this abuse, or are you misunderstanding each other?

Not every painful relationship problem is manipulation.

People can be inconsiderate, distracted, defensive, inexperienced, or simply incompatible without having an abusive pattern.

Two partners might disagree about spending, time alone, sexual frequency, or how often to visit relatives.

One person may need direct language while another communicates indirectly.

A person may withdraw during overload or shutdown without intending to punish anyone.

These situations can require substantial communication and adjustment. The double empathy problem reminds you that misunderstandings between different ways of experiencing the world can go in both directions.

But different perspectives do not make every interpretation of harmful behavior equally acceptable.

If one person is frightened to disagree, cannot maintain independent relationships, or has repeatedly lost control over ordinary choices, treating the problem as merely a communication mismatch can miss something important.

Consider what happens when someone makes a mistake.

In a healthy relationship, the person can acknowledge the mistake, listen to its effects, and make a meaningful effort to change. The other person is not required to forgive immediately, but both remain free to express themselves.

In a controlling relationship, the problem may not be one mistake. It may be that your freedom continues to shrink regardless of how carefully you explain your needs.

The distinction is not whether you can produce a perfect explanation.

It is whether there is room for your needs and choices at all.

Three questions worth asking yourself

This is a reflection exercise, not a validated screening test. Use experiences that have already occurred. Do not deliberately provoke a confrontation to test your partner.

  1. What happens when you disagree? Can you express a different preference without being afraid of the consequences?
  2. What parts of your life remain yours? Can you maintain friendships, interests, privacy, and reasonable independence?
  3. What has changed since the relationship began? Do you have more freedom to be yourself, or have you increasingly changed your behavior to avoid another person’s reactions?

You may have clear answers. You may have conflicting answers. You may need to talk with someone else to understand what you are noticing.

You do not need to decide immediately whether your partner is an abuser.

You can begin by recognizing that something deserves attention.

When someone you care about may be experiencing abuse

Suppose your friend Luis has been dating his boyfriend for a year.

You notice that Luis rarely comes to gatherings anymore. When you speak privately, he seems anxious and says that things at home have become complicated.

It might be tempting to tell him exactly what to do.

But someone experiencing control may already have too little freedom to make decisions. Another person taking over, even with good intentions, may not be helpful.

Instead, offer attention without demanding a conclusion.

You might say:

“I’ve noticed you seem less comfortable lately. Is everything okay?”

Or:

“You don’t have to explain anything you’re not ready to explain. If you’d like to talk, I’m available.”

If Luis describes frightening behavior, take it seriously. Avoid arguing with him about why he stayed or insisting that he immediately make a particular decision.

You can offer practical support that he chooses, such as accompanying him to an appointment, helping him contact a domestic violence advocate, or simply remaining available.

Respect his privacy, but do not promise secrecy if you cannot safely keep that promise, particularly when someone faces immediate danger.

A helpful friend does not need to investigate, confront the partner, or prove that abuse occurred.

Maintaining a trustworthy connection can be valuable.

Getting help without increasing the danger

If you suspect your partner is controlling or abusing you, you do not have to confront them, announce that you are leaving, or gather proof before asking for help.

Some relationships involve risks that are difficult to evaluate from the outside. What is safe for one person may be dangerous for another.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline recommends individualized safety planning rather than assuming one universal response will work (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

A safety plan is a practical set of choices based on your circumstances, including how to access support and respond if danger increases.

An advocate can help you think through options without requiring you to make every decision immediately.

If you are in immediate danger, call 911 if you can do so safely.

For confidential support and safety planning in the United States:

National Domestic Violence Hotline

  • Call: 800-799-SAFE (7233)
  • Website: https://www.thehotline.org/
  • Safety planning: https://www.thehotline.org/plan-for-safety/

The Hotline provides 24/7 support and information about local resources (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

If your partner monitors your phone, messages, or internet activity, contacting services from your usual device may not be private. Consider asking an advocate or trusted person about safer ways to communicate. Do not assume that deleting browsing history guarantees privacy.

You can contact a domestic violence service even if you have not experienced physical violence, do not know whether your situation meets a legal definition, or are not ready to leave.

You can also ask about accessible communication. For example:

“I need information in writing so I can think about it.”

Or:

“I need a little extra time to answer questions.”

Or:

“I’m not ready to decide what to do. Can you explain my options?”

If an advocate cannot meet your communication needs, you can ask whether another worker or service is available.

Support should help you make informed choices, not make those choices for you.

US laws concerning domestic violence, protective orders, and coercive behavior vary by state. An advocate or qualified local legal professional can explain which options are relevant to your situation (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

Chapters 59 and 60 cover additional therapy, support, and crisis resources.

If you recognize yourself controlling someone

It is also possible to read this chapter and recognize your own behavior.

Perhaps you have repeatedly interfered with a partner’s friendships. Perhaps you have monitored their private communication, restricted their independence, or made them afraid to disagree.

Perhaps you believed you were acting out of love, anxiety, jealousy, or fear of abandonment.

Those feelings may help explain why you acted as you did. They do not justify controlling another person.

The first responsibility is to stop the harmful behavior.

Do not treat your partner’s distress as a problem they must solve for you. Do not demand reassurance, forgiveness, explanations, or another chance.

Respect their boundaries, including requests for distance or no contact. Do not contact them indirectly or use another person to pressure them.

Take responsibility without requiring the person you harmed to comfort or educate you.

If you have trouble understanding why a behavior was harmful, seek help from a qualified mental health professional who has experience addressing controlling or abusive behavior.

Be clear about the actions you took rather than describing only your feelings or intentions.

For example, recognizing that you felt afraid of losing someone may be relevant to understanding yourself. But the important question remains whether you restricted that person’s freedom and what you must change to prevent that from happening again.

If you are autistic, ADHD, or have experienced trauma, you may need support tailored to how you process emotions, uncertainty, or rejection. That support can help you take responsibility. It does not remove the responsibility.

An apology cannot undo a pattern of control by itself.

And changing your behavior does not create an obligation for someone to continue the relationship.

When abuse is present, ordinary couples communication exercises or couples counseling should not be assumed to be a safe substitute for specialized individual help.

The goal is not to persuade someone that you are safe. It is to stop behaving in ways that take away their safety.

You can love someone and still need distance

One of the hardest things about an abusive relationship is that your feelings may not line up neatly.

You might feel frightened and affectionate toward the same person.

You might remember wonderful experiences and still recognize that the relationship has harmed you.

You might miss someone after leaving. You might want to stay because the relationship matters deeply to you. You might not know what you want.

There is no requirement to stop loving someone before acknowledging harmful behavior.

There is also no requirement to prove that the other person intended to hurt you before setting a boundary or seeking help.

You can take your experiences seriously even while you are uncertain about what they mean.

And you can make decisions in stages, with support, rather than demanding an immediate answer from yourself.

Healthy relationships are not free from disagreement, frustration, or mistakes.

They are relationships in which people remain separate individuals who can express needs, retain choices, and say no.

The next chapter, “Green flags and red flags,” looks more closely at the everyday behaviors that help you recognize healthy and unhealthy patterns before becoming deeply involved.

The important distinction to carry forward is simple:

Affection is not a substitute for respect. Being needed is not the same as being loved. And being in a relationship does not mean giving up your right to choose.
