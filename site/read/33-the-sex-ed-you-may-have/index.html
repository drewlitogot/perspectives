You can be 35 years old, have a career, pay taxes, and still be unsure exactly where the clitoris is.

You might know that condoms prevent pregnancy but not realize they also reduce the risk of certain infections. You might have heard about sexually transmitted infections without knowing how testing works. You might understand consent perfectly well but have little idea what happens during sex.

Or perhaps you learned about reproduction in school, but nobody explained pleasure, sexual differences, or how to ask a doctor a question that makes you uncomfortable.

There is no deadline for learning this.

In one Canadian study, 95 autistic adults reported less sexual knowledge than 117 comparison adults and had learned about sexuality from different sources. The researchers also found an association between lower sexual knowledge and reported sexual victimization. Their study could not establish that missing information caused victimization, or that education alone would prevent it (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

A separate US study interviewed 24 autistic adults, ages 18 to 61. Participants described gaps in their sex education, confusion about social expectations, and sensory experiences that ordinary lessons had not addressed. Their experiences varied considerably (Barnett & Maticka-Tyndale, 2015).

Neither study speaks for every autistic person, and neither suggests that people who lack information are responsible for others mistreating them. They do offer a reason to take adult sex education seriously.

You deserve accurate information whether you have been sexually active for decades, have never had sex, or never intend to.

Start with the body, not the assumptions

Sexual health information is often divided into “male anatomy” and “female anatomy.” Those categories can be useful for introducing reproductive systems, but they do not describe every person’s body.

Someone may be transgender, nonbinary, intersex, have had surgery, or have reproductive organs that function differently from those shown in a standard textbook.

A person’s gender does not tell you everything about their anatomy, what activities they enjoy, or whether pregnancy is possible.

For practical purposes, it helps to learn the actual names of body parts.

The vulva, vagina and internal reproductive organs

The vulva is the external genital area. It includes the inner and outer labia, the clitoris, and the openings to the urethra and vagina.

The vagina is the internal muscular canal leading from the vaginal opening to the cervix.

These are not interchangeable terms. Calling the entire external genital area the vagina is a common mistake (Cleveland Clinic, 2022).

Here are the main parts and what they do:

  • Labia: Folds of tissue around the openings of the vulva. Their appearance varies.
  • Clitoris: An organ with sensitive tissue involved in sexual pleasure. Part of it is visible externally, while other portions extend inside the body.
  • Urethra: The tube that carries urine out of the bladder. Its external opening is separate from the vaginal opening.
  • Vagina: A muscular canal through which menstrual blood leaves the body. Vaginal penetration can involve fingers, toys or a penis.
  • Cervix: The lower part of the uterus, located at the upper end of the vagina.
  • Uterus: The organ in which a pregnancy can develop.
  • Ovaries: Organs that produce eggs and reproductive hormones.
  • Fallopian tubes: Pathways through which eggs travel from the ovaries toward the uterus. Fertilization usually occurs here.

These structures work together in reproduction, but reproduction is not their only function (Cleveland Clinic, 2022).

The menstrual cycle is a recurring process involving hormones, egg development, and changes to the uterine lining. Menstruation, commonly called a period, is the shedding of that lining when pregnancy has not occurred in that cycle.

Ovulation is when an ovary releases an egg.

The timing of these events varies. Menstrual cycles are not all exactly 28 days, and their timing alone is not a dependable way to guess whether pregnancy is possible (Cleveland Clinic, 2022; Planned Parenthood Federation of America, n.d.).

The penis, testes and semen-producing organs

The penis contains tissue that can fill with blood and become firm. This is called an erection.

The urethra runs through the penis. It carries urine and can also carry semen out of the body.

The testes, also called testicles, produce sperm and hormones. They are usually held in a skin pouch called the scrotum.

Sperm travel through reproductive ducts. Other organs, including the prostate and seminal vesicles, contribute fluids that combine with sperm to form semen (Cleveland Clinic, 2023a).

A few distinctions matter:

  • Sperm are reproductive cells.
  • Semen is the fluid that may contain sperm.
  • Ejaculation is the release of semen from the penis.
  • Erection is the physical firmness of the penis.
  • Orgasm is an experience of sexual climax.

These are related processes, not different names for the same event.

An erection does not necessarily mean someone wants sex. Ejaculation and orgasm are not identical experiences, and the timing of physical responses varies (Cleveland Clinic, 2023b).

The anus and rectum

The anus is the external opening through which stool leaves the body. The rectum is the section of the digestive tract immediately inside it.

These structures are separate from the reproductive system.

Anal sexual contact is one possible sexual activity, but not a required part of anyone’s sexual life. Because some infections can be transmitted through anal sex, the type of contact matters when deciding which sexual health tests or protection may be appropriate (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024c, 2026a).

What if your body doesn’t match a diagram?

A diagram is a teaching tool, not a standard your body has to meet.

Bodies differ in appearance and function. Medical history, hormones, age and surgery can also matter.

If you are unsure about a structure or whether it affects your health, a clinician can explain your particular anatomy.

You do not need to know the correct medical terms before making an appointment. Learning those terms is one of the reasons to go.

Perspective: Non-binary

Medical paperwork may describe anatomy using gender categories that do not fit your identity. You can ask a clinician to discuss the organs and tests relevant to your body without making assumptions about your gender or your partners.

CDC screening guidance specifically recommends adapting some recommendations for transgender and gender-diverse patients according to anatomy and sexual exposure (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021).

What does “having sex” actually mean?

Movies often present sex as a predictable sequence: kissing, removing clothes, penetration, orgasm, finished.

That is a story convention, not a requirement.

Sexual activity can include kissing, touching another person’s genitals, masturbation, oral sex, vaginal intercourse, anal intercourse, or other mutually chosen forms of contact.

Some people use the word sex primarily to mean intercourse. Others include oral sex or other sexual contact.

That difference can cause genuine confusion.

If a partner or clinician asks whether you have had sex, it is reasonable to ask what kinds of activity they mean.

This is particularly important in health care, because different activities can involve different risks of infection or pregnancy (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024b).

There is no mandatory sequence. Penetration is not the final level of intimacy, and orgasm is not proof that an encounter was successful.

Likewise, a person who has never had intercourse is not automatically less knowledgeable, less capable of intimacy, or less ready for a relationship.

Five things that are easy to confuse

Attraction means finding someone appealing in a romantic or sexual way. You might experience one kind without the other.

Desire means wanting sexual activity. It can change from one situation to another.

Arousal refers to physical or psychological responses associated with sexual excitement. These may include increased genital blood flow, an erection, or vaginal lubrication, meaning natural moisture.

Orgasm is a period of sexual climax, often involving intense sensation and muscular contractions.

Ejaculation is the release of semen. It is not simply another word for orgasm.

Clinical descriptions sometimes organize sexual response into stages involving desire, arousal, orgasm and resolution, when the body returns toward its usual state. But these stages need not occur in a fixed order, and some may not happen at all (Cleveland Clinic, 2023b).

This matters for two reasons.

First, someone may want intimacy without experiencing the physical response they expected. Someone else may experience a physical response without wanting sexual activity.

Second, physical arousal is not consent. Neither an erection nor lubrication gives anyone permission to continue. Consent comes from the person, not from interpreting their body.

Chapter 30 covers that distinction in detail. Chapter 34 applies it specifically to sexual situations.

Sexual activity is optional

You may want sex frequently, occasionally, rarely or never.

You may want a romantic relationship without sex, or sexual activity without a romantic relationship. You might be unsure what you want because you have not experienced enough to know.

You can also decide that something is not for you without trying it first.

A partner’s preference does not create an obligation for you. Your preference does not create an obligation for them.

Compatibility includes whether two people can respect these differences.

Pleasure, discomfort and sensory experiences

Physical intimacy involves sensations. Some are welcome; some are distracting or unpleasant.

Temperature, smell, pressure, moisture, skin contact, movement and sound may all affect how someone experiences sexual activity.

For example, at 39, Rowan finds that unexpected light touch is unpleasant, while firmer pressure feels comfortable. Their partner, Alex, enjoys gentle touch and initially assumes Rowan will too.

Neither person is experiencing touch incorrectly. They simply need to describe what they experience instead of assuming their partner experiences the same thing.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture: two people can be looking at the same situation while experiencing different parts of it.

In the 2015 interviews with autistic adults, sensory experiences were an important part of participants’ accounts of sexuality. Some described adapting their environments or communicating preferences more explicitly. This was a small qualitative study, not evidence that every autistic person experiences touch in a particular way (Barnett & Maticka-Tyndale, 2015).

You may also discover that a sensation feels fine initially but becomes unpleasant after several minutes. If you experience overload, a pause or change of activity may be necessary.

None of these preferences needs a diagnosis to be legitimate.

If touch, smells or sounds are hard to tolerate

You may find it easier to identify sensory preferences separately from sexual preferences. For example, you might enjoy closeness but dislike a particular fabric, strong fragrance, or unexpected contact.

You can write down which sensations are comfortable, uncomfortable or uncertain before discussing them with a partner. A preference does not have to be permanent, and you do not have to tolerate an unpleasant sensation to prove affection.

Pain is different from having an unusual preference. If sexual activity hurts, you can stop. You never need to continue through pain to satisfy someone else.

If pain, irritation, bleeding or another physical change concerns you, ask a qualified clinician about it, particularly if it continues or happens again. You do not need to keep trying an activity that causes discomfort while you figure out what is happening.

Learning about pleasure does not mean learning techniques to perform for somebody else. Understanding your body, recognizing discomfort and knowing that questions are permitted are useful foundations.

The details of building mutually satisfying intimacy belong in Chapter 55.

How pregnancy happens

Pregnancy requires a particular biological process. It does not happen simply because two people are attracted to each other, share a bed, kiss or touch.

For pregnancy to begin, a sperm cell must fertilize an egg, and the resulting fertilized egg must implant in the lining of the uterus (Planned Parenthood Federation of America, n.d.).

A simplified sequence looks like this:

  1. An ovary releases an egg during ovulation.
  2. Sperm may enter the reproductive tract through the vagina.
  3. A sperm cell may reach and fertilize the egg, usually in a fallopian tube.
  4. The fertilized egg may travel to the uterus and implant in its lining.

Sperm can survive for several days in the reproductive tract, so pregnancy can result from intercourse that occurs before ovulation (Planned Parenthood Federation of America, n.d.).

A person’s orgasm is not required for pregnancy. Neither partner needs to experience pleasure for fertilization to become possible.

Pregnancy risk depends on whether sperm can reach an egg, not simply on the genders or relationship status of the people involved.

Someone with a uterus and functioning ovaries may have pregnancy potential under some circumstances. Someone who produces viable sperm may contribute to a pregnancy. Those possibilities can change with individual medical circumstances.

For people who do not want pregnancy, the safest approach is to consider contraception before participating in activities that could make pregnancy possible.

Why guessing “safe days” is unreliable

A person may think pregnancy is impossible because menstruation recently ended, because the expected ovulation date is several days away, or because an app predicts a low-fertility day.

But the timing of ovulation can vary, and sperm may remain viable for several days.

Structured fertility-awareness methods exist, but they require more than guessing a date. The CDC reports widely varying effectiveness for these methods (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a; Planned Parenthood Federation of America, n.d.).

If avoiding pregnancy is important, ask a qualified clinician about a method that fits your circumstances rather than relying on assumptions.

Contraception: what the options actually do

Contraception, also called birth control, means methods used to prevent pregnancy.

Different methods work in different ways. Some prevent sperm from reaching an egg. Others use hormones or devices to prevent pregnancy through other mechanisms.

Methods vary in effectiveness, effort, cost, medical suitability and possible side effects.

The following figures come from the CDC and describe typical-use failure rates over one year. Typical use includes what happens when people sometimes forget, delay, or use a method imperfectly.

Method Pregnancy rate with typical use over one year
Contraceptive implant 0.1%
Hormonal IUD 0.1–0.4%
Copper IUD 0.8%
Contraceptive injection 4%
Birth control pill 7%
Birth control patch 7%
Vaginal contraceptive ring 7%
External condom 13%
Internal condom 21%

Source: CDC contraception guidance (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a).

These numbers are not the probability of pregnancy from a single sexual encounter.

For example, a 13% typical-use failure rate for external condoms means that approximately 13 out of 100 users relying on that method experience pregnancy during a year of typical use. It does not mean there is a 13% chance of pregnancy every time someone uses a condom.

With correct, consistent use, some methods are more effective than their typical-use rates suggest.

An IUD, or intrauterine device, is a small device placed inside the uterus by a clinician. An implant is a small rod placed beneath the skin of the upper arm.

Other options include permanent contraception, such as vasectomy or tubal surgery. These are medical procedures intended to provide lasting pregnancy prevention (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a).

There is no single best option for everybody.

For example, one person might prefer a method that does not require remembering a daily pill. Another might want a method they can stop without a procedure. Someone else may need to avoid certain hormones for medical reasons.

You can ask a clinician to compare options according to what matters to you.

Pregnancy prevention and infection prevention are different questions

This distinction is essential:

Most birth control methods do not protect against sexually transmitted infections.

The pill, patch, ring, implant and IUD may prevent pregnancy, but they do not provide protection against HIV or other sexually transmitted infections.

Condoms are different because they can reduce both pregnancy risk and the risk of certain infections (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a, 2024d).

That is why a person may use condoms alongside another contraceptive method.

Emergency contraception

If intercourse occurs without effective contraception, or a method fails, there may still be time-sensitive options.

Emergency contraception means methods used after intercourse to prevent pregnancy.

Depending on the method and circumstances, options include emergency contraceptive pills and the copper IUD.

The CDC states that emergency contraceptive pills can be used up to five days after unprotected intercourse, and that a copper IUD can be placed within five days. Pills generally work better when taken sooner. Different pills have different requirements, so prompt professional or pharmacist guidance matters (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a).

Do not assume that every emergency contraception option is equally suitable or available for every person.

If you are concerned about pregnancy after an encounter, seek advice promptly rather than waiting to see what happens.

Sexually transmitted infections: what you need to know

A sexually transmitted infection (STI) is an infection that can pass between people through certain types of sexual contact.

Examples include chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, HIV, herpes and human papillomavirus (HPV).

Different infections have different transmission routes, health effects, testing methods and treatments.

STIs may spread through vaginal, anal or oral sex. Some can also spread through genital skin-to-skin contact (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024c).

You cannot reliably tell whether someone has an STI by looking at them.

Someone may appear healthy, have no noticeable symptoms, and still have an infection. The CDC emphasizes that many STIs cause no obvious symptoms (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026a).

This also means that appearance, hygiene and social reputation do not establish someone’s STI status.

Having an STI is a health matter, not a verdict on someone’s character.

What protection can and cannot do

External condoms are worn over the penis. Internal condoms are barriers placed inside the vagina or anus.

Both can reduce certain infection risks. External latex condoms are particularly important for HIV prevention.

But condoms do not cover all genital skin, so they provide less complete protection against infections that can spread through uncovered skin, including herpes, HPV and syphilis (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024d).

There are a few practical details worth knowing:

  • Natural membrane condoms, sometimes labeled lambskin, should not be relied upon for STI prevention.
  • Water-based and silicone-based lubricants are compatible with latex condoms.
  • Oil-based lubricants can weaken latex condoms and increase the chance of breakage.
  • Some infections can also be prevented through vaccination, including HPV and hepatitis B (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024d, 2024c).

A lubricant is a product used to reduce friction during sexual contact. It is not the same as contraception or STI protection.

If you do not know how to use a particular protective product, ask a health professional or consult the manufacturer’s instructions before relying on it. Chapter 34 returns to sexual protection and safety in more detail.

HIV prevention has additional options

HIV is a virus that affects the immune system.

Two important medical prevention options have similar names but different purposes.

PrEP, or pre-exposure prophylaxis, is medicine taken by eligible people without HIV before potential exposure to reduce the chance of acquiring HIV. Options include pills and injections, with medical testing and follow-up.

PrEP does not prevent pregnancy or other STIs (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026b).

PEP, or post-exposure prophylaxis, is medication that may be prescribed after a possible HIV exposure.

PEP is time-sensitive. It must be started as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours after a potential exposure. If prescribed, treatment generally continues for 28 days (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024e).

If you think you may have been exposed to HIV recently, seek immediate advice from an emergency department, urgent care provider or other qualified clinician. Do not wait for an ordinary appointment or for symptoms.

Not every contact requires PEP. A clinician assesses whether it is appropriate.

Testing is part of sexual health, not a sign of wrongdoing

Imagine Nina, 42, has started dating someone new.

She has no symptoms and has never been told she has an STI. She assumes this means testing is unnecessary.

But a lack of symptoms is not a reliable indication that no infection is present.

Testing can identify infections that would otherwise go unnoticed (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026a).

A clinician may recommend tests depending on age, anatomy, sexual activities, previous results, exposure and other health circumstances.

The CDC recommends that everyone ages 13 through 64 have at least one HIV test. Some people need testing more frequently, and recommendations for particular infections differ according to risk and clinical circumstances (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021, 2026a).

What happens during testing?

Testing may use:

  • Urine: A sample collected in a container.
  • Blood: A sample collected by a blood draw or, for some tests, a finger prick.
  • Swabs: Small collection devices used to take samples from relevant body sites, which may include the vagina, throat or rectum.

Not every test requires a genital examination.

Some tests can be performed using self-collected samples, including certain home-testing and laboratory mail-in options (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026a).

The type of sexual contact matters because an infection may be present at one body site but not another.

For example, a urine sample does not automatically test the throat or rectum.

That is why the phrase “I had a full STI panel” can be misleading. There is no single universal panel covering every infection at every possible site.

A useful set of questions is:

“Which infections am I being tested for? Does the testing include the body sites that matter for my sexual history? Is it too soon after a possible exposure for the results to be reliable?”

Timing matters because tests differ in how soon they can detect an infection. A clinician can advise whether repeat testing is needed.

A negative result is useful information, but it should not be interpreted as a guarantee about exposures that occurred too recently to detect.

What if a test is positive?

A positive result means you need information about that particular infection and the appropriate next steps.

Many STIs can be treated, and some can be cured. Treatment, follow-up testing and advice about partners depend on the infection (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024c).

You do not have to learn every infection’s treatment plan in advance. What matters is knowing where to get care and asking for an explanation you understand.

Getting sexual health care in the United States

Sexual health care is not limited to pregnancy or illness. You can seek it for education, contraception, screening, symptoms, or questions about your body.

Possible places to start include a primary care practice, sexual health clinic, community health center, local health department, or family-planning clinic.

You do not need to be in a relationship, have a particular sexual orientation, or already be sexually active to ask questions about sexual health.

Finding a clinic

The CDC offers a US service locator for STI and HIV testing:

https://gettested.cdc.gov/

It can help you find testing services, including clinics offering free or lower-cost options (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026a).

Another option is a clinic supported by Title X, a federal family-planning program.

Title X clinics provide voluntary family-planning services and related sexual health care. The program includes confidential services and income-based fees, although the exact services and costs need to be checked with the individual clinic (Office of Population Affairs, n.d.).

Before an appointment, ask:

  • “What will this visit cost without insurance?”
  • “Do you offer fees based on income?”
  • “What information will appear on my insurance paperwork?”
  • “Who can access my visit information?”
  • “Can someone explain the examination before it begins?”

If privacy matters to you, do not guess how confidential the billing will be. Before using insurance, ask the clinic’s billing staff what information would be sent to the insurer and what notices, if any, would be generated. Ask whether there are other payment options if you are uncomfortable with that information being shared.

Get a clear explanation before deciding how to pay for the visit.

What if talking to a clinician is difficult?

Medical appointments sometimes involve personal questions asked quickly.

A clinician may ask about your partners, sexual activities, protection, previous STI diagnoses and pregnancy plans. The CDC recommends this kind of sexual history as part of appropriate care, with sensitivity to gender, identity, trauma and individual circumstances (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024b).

You may feel uncomfortable answering aloud, particularly if the question is unexpected or uses unfamiliar language.

You can prepare written notes or request that the clinician explain terms before asking for an answer.

For example:

“I haven’t had much sex education, and some of the medical terms are unfamiliar. Could you explain things in plain language?”

Or:

“I can answer these questions, but I need a little time to think. Is there a written form I can use?”

If an examination is proposed, you can ask what it involves, why it is recommended, what alternatives exist, and whether it can be paused.

These are reasonable questions about your own health care.

If you forget questions during appointments

You may arrive with five things you wanted to ask and remember only one when the appointment begins.

Keep a short note on your phone or on paper. Start with your most important question, and ask the clinician to write down any instructions or follow-up dates.

You do not have to remember everything from a spoken explanation.

When you are learning alongside a partner

Suppose two people in their thirties begin dating.

Jamie has had several relationships and knows a fair amount about contraception. Ren has never had intercourse and knows the basic reproductive anatomy but very little about STI testing.

Ren worries that asking questions will make Jamie think they are inexperienced or unsuitable.

Jamie, meanwhile, assumes Ren already understands everything because they are both adults.

This is an ordinary communication problem with a straightforward solution: replace the assumptions with information.

Ren could say:

“There are a few sexual health things I never learned properly. I’d like to understand them rather than pretend I already do.”

Jamie might respond:

“That’s fine. There are things I still have questions about too.”

Neither person needs to become the other’s teacher or medical adviser.

They can learn independently, consult reliable sources, or ask a clinician when needed.

A partner who has more experience should not treat that experience as automatic authority. Someone who has had sex many times can still misunderstand anatomy, infection risk or contraception.

And someone who is sexually inexperienced may already have strong communication skills, clear boundaries and substantial knowledge.

Experience and understanding are not the same thing.

If you’re dating someone who needs things explained

Your partner may ask a question you consider basic.

Try not to respond with disbelief or jokes about their age.

You can answer what you know, admit what you do not know, and suggest checking a reliable source together if they want that.

Do not assume a partner’s autism, ADHD, educational background or previous relationship history tells you exactly what they understand.

People can know a great deal about one part of sexuality and very little about another.

Respecting the question helps both people make informed choices.

A private exercise: what do you actually know?

You do not need a partner to complete this.

Take a sheet of paper or open a note. Make three headings:

I understand this.

I partly understand this.

I need to learn this.

Then consider these questions:

  1. Can you identify the main reproductive structures relevant to your body and explain their functions?
  2. Can you distinguish attraction, desire, arousal and orgasm?
  3. Do you understand which sexual activities could lead to pregnancy?
  4. If pregnancy is possible, do you know which contraceptive methods may be appropriate for you?
  5. Do you know the difference between pregnancy prevention and STI prevention?
  6. Do you know where you could obtain STI testing?
  7. Would you know where to seek help after a possible HIV exposure?
  8. Do you know how to ask a medical professional about something you do not understand?

You are not being graded.

If you put seven questions under “I need to learn this,” the list has done its job. You now know what information to seek.

Choose just one topic to investigate first.

You might read a medically reviewed anatomy page, look up the CDC’s contraception guide, find a local clinic, or prepare a question for your next appointment.

For a particularly useful first step, try this sentence:

“I want to make informed decisions about sexual health, but I need help understanding my options.”

You can say it to a clinician, a health educator, or another qualified professional.

No explanation about why you did not learn these things earlier is required.

Knowing enough to make a choice

You do not have to memorize every reproductive organ, contraceptive percentage or STI screening recommendation before dating.

You do need to recognize when something matters and when you need more information.

The foundations are straightforward: bodies differ, sexual responses differ, pregnancy and infection involve different risks, and protective methods have different purposes. A person’s experience or appearance cannot substitute for reliable sexual health information.

Knowing more also gives you a clearer understanding of your own boundaries. You can decide what activities you want, what you do not want, what questions remain unanswered, and when you would rather wait.

Knowledge does not replace consent, and experience does not make someone entitled to another person’s body.

The next chapter, Sexual consent and safety, puts these foundations into practice: how to check in, recognize uncertainty, and protect both people when a situation becomes sexual.

For now, the important point is that learning is allowed at any age. You can ask questions, take your time and decide that something is not right for you.
