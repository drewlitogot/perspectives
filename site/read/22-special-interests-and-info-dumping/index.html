You’ve been talking about something you love for fifteen minutes. You know exactly why it’s fascinating. You’ve explained the history, the unusual details, and the surprising connection nobody seems to notice.

Then you look at your date.

They’re smiling politely, but they haven’t said much. You suddenly wonder whether you’ve talked too long. Maybe they were bored ten minutes ago. Maybe they’ve been enjoying every word. You can’t tell.

Or perhaps you’re the person listening. Your date is explaining a subject you know almost nothing about. Their enthusiasm is wonderful, but your attention is fading. You want to know them better. You also want to talk about something else.

Neither person has necessarily done anything wrong.

An interest that matters deeply to one person may be unfamiliar to another. The challenge isn’t deciding who should change their personality. It’s figuring out how to share something meaningful while making room for both people.

You don’t need to become less enthusiastic to be considerate. And you don’t need to pretend to love someone else’s hobbies to care about them.

When an interest is more than a hobby

Some interests occupy a particularly important place in your life.

Perhaps you know the history of every roller coaster at your favorite amusement park. You can identify birds from their calls. You spend hours learning a language, studying historical events, restoring old computers, researching family history, or understanding how a fictional universe fits together.

You might enjoy collecting facts, comparing details, organizing information, building something, or returning to a familiar activity.

The subject doesn’t have to be unusual. Gardening, music, cooking, animals, sports, and books can matter just as deeply as subjects other people consider obscure.

A special interest is an interest someone pursues with unusual depth, enjoyment, or personal importance. It’s a term often associated with autism, although people have different views about exactly what it means.

In interviews with fifteen autistic advocates in the United States, participants described special interests in several ways. Some emphasized the time they spent on them. Others emphasized how the interests felt, what they meant personally, or how they differed from ordinary hobbies. Not everyone liked the label itself (Long, 2025).

You don’t need a diagnosis, an unusual hobby, or a particular label to recognize the experience.

The useful question is what the interest means to you.

For one person, researching trains might be enjoyable entertainment. For another, it might provide a sense of order, a way to relax, a connection to childhood, and a subject they can return to when the rest of life feels unpredictable.

Those experiences aren’t interchangeable.

Research also suggests that intense interests can have both benefits and difficulties.

In a Dutch survey of 687 autistic adults, approximately two-thirds reported having a special interest. Among participants with interests, stronger personal motivation to pursue them was associated with greater wellbeing and satisfaction with social contact and leisure. However, spending particularly large amounts of time on interests was associated with lower wellbeing. The researchers couldn’t determine whether extensive engagement contributed to difficulties or whether people experiencing difficulties spent more time on their interests (Grove et al., 2018).

A German study comparing sixty autistic adults with 122 non-autistic adults similarly found that autistic respondents reported using interests for purposes such as learning and emotional regulation, while also reporting stigma and negative consequences. The results came from self-reports and cannot establish cause and effect (Bayer & Dziobek, 2026).

A review of qualitative studies likewise identified focused interests as one way autistic people described experiencing enjoyment, developing abilities, and connecting with others (Chow & Cooper, 2024).

These findings don’t mean that more interest is always better, or that intense interests are inherently unhealthy.

They suggest a more useful distinction:

An interest can be important and beneficial while still needing room alongside the rest of your life.

That’s especially relevant when you’re dating. Your interest may be a meaningful part of who you are. Your date is also a person with their own interests, responsibilities, preferences, and limits.

A relationship needs room for both.

When sharing information is a way of connecting

Imagine two people discussing their weekends.

One says, “I went hiking.”

The other replies, “That’s nice. Where?”

Now imagine a different conversation.

“I went hiking. Did you know the park used to be a limestone quarry? There’s a reason the trails follow those strange curves.”

Both people are offering something about their experience. The second person is offering information along with it.

For some people, sharing knowledge is one of the most natural ways to connect.

They might respond to something you mention by offering an interesting fact, explaining a related experience, or sharing a discovery they think you’ll appreciate.

They may be saying, in effect, This matters to me, and I’d like to share it with you.

In a UK study involving an autistic online community and interviews with fifteen autistic adults, participants described information exchange as an enjoyable form of social interaction. Some contrasted accepting online spaces, where detailed information-sharing was welcome, with other settings where their enthusiasm wasn’t always received in the same way (Duffin et al., 2025).

Researchers interviewing nine autistic people in Belgium also described specific interests as serving two roles: providing a protected space to retreat into and helping people navigate social situations and find connection (Lizon et al., 2024).

This matters because lengthy explanations can be misunderstood.

A listener might think:

Why are they giving me all these facts? I just asked how their day went.

Meanwhile, the speaker might think:

I’m sharing something I love. Why don’t they seem interested in me?

This is another place where the double empathy problem becomes visible. The two people can be trying to connect while interpreting the interaction differently.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from earlier in the book. The speaker sees the information as an invitation into their world. The listener may see a conversation that has stopped making room for them.

Neither interpretation needs to be dishonest.

Instead of guessing which meaning is correct, both people can explain what they’re experiencing.

There’s also an important distinction between sharing information and expecting someone to receive unlimited information.

Your enthusiasm can be sincere and generous. The other person can still have reached their limit.

Both things can be true.

What people mean by “info-dumping”

You may have heard the word info-dumping.

It’s an informal term for sharing a large amount of information at once, often about something you find especially interesting.

Someone might describe spending twenty minutes explaining an obscure historical event, a video game’s mechanics, or why an ordinary household object is surprisingly complicated.

The term appears in peer-reviewed research as language used by autistic participants. It isn’t a clinical diagnosis or a standardized measurement of someone’s romantic communication style (Duffin et al., 2025).

Different people also feel differently about the word.

Some use it affectionately. For them, an info-dump is a joyful exchange of knowledge.

Others dislike it because “dumping” makes their enthusiasm sound like unwanted material being placed on another person.

You don’t have to call your way of speaking an info-dump. “Explaining,” “sharing,” or “talking about an interest” may fit better.

The practical issue is not what you call it. It’s whether the conversation works for the people involved.

A ten-minute explanation can be wonderful when both people want it. A two-minute explanation can feel uncomfortable when someone has clearly asked to stop.

Length alone doesn’t tell you whether an interaction is welcome.

What matters includes the listener’s interest, available attention, the setting, and whether both people can influence where the conversation goes.

Offer an invitation, not an obligation

Suppose you’re having coffee with someone new.

They mention visiting Chicago. You happen to know an enormous amount about the city’s architecture.

You could immediately begin describing every building you’ve studied.

Or you could give them a way to participate.

“Chicago has some fascinating architecture. Are you interested in that stuff?”

They might say yes.

They might say they love architecture but don’t know much about Chicago.

Or they might explain that they mostly went for the food.

Now you know a little more about what kind of conversation might work.

You haven’t hidden your enthusiasm. You’ve given the other person a choice.

Start with the part that matters most

You don’t have to explain everything you know to communicate why something interests you.

Suppose you love restoring vintage electronics.

A complete explanation might involve manufacturing history, component differences, repair techniques, and the reasons certain machines are difficult to find.

A shorter beginning could be:

“I restore old computers. I love figuring out how something that hasn’t worked in thirty years can work again.”

That gives the listener several ways into the conversation.

They might ask what you’ve restored, whether it’s expensive, or what originally got you interested.

They might even tell you about something they enjoy repairing.

You’ve offered a beginning rather than the entire subject.

A useful habit is to start with the smallest explanation that communicates why you care.

Then see whether the other person wants more.

Let the other person choose the amount of detail

If someone seems interested, you can ask directly.

“There’s a much longer explanation. Want to hear it?”

Or:

“Do you want the short version or the detailed one?”

These aren’t tricks for getting permission to talk indefinitely.

They’re ways of discovering what the other person actually wants.

If they choose the short version, give them the short version.

If they choose the detailed one, that doesn’t mean they’ve agreed to listen for the rest of the evening. They can change their mind, ask questions, or move to another topic.

And if they say no, you don’t need to argue that the subject is fascinating.

You can say, “Fair enough,” and continue with something else.

Someone declining an explanation is not necessarily rejecting you.

Leave places for the listener to enter

Imagine a conversation as something two people can contribute to, rather than a presentation one person must finish.

You can pause after an interesting point.

You might ask:

“Does that make sense?”

“Have you ever seen anything like that?”

“What’s your favorite part of that kind of thing?”

Only ask questions you’re willing to hear the answers to.

If you’re mentally preparing your next fifteen facts while the other person replies, the conversation may still feel one-sided.

A question is useful because it gives you new information about the person you’re getting to know.

It isn’t just a pause between explanations.

And if the other person takes the conversation somewhere unexpected, consider following them.

They may be showing you an aspect of the subject you hadn’t considered, or telling you something important about themselves.

Share what the interest means to you

Facts can tell someone what you know.

Your reasons for caring can tell them something about you.

Consider two ways to describe the same hobby.

Version one:

“The first commercial pinball machines appeared in the early twentieth century. The scoring mechanisms changed as electrical components became more sophisticated.”

Version two:

“I love restoring pinball machines because my grandfather taught me how they worked. I still think about those afternoons whenever I repair one.”

Neither is the correct version for every conversation.

Someone who loves machinery might be fascinated by the first explanation. Someone getting to know you romantically might find the second particularly meaningful.

You can also combine them.

“My grandfather taught me how to restore pinball machines. That’s probably why I know so much about the mechanical ones. The scoring systems are surprisingly clever.”

Now you’ve shared information, history, and a personal connection.

General relationship research helps explain why this distinction may matter.

In two diary studies of interpersonal interactions, researchers found that sharing information about yourself and feeling that another person responds with understanding were associated with intimacy. In one study, sharing emotions was more strongly associated with intimacy than sharing facts alone (Laurenceau et al., 1998).

These studies weren’t specifically about autistic people or special interests. They don’t mean you have to become emotionally expressive in a particular way.

They do suggest a useful possibility: sometimes a listener wants to know not just what you understand, but why it matters to you.

That can be difficult to explain, particularly when an interest feels so familiar that you’ve never needed to put its meaning into words.

You don’t have to produce a dramatic personal story.

Simple statements are enough:

“I like how predictable the patterns are.”

“Learning about this helps me relax.”

“I’ve loved it since I was little.”

“I get excited whenever I find something new.”

“This is one of the few activities where I can completely concentrate.”

These explanations invite someone to understand your experience without requiring them to become an expert.

They also give a partner something meaningful to appreciate even when the technical details don’t interest them.

If talking about what you love is how you get close to people

Explaining something you care about in detail can be an act of trust rather than a lecture. If you’ve been told you talk too much, you may have learned to suppress the one thing that makes you easiest to know.

The fix usually isn’t saying less; it’s leaving doors open. Pause where the other person could step in, ask what they’d like more of, and notice whether their questions are curious or polite.

Someone who enjoys your enthusiasm without sharing the interest is a good sign. Someone who needs you to be quieter in order to be comfortable is telling you something worth knowing early.

If you’re the person listening

Being a good listener doesn’t require pretending every subject fascinates you.

Suppose your date, Mateo, loves astronomy. You’re interested in Mateo, but you have little interest in astronomy itself.

He starts explaining different kinds of stars.

You could pretend to understand and hope he stops soon.

You could interrupt and tell him astronomy is boring.

Or you could respond honestly:

“I don’t know much about stars, but you seem really excited about them. What got you interested?”

That question is about Mateo rather than astronomy.

You might learn that he used to look through a telescope with his father, that he finds the night sky calming, or that he’s been fascinated by space since childhood.

You still don’t need to memorize the differences between types of stars.

You can appreciate what the subject means to him.

Respond to the excitement, not just the facts

In a study involving seventy-nine heterosexual dating couples, researchers examined how partners responded when someone shared a positive event. Supportive responses were associated with relationship quality and later relationship outcomes (Gable et al., 2006).

The study wasn’t about special interests, and its participants don’t represent every kind of couple. It does offer a useful general principle: the way you respond to something important to your partner can matter, even when the event itself isn’t important to you.

For example:

Your girlfriend finishes building a complicated model railway layout.

You don’t care about model railways.

But you can still say:

“You’ve been working on that for weeks. You look really pleased with how it turned out.”

You’re recognizing her accomplishment.

That is different from claiming to share her enthusiasm.

Likewise, if your boyfriend spends an afternoon identifying a rare bird, you can be pleased that he had a good day without pretending birdwatching has become your favorite activity.

You can care about someone’s happiness without sharing every source of it.

The reverse matters too.

If your date cares deeply about sports and you don’t, you can offer curiosity and respect without promising to attend every game.

A relationship doesn’t require identical hobbies.

It requires a way to treat each other’s interests as legitimate, even when they aren’t shared.

You can listen differently

Sometimes you might enjoy hearing someone talk while doing a quiet activity.

You might prefer asking questions rather than listening to a continuous explanation.

You might enjoy looking at pictures or seeing the object someone is describing.

You might need explanations broken into smaller pieces.

It’s reasonable to say what works for you.

“Can you show me a picture? I’m having trouble imagining it.”

“Can you explain that one part again?”

“I’d like to hear about it, but I follow better when we go slowly.”

These are invitations to find a way of sharing that works for both people.

You don’t have to demonstrate love by enduring a conversation you can’t follow.

And the speaker doesn’t have to treat every request for a different explanation as criticism.

When enthusiasm becomes exhausting

Now imagine two partners who have been dating for several months.

Tasha loves gardening. Her partner, Elise, enjoys hearing about it, particularly when Tasha describes what she’s growing or shows her something beautiful.

Tonight, however, Elise has finished a difficult workday. She’s tired and wants quiet.

Tasha starts explaining a problem with the soil in her vegetable garden.

Elise listens for a few minutes, then says:

“I want to hear about your garden, but I don’t have the attention for a long conversation tonight. Could you tell me tomorrow?”

Tasha is disappointed. She was excited to explain what she’d discovered.

But Elise hasn’t said that gardening is stupid or that Tasha talks too much.

She’s explained what she can manage.

Tasha can respect that limit without having to stop loving gardening.

A considerate reply might be:

“Okay. I was excited about it, but tomorrow works.”

The important part is not that neither person feels disappointed.

It’s that disappointment doesn’t turn into pressure.

You don’t have to finish the explanation

If you begin talking and discover that the other person needs to stop, you can leave the subject unfinished.

You might say:

“Okay, I’ll stop there.”

Or:

“I have more to say, but it can wait.”

You don’t need to squeeze in the final six facts because you’re almost finished.

A person’s attention isn’t something you’re owed because you started speaking.

At the same time, needing a pause doesn’t mean the conversation can never happen.

If both people want to continue later, they can make room for it at another time.

A listener can be clear without being cruel

If you’re listening, try to describe your own limits instead of making a judgment about the speaker.

Compare:

“You always talk about things nobody cares about.”

With:

“I’m getting tired and can’t follow the details anymore. I need a break.”

The first attacks the person’s interests and assumes nobody values them.

The second communicates what you can manage.

You can also be honest when the subject doesn’t interest you.

“I like hearing about what you’ve been doing, but the technical details aren’t really my thing.”

That might disappoint someone. Disappointment alone doesn’t mean you’ve been unkind.

You aren’t required to enjoy a particular hobby to be a good partner.

And you shouldn’t have to invent enthusiasm to avoid an argument.

If you're dating someone who talks at length about one subject

Depth of interest is often where the person is most themselves, and most delighted. Treating it as a problem to ration tends to shut down the warmest part of them.

You’re still allowed a limit. Name it about capacity rather than worth: “I’m running out of attention, not interest. Can we come back to this tomorrow?” That is very different from implying the subject was a burden.

Asking one real question costs little and lands enormously. You don’t have to care about the topic to enjoy watching someone care about it.

When your attention works differently

Some people become so absorbed in an enjoyable activity or explanation that they lose track of time.

Others find that attention moves quickly, even when they’re genuinely interested.

ADHD is sometimes associated with hyperfocus, a term for becoming intensely absorbed in an activity.

A study of adults found that higher ADHD traits were associated with more self-reported hyperfocus in several settings (Hupfeld et al., 2019).

But another study found a more complicated result. ADHD traits were associated with hyperfocus in a large nonclinical sample, while a smaller clinical ADHD group did not report more overall hyperfocus than matched controls (Groen et al., 2020).

Neither study investigated how long people talk to romantic partners.

So hyperfocus shouldn’t be used as an explanation for every lengthy conversation, and info-dumping isn’t a test for ADHD.

What matters in everyday conversation is recognizing when attention or time becomes difficult to track.

If you sometimes look up and discover you’ve been explaining something for half an hour, you and your partner might agree on a simple way to check in.

For example:

“Sometimes I get really involved in explaining things and don’t notice how long I’ve been talking. You’re welcome to tell me when you want to change topics.”

Your partner could respond:

“That’s good to know. If I’m tired, I’ll say so directly.”

This is an agreement, not a promise that everything will go perfectly.

A time check may help. So might a brief pause after finishing one idea, or a clear signal that either person can use when they need a change.

The best choice is one both people genuinely accept.

It should also work in both directions.

If your partner can ask you to pause a subject, you should be able to ask them to pause theirs.

Neither person’s way of thinking makes them the permanent speaker or permanent listener.

You don’t have to love the same things

A common worry when dating is that your interests are too unusual, too intense, or too important for another person to understand.

Perhaps you’ve spent years enjoying something that other people have dismissed.

Now you’re wondering whether to tell a date about it at all.

There are two different questions here:

Does this person enjoy my interest?

Does this person respect that the interest matters to me?

The answers don’t have to be the same.

Your date may have no desire to learn about historical railroad timetables, but they might enjoy hearing how you found a rare document.

They may never want to play your favorite video game, but they might appreciate the artwork you’ve created around it.

They might not understand your fascination with weather patterns, but they can still ask whether you enjoyed the storm-watching trip you planned.

A small US study of 106 autistic adults in romantic relationships included accounts of people connecting through shared special interests. Some participants particularly valued this in relationships with other autistic partners (Khaw & Vernon, 2026).

However, the same study found no statistically significant overall difference in relationship satisfaction across groups defined by whether participants’ partners were autistic, otherwise neurodivergent, or neurotypical.

The findings came from one partner in each relationship, not both, and the sample was self-selected. They cannot tell you which kind of partner is best for an individual.

A review of fourteen studies of autistic adults’ relationship experiences also found that shared interests and structured activities appeared in participants’ descriptions of preferred ways to connect, although much of that research concerned friendship rather than romance (Minnell et al., 2025).

You might find someone who shares your favorite interest.

You might find someone who enjoys a completely different subject.

Or you might both have intense interests and enjoy sitting together while working on separate things.

What matters is whether you can build a relationship that accommodates both people’s lives.

When both partners have intense interests

Consider Jordan and Priya, who are both autistic.

Jordan loves collecting and restoring old radios. Priya studies local history and spends weekends exploring archives.

They understand each other’s enthusiasm, but that doesn’t mean every conversation is effortless.

Jordan can talk about radio circuits for an hour. Priya can spend just as long explaining an unusual discovery in nineteenth-century city records.

Sometimes each person wants to share something at the same moment.

They might agree:

“Tell me about the radio first, and then I’d like to show you what I found.”

On another evening, neither may want a detailed explanation.

They might simply sit together while Jordan repairs a radio and Priya reads.

That can be a perfectly satisfying way to spend time, if both people enjoy it.

Being similar in one way doesn’t remove the need to communicate preferences.

And being different doesn’t mean a relationship is doomed to misunderstanding.

Two people still have to learn what works for them.

The difference between adjustment and masking

There is an important difference between choosing how to share an interest and hiding the fact that you have one.

Masking means hiding or suppressing your natural traits to fit in.

If you’ve spent years being told that you’re too intense, too talkative, or too interested in the wrong things, you may have learned to keep your enthusiasm private.

Perhaps you don’t mention your hobbies on dates.

Perhaps you pretend to enjoy whatever the other person enjoys.

Perhaps you carefully monitor every sentence until talking stops feeling natural.

You may be trying to avoid another experience of rejection.

But a relationship built around hiding important parts of yourself gives the other person an incomplete picture of who they’re dating.

You don’t need to reveal everything about yourself on a first date. You can choose how much to share and when.

Still, there’s a difference between saying, “This isn’t the moment for a long explanation,” and deciding, “Nobody I’m dating should ever know I care about this.”

The first is an adjustment to the conversation.

The second can mean concealing something meaningful about yourself.

In interviews with autistic advocates, special interests emerged as personally significant experiences, and participants raised concerns about expectations that they restrict or diminish those interests to fit other people’s preferences (Long, 2025).

A respectful relationship shouldn’t require you to give up harmless interests simply because your partner doesn’t share them.

That doesn’t mean every interest must take priority over shared responsibilities.

If you regularly miss plans, neglect responsibilities you’ve agreed to, or leave all the practical work to your partner because you’re absorbed in a hobby, those effects deserve attention.

The problem isn’t that you love your hobby.

It’s that your choices are affecting another person’s life.

Likewise, if a partner repeatedly mocks your interests, pressures you to abandon them, or treats your enjoyment as embarrassing, you don’t have to accept that treatment as the price of being loved.

You can distinguish between a reasonable limit on someone’s time and a demand that you become a different person.

For example:

“I can’t listen to a detailed explanation right now” is a limit on someone’s availability.

“I don’t want you to enjoy that anymore” is a very different demand.

Neither person has to surrender their identity to make a conversation work.

When sharing becomes one-sided

There is another difficulty worth recognizing.

Sometimes one person talks enthusiastically about their interests but rarely asks about the other person’s life.

This may happen without any intention to dismiss the listener.

The speaker might believe that sharing information is how the relationship works. They might not realize the other person wants more questions, shared activities, or attention to their experiences.

But good intentions don’t erase the effects.

If your partner has listened to you describe your hobbies for an hour, and you have no idea what their day was like, something may need to change.

The earlier chapter on effort and reciprocity applies here.

Reciprocity doesn’t mean counting minutes and demanding perfectly equal speaking time.

It means both people have meaningful opportunities to contribute.

You might talk for twenty minutes about a discovery because your partner genuinely enjoys listening. Later, your partner might explain a difficult experience at work, and you listen carefully.

Those exchanges are different in length and subject, but both people are receiving attention.

The question is whether the relationship allows each person to matter.

Notice what happens when the subject changes

Here’s a simple way to examine your own habits.

After you’ve described something you enjoy, consider:

  • Do you know whether the other person wanted the explanation?
  • Did they have opportunities to ask questions or change the subject?
  • Have you shown curiosity about something that matters to them?
  • When they asked for a pause, did you respect it?

You don’t have to answer yes to all four every time you speak.

These are things to notice, not a test you must pass.

If you discover that you often return to your own topic immediately, try staying with the other person’s subject a little longer.

Ask a genuine follow-up question.

Listen to the answer.

You might discover something about them that you’d otherwise have missed.

The same reflection applies if you’re usually the listener. Have you told your partner what you enjoy? Have you explained when you need a different kind of conversation? Have you allowed them to know you, rather than assuming they should work out your preferences without being told?

Responsibility for understanding a conversation doesn’t belong entirely to one person.

Both people have something to communicate.

A small exercise: share an interest in three ways

Choose something you enjoy. It can be ordinary or unusual, something you’ve loved for years or something you recently discovered.

Imagine explaining it to someone who knows very little about it.

First, give the short version.

Try explaining what it is and why you enjoy it in two or three sentences.

For example:

“I like growing hot peppers. There are so many varieties, and I love comparing how they taste. It’s satisfying to grow something from a seed and eventually cook with it.”

Next, offer a way into the conversation.

Think of one genuine question or invitation.

“Have you ever grown anything?”

“Do you like spicy food?”

“Want to see a picture of what I’m growing?”

The question should be easy to answer honestly, including with no.

Finally, practice leaving the subject unfinished.

Imagine the other person says:

“That’s interesting, but I’m getting tired.”

Try responding without apologizing for the existence of your interest or insisting on completing the explanation.

“Okay. What would you rather do?”

Or simply:

“No problem.”

You can practice this with a friend, a partner, or by speaking the words aloud on your own.

Then reverse the exercise.

Imagine someone explaining a hobby you don’t share. Practice expressing curiosity, asking for clarification, or saying kindly that you need to stop listening.

The goal isn’t to memorize a perfect script.

It’s to become comfortable with the idea that sharing an interest involves two people, both of whom get a choice.

You are more than your ability to explain things

An intense interest can be a remarkable part of someone’s life.

It may give them pleasure, familiarity, knowledge, creativity, or a way to connect with other people.

A partner doesn’t need to understand every detail to appreciate that.

But being known also involves letting someone see more than your expertise.

Your date may want to know what makes you laugh, what you value, how you spend an ordinary evening, and how you respond when they need something.

They may want to hear about your interests and also tell you about theirs.

And sometimes, after a long day, they may simply want to sit beside you without discussing anything.

There’s room for all of that.

You don’t have to turn every conversation into a lecture. You don’t have to hide the subjects that make you happy. You don’t have to pretend to enjoy things you find boring.

You can share what matters, invite another person into it, and respect their choice about how far they want to follow.

You can also accept their invitation into something you wouldn’t have explored on your own.

That’s the difference between knowing a great deal about a subject and getting to know a person.

And sometimes the next chance to share something doesn’t happen face to face. It happens through a message, where you can’t immediately hear someone’s voice or see whether they’re following along.

That brings its own questions about timing, attention, and what silence means.
