You’re getting along with someone. You’ve exchanged messages, made plans, and started looking forward to seeing them.

Then a question appears in your mind: Should you tell them?

Maybe you’re autistic. Maybe you have ADHD. Maybe you’ve spent years wondering why conversations, crowded places, or unexpected changes seem harder for you than for other people. You might have a diagnosis, suspect one, or have no interest in finding a label.

You could explain why you prefer a quiet restaurant. You could tell your date why you sometimes need extra time to answer a question. You could share your diagnosis and hope they’ll understand.

Or you could say nothing for now.

None of those choices is automatically wrong.

Disclosure means choosing to share personal information about your neurotype, diagnosis, or related experiences with someone else. It can be as direct as saying you’re autistic, or as gradual as explaining a difficulty before deciding whether to name it.

The important questions aren’t simply whether to tell someone. They’re what you want the person to understand, whether you trust them with the information, and how much you’re comfortable sharing.

You have choices. So does the person you’re dating.

You don’t have to explain your entire brain

Consider two people arranging a second date.

Leah, 36, finds crowded restaurants exhausting. Music, overlapping conversations, and bright lights make it difficult for her to concentrate on the person sitting across from her.

Her date suggests a busy restaurant.

Leah could say:

“Could we go somewhere quieter? I have trouble following conversations when there’s a lot of background noise.”

That explains her immediate need.

She could also say:

“I’m autistic, and noisy places can be difficult for me. I’d enjoy talking somewhere quieter.”

That shares a label and explains what it means for her.

Or she could say:

“I’d really prefer somewhere quiet where it’s easier to talk.”

That communicates a preference without explaining the reason.

All three are reasonable. Leah doesn’t owe someone her medical history just to choose a restaurant.

This is an important distinction: sharing what helps you is not the same as disclosing a diagnosis.

You might tell someone that you prefer direct questions, need plans in advance, or occasionally forget to respond to messages. Those things can matter to a relationship whether or not you attach a label to them.

You might also want to tell someone you’re autistic or have ADHD because the label is an important part of how you understand yourself. You might be tired of hiding that part of your life.

Both motivations are legitimate.

A small UK interview study of autistic adults diagnosed later in life found that people described several reasons for sharing their diagnosis, including wanting support, explaining their experiences, being authentic, and connecting with others. Some had talked about suspected differences or support needs before receiving a formal diagnosis. Participants also described difficult reactions that influenced whom they told afterward (Au-Yeung et al., 2025).

The study involved twelve people. It cannot tell you what will happen on your next date, but it illustrates why disclosure is not a single decision everyone makes for the same reason.

A label can be useful information. It does not have to be the price of being understood.

Will telling someone make them like you more?

There is encouraging research, but it comes with qualifications.

In one US experiment, researchers examined how non-autistic people formed first impressions of autistic adults. The study involved 215 non-autistic observers rating 40 autistic and non-autistic adults.

When observers knew someone was autistic, their impressions of autistic adults improved. Observers with greater knowledge of autism also tended to evaluate autistic adults more positively (Sasson & Morrison, 2019).

That suggests a label can help someone interpret behavior they might otherwise misunderstand.

But a favorable first impression is not the same as romantic attraction.

Another study tested something closer to dating.

Researchers showed hypothetical online dating profiles of men to 306 young women in the UK. The profiles varied in whether they mentioned autism and how positively they were worded.

Including an autism label improved some evaluations, such as perceived trustworthiness and social attractiveness. However, the autism label did not produce an overall increase in willingness to date the person.

More positive profile wording did increase willingness to date.

The results also depended on the observer’s attitudes. Among women with greater stigma toward autism, disclosure could lower ratings of social attractiveness and dating interest (Brosnan & Gavin, 2021).

This does not mean you should conceal your neurotype to appeal to someone prejudiced. It means a person can respond to disclosure in ways that reflect their own knowledge and attitudes.

The study also had narrow limits. Its participants were young UK women considering hypothetical male profiles. It did not examine actual dates, lasting relationships, older adults, or the many other combinations of gender and orientation.

There is no reliable percentage you can use to calculate your chances of finding a partner by revealing or concealing a diagnosis.

And there is an important difference between what people say when rating a profile and how they behave when another person trusts them with personal information.

What happens outside an experiment?

A review of autism disclosure research found a notable difference between the perspectives of observers and autistic people themselves.

Outside observers often reported more favorable impressions after learning someone was autistic. Autistic people describing their own experiences also reported negative reactions, stigma, and other difficulties (Thompson-Hodgetts et al., 2020).

A later UK interview study likewise documented both supportive reactions and experiences of disbelief or dismissal (Au-Yeung et al., 2025).

Those findings aren’t necessarily contradictory.

An experiment might measure whether a label makes an unfamiliar person seem more approachable. An autistic person describing a past relationship may be reporting what happened when someone doubted their diagnosis, treated them differently, or failed to respect their needs.

Those are different outcomes.

Disclosure can help someone understand you. It can also reveal that someone isn’t willing to understand you.

Neither possibility makes disclosure a guaranteed way to find a compatible partner.

Reasons to share, and reasons to wait

Imagine meeting someone you really like. You want them to know the real you, but you also know that people sometimes make assumptions.

There can be good reasons to share information early:

  • You want to describe yourself openly rather than spend dates hiding an important part of your life.
  • Your neurotype affects how you communicate or arrange dates, and explaining it could prevent confusion.
  • You want to discuss something important to you without repeatedly changing the subject.
  • You feel comfortable with the person and want them to understand you more deeply.

There can also be good reasons to wait:

  • You don’t know the person well enough to trust them with private information.
  • You’ve experienced stigma or disbelief before.
  • You haven’t decided what the label means to you.
  • You’re still determining whether you want another date.
  • The information isn’t relevant to anything happening between you yet.

These are choices to consider, not experimentally proven strategies.

In an Australian study, 36 autistic adults recorded 231 situations over two months in which disclosure was an option. Decisions varied according to the person, setting, possible benefits, and anticipated costs (Love et al., 2023).

The study wasn’t about dating specifically. Still, it challenges the idea that disclosure must be a single permanent decision.

You can tell one person and not another. You can tell someone about your sensory needs without sharing a diagnosis. You can become more open as trust develops.

Privacy isn’t the same as dishonesty.

There is, however, a responsibility to communicate honestly about matters that affect your shared plans and choices.

Suppose you know you cannot comfortably spend hours in a crowded venue. You don’t have to disclose autism, but repeatedly agreeing to crowded dates while hoping the problem will somehow disappear isn’t helping either person.

Or suppose you often forget arrangements. You don’t have to explain your medical history, but your date still needs to know whether you intend to keep a plan.

Being private about the reasons for a difficulty doesn’t remove the responsibility to address its practical consequences.

When should you tell someone?

There is no established best moment.

A survey of 102 autistic adults who had used dating apps found that 54.5% did not disclose autism in their dating profiles. Some shared it later.

Participants described different reasons for their choices. Those who disclosed sometimes wanted to be authentic, communicate their needs, or find people who might understand them. Those who did not disclose often mentioned stigma and safety concerns (Gibbs et al., 2026b).

A related study interviewed twenty participants from the same Australian research project. Their experiences showed that the timing and setting of disclosure mattered to them, but there was no single approach that consistently produced good outcomes (Gibbs et al., 2026a).

These were self-selected participants reporting their experiences. The two studies also shared participants, so they are not independent confirmations.

Research has not established whether mentioning autism or ADHD in a profile, an early message, a first date, or a later relationship produces better outcomes.

You can choose based on your circumstances.

In a dating profile

A profile is an option if you want your neurotype to be part of how you introduce yourself.

For example:

“Autistic, happiest with thoughtful conversations, quiet cafés, and people who communicate directly.”

Or:

“ADHD, enthusiastic about new ideas, and a fan of making actual plans instead of endlessly saying ‘sometime.’”

These descriptions say something about the person beyond the label.

You might instead describe your interests and preferences without mentioning a diagnosis. A profile is a short introduction, not an autobiography.

Remember that profile information can be seen by people you don’t know. Think carefully before adding anything you would be uncomfortable having shared beyond a potential date.

During early messages

Perhaps a person you’ve matched with asks why you prefer specific plans.

You could say:

“I find it easier when plans are clear. I have ADHD, and putting the details in my calendar helps me keep track.”

Or:

“I do better when I know the time and place in advance. That helps me be present when we’re together.”

Both answer the question. One also shares a diagnosis.

Texting may give you time to choose your words. It can also leave room for misunderstandings about tone, as the earlier chapter on messaging explains.

You don’t have to turn a normal conversation into a formal announcement.

During an early date

Sometimes the subject comes up naturally.

A date notices that you need a few seconds before answering a complicated question.

You might explain:

“Sometimes I need a moment to work out what I want to say. I’m autistic, and having a little thinking time helps.”

The person might ask a respectful question. They might simply accept the explanation and continue talking.

They don’t have to react dramatically for the disclosure to have been worthwhile.

If you’re already dealing with overload or struggling to speak, you can also wait until you have more energy. Important conversations do not need to happen at the hardest possible moment.

After you’ve developed trust

You may prefer to learn more about someone before sharing a diagnosis.

That can make sense, especially if you’ve previously encountered disbelief or stigma.

Suppose Marcus, 43, has been dating another man for several weeks. He likes their conversations and feels comfortable around him.

One evening, Marcus says:

“There’s something about me I’d like you to know. I’m autistic. I’ve been hesitant to tell people because I’ve had some unhelpful reactions before. For me, it mostly matters in how I handle sensory overload and communication.”

His date listens and asks whether there’s anything Marcus would like him to do differently.

Later, his date shares that he is autistic too, although they experience it differently.

Neither had known about the other’s neurotype when they met.

They still need to learn each other’s needs. Sharing a label doesn’t mean they share every preference, strength, or difficulty.

The point isn’t that waiting creates a better outcome. It is simply one reasonable way of choosing when to share personal information.

You can change how much you share as a relationship develops. You cannot guarantee how another person will react.

How to explain what the label means for you

Suppose you tell someone you’re autistic and they respond:

“What does that mean for you?”

That’s an opportunity to explain your actual experiences.

A useful way to organize an explanation is to cover three things:

  1. What you want them to know.
  2. How it affects you in situations they might encounter.
  3. What would help, if anything.

You don’t need a speech. A few sentences may be enough.

For example:

“I’m autistic. I sometimes miss hints, especially when someone expects me to understand something they haven’t said directly. If something matters to you, I’d rather you tell me plainly.”

Or:

“I have ADHD. I can get deeply involved in what I’m doing and lose track of time. I’m working on using reminders so I can be reliable about our plans.”

Or:

“I’m autistic, and I don’t always show excitement in the way people expect. If I’m quiet, that doesn’t necessarily mean I’m unhappy or uninterested.”

These statements don’t describe every autistic person or every person with ADHD. They describe the speaker.

That’s why specific information is often more useful than assuming the label explains everything.

You can also make clear that you’re still learning about yourself.

If you're still figuring out what fits

You may recognize patterns in yourself without having a diagnosis. Perhaps you’ve been assessed before and received conflicting explanations. Perhaps you aren’t interested in an assessment right now.

You can describe your experiences without presenting a suspicion as a confirmed diagnosis: “I think I might be autistic, but I’m still figuring that out. What I do know is that I find indirect hints confusing, so clear communication helps me.”

You can also leave the suspected label out entirely. You don’t need someone else’s agreement about a diagnosis to explain a preference or ask for something reasonable.

You don’t have to apologize for the label

Notice the difference between these two statements:

“I’m sorry, I’m autistic, so I might be difficult to deal with.”

And:

“I’m autistic. Sometimes I need more time to process a question, and I’m happy to explain what helps.”

The first describes the person as a problem before the date has even had an opportunity to know them.

The second gives information.

You can acknowledge real difficulties without describing yourself as a burden.

At the same time, you don’t have to present your neurotype as an extraordinary gift or list only positive traits. A realistic explanation can include things that are easy, things that are difficult, and things you’re learning to handle.

You don’t need to market yourself. You’re helping another person understand you.

ADHD disclosure has its own considerations

If you lose track of messages, interrupt when excited, struggle to begin tasks, or have trouble organizing plans, you may wonder whether naming ADHD would help someone understand those behaviors.

Some ADHD research directly addresses disclosure, although not specifically romantic dating.

A 2026 qualitative study of sixty emerging adults, mostly from the US and aged 18 to 29, examined how they chose whether to disclose ADHD in social relationships. Identity, stigma, earlier experiences, and the circumstances of the interaction shaped their decisions (Mazur, 2026).

Another qualitative study, involving 43 adults with ADHD recruited through online communities, described experiences involving masking, stigma, communication, and misunderstanding in interpersonal relationships (Ginapp et al., 2023).

Neither study establishes when someone should disclose ADHD to a romantic partner.

One practical distinction matters: an explanation is not the same as an exemption from responsibility.

Imagine Tessa, 32, has ADHD and has forgotten to reply to her girlfriend’s messages several times.

Tessa might say:

“I have ADHD, and sometimes I read a message and forget to answer. That doesn’t mean I don’t care. I’m going to try setting a reminder when I can’t reply right away.”

That explains something about her experience while acknowledging its effect on the relationship.

Her girlfriend might appreciate the explanation and still want more reliable communication.

Both things can be true.

The girlfriend isn’t required to pretend that missed messages never bother her. Tessa isn’t required to hide her ADHD to be deserving of affection.

They can talk about what each person needs and whether they can find an arrangement that works for both.

An ADHD label doesn’t mean you are unreliable by definition. Nor does it make someone else’s disappointment meaningless.

The goal is understanding and shared responsibility, not finding a label that ends the discussion.

What about trauma and other personal history?

Some people find dating difficult because of experiences that happened to them, rather than because of a neurotype. Others may have both.

You might find raised voices frightening. You might need more time before trusting someone. You might feel uneasy about certain situations without wanting to describe why.

You can communicate the present need without recounting the entire experience.

For example:

“I don’t do well when conversations get loud. I’d prefer that we pause and talk when things are calmer.”

Or:

“There are some personal experiences I’m not ready to talk about yet. I can tell you what helps me feel comfortable.”

You don’t have to turn a date into a detailed account of childhood events or past relationships.

Sharing a trauma history is also a different decision from disclosing autism or ADHD. The studies in this chapter do not establish an ideal time or method for telling a romantic partner about past trauma.

Someone might respond with care, uncertainty, or questions. You’re allowed to decide which questions you’re comfortable answering.

If you want help understanding how past experiences affect your current relationships, a qualified mental health professional can be a useful source of support. That does not mean you owe a diagnosis or a personal history to someone you’re dating.

What a respectful response looks like

Now consider the other side.

You’re dating someone who tells you they’re autistic, have ADHD, or are still exploring a diagnosis.

What should you do?

You don’t need to have an expert understanding of their neurotype. You don’t need to analyze every behavior they’ve shown since meeting you.

Start by listening.

You might say:

“Thanks for telling me. Is there anything about how you like to communicate or make plans that would be helpful for me to know?”

Or:

“I’m glad you felt comfortable sharing that. What does it mean for you?”

If they’re not ready to explain more, accept that.

Avoid turning the moment into an interview. A person who mentions autism hasn’t necessarily invited you to ask about their childhood, medical records, or every challenge they’ve ever experienced.

Research suggests that autism knowledge and diagnostic information can influence social impressions. In the first-impression experiment discussed earlier, greater autism knowledge was associated with more favorable evaluations (Sasson & Morrison, 2019).

Another study involving 311 autistic and non-autistic adults found that participants reported better rapport in an information-sharing task when they knew the diagnostic makeup of the group. That was a structured, nonromantic experiment, not a test of dating disclosure (Crompton et al., 2025).

A separate study of brief social interactions found that autistic adults shared more personal information with autistic conversational partners than with non-autistic partners. Importantly, sharing personal information in conversation is not the same thing as revealing an autism diagnosis (Morrison et al., 2020).

Taken together, these studies suggest that context and understanding can matter in social interactions. They do not provide a guaranteed script for becoming a better romantic partner.

If you're dating someone who has just shared their diagnosis

A diagnosis tells you something important about how the person understands themselves. It doesn’t tell you everything about how they communicate, experience affection, or want to spend time together.

Ask about their individual experiences instead of relying on a list of traits you’ve read online. If they say loud restaurants are difficult, take that information seriously. If they say they enjoy hugs, don’t assume they must dislike touch because they’re autistic.

You can share your own preferences too. Understanding goes in both directions. The person who disclosed doesn’t become responsible for explaining every difference between you.

You can still decide whether you’re compatible

Responding respectfully doesn’t mean agreeing to a relationship.

Someone might tell you they need a great deal of alone time, for instance, while you prefer frequent shared activities.

Those preferences may be difficult to combine. That doesn’t make either person defective.

You can respect someone’s diagnosis, believe what they’re telling you, and still decide you aren’t a good romantic match.

Similarly, when you disclose something about yourself, the other person may need time to consider what it means for the relationship.

You cannot require enthusiasm, agreement, or continued romantic interest.

What you can reasonably expect is to be treated with basic respect.

When disclosure is met with disbelief or stigma

Not every reaction is thoughtful.

Someone might insist that you cannot be autistic because you make eye contact. They might claim that ADHD is merely a lack of discipline. They might start treating you as incapable of making your own decisions.

These are examples of assumptions and dismissive reactions, not reliable judgments about the person being discussed.

Autistic adults in qualitative studies have reported disbelief, stereotyping, and other negative experiences after disclosure (Au-Yeung et al., 2025; Gibbs et al., 2026a).

You do not have to convince a date that your diagnosis is legitimate.

You can correct a misunderstanding if you want to:

“Autistic people aren’t all the same. I’m telling you what it means for me.”

You can also decide not to continue the discussion:

“I don’t want to debate my diagnosis.”

And you can choose not to see the person again.

The difference between unfamiliarity and disrespect matters.

Someone who asks what autism means for you may simply be curious. Someone who repeatedly rejects your explanation, mocks you, or treats you as less capable is showing something different.

You don’t have to treat every awkward question as hostility. You also don’t have to remain in a conversation where you’re being treated badly.

Disclosure isn’t a safety guarantee

Some dating-app participants described disclosure as a way to find people who might understand them and avoid people with negative attitudes (Gibbs et al., 2026b).

That can be a reasonable personal motivation.

But the research does not establish disclosure as a reliable safety test.

A person who responds kindly to your diagnosis might still be a poor partner. A person who knows very little about autism or ADHD might be willing to learn.

Likewise, someone who shares your neurotype isn’t automatically safe, considerate, or compatible.

Look at what people actually do over time, not only what they say about a label.

US safety guidance from RAINN recommends being cautious about sharing identifying details such as your address, workplace, or routine with people you don’t yet know well. It also emphasizes that precautions cannot guarantee safety (RAINN, 2026).

You can choose to disclose your neurotype while still protecting other personal information.

Perspective: Women

In one Australian survey of autistic dating-app users, women and nonheterosexual participants reported more or more frequent forms of negative app experiences than the relevant comparison groups. The study was small and self-selected. It did not establish that disclosing autism caused those experiences (Gibbs et al., 2026b).

If you’ve already experienced unwanted sexual messages, hostility, or harassment while dating, wanting more time before sharing private information is understandable. You can prioritize privacy without treating it as a personal failure to be open.

These concerns aren’t exclusive to women. Men and nonbinary people can also face harassment and stigma, and the study did not provide enough nonbinary or agender participants for reliable separate estimates.

For nonbinary and transgender readers, the available dating-disclosure research leaves significant unanswered questions. The Australian sample included nine nonbinary and two agender participants, too few to support strong conclusions about their distinct experiences (Gibbs et al., 2026b). Decisions about whether to share a neurotype, a gender identity, or other personal information should not be treated as interchangeable. Each deserves its own consideration of privacy, trust, and relevance.

What if the other person tells someone else?

Suppose you tell someone you’re autistic during a private conversation. Later, they mention it to a mutual friend without asking.

Even if they meant no harm, you may feel exposed.

You can explain that you wanted the information kept private:

“I shared that with you because I trusted you. I wasn’t ready for other people to know. Please ask me before sharing personal information about me.”

It is also worth remembering this responsibility when you’re the person receiving a disclosure.

Someone telling you about their diagnosis doesn’t automatically give you permission to discuss it with friends, family, coworkers, or people you’re dating.

If you’re unsure whether something is private, ask.

And recognize that once someone knows personal information, you cannot completely control whether they repeat it. That possibility may affect when and with whom you choose to share.

Practice a disclosure before you need it

If deciding what to say feels difficult, try writing a short explanation when you’re alone and not under pressure.

Imagine three situations:

Situation A: You’ve just matched with someone, and they ask why you prefer detailed plans.

Situation B: You’ve been on three dates. You like the person, and you’d like them to understand your neurotype better.

Situation C: Someone you’ve been dating for a while tells you they’re autistic or have ADHD.

Choose whichever situation feels most relevant.

For A or B, write two or three sentences. Include only what you’re comfortable sharing.

For C, write one response that expresses interest without demanding more information.

Then consider:

  • What do you actually want this person to understand?
  • Which details are private for now?
  • Are you sharing information because you want to, or because you feel pressured?
  • What could you do if their response makes you uncomfortable?

For example, your answer might be:

“I have ADHD, and sometimes I struggle with time management. I use calendar reminders because I care about keeping plans. I’d rather explain that than have you think I’m not interested.”

Or simply:

“I prefer direct communication. If you’re unsure what I mean, please ask instead of guessing.”

You can revise your explanation as you learn more about yourself. You don’t need one permanent statement that covers everything.

If the conversation doesn’t go as expected, that doesn’t automatically mean you chose the wrong words. The other person has their own experiences, assumptions, and choices.

Being known is different from being explained

There is a temptation to think that if you can find the perfect explanation of your brain, someone will finally understand you completely.

But people aren’t puzzles with one correct answer.

A diagnosis can explain part of your experience. It cannot tell another person everything about what makes you laugh, what you value, how you show affection, or how you want your future to look.

And even a clear explanation doesn’t replace the ordinary work of learning another person.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book. Telling someone about your neurotype can help them understand the glasses you’re looking through. It doesn’t mean they’ll instantly see everything you see.

You still need to describe your experience. They still need to describe theirs.

Sometimes you’ll misunderstand each other. You can ask questions, clarify, and repair those moments, using the communication skills from the preceding chapters.

Disclosure is one way of being known. It isn’t the only way.

You can be open without sharing everything. You can be private without pretending to be someone else. You can ask someone to understand a practical need even when you’re uncertain how to explain its cause.

And someone can care about you without understanding every part of your experience immediately.

As you continue dating, knowing what to share is only one part of communicating well. Another is knowing what you will accept, what you aren’t comfortable with, and how to express those limits.

Those are boundaries.

The next chapters turn to those messages: what your limits are, how to recognize them, and how to communicate them clearly while respecting someone else’s.
