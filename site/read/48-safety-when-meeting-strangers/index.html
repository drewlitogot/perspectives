Lena is 37 and has been talking to Priya on a dating app for two weeks. The conversations have been easy. They both love old horror movies, neither wants children, and they agree that a quiet Sunday sounds better than a crowded Friday night.

Priya suggests meeting for dinner. Lena wants to say yes.

But then the questions start. Should Priya pick her up? Should Lena tell her roommate where she’s going? Would asking to meet somewhere public make Priya think she doesn’t trust her? What if the dinner goes badly and Lena wants to leave?

None of these questions means Priya has done anything wrong. Lena simply doesn’t know her very well yet.

You can be interested in someone and still take precautions around them.

Meeting a stranger is not a test of how trusting, confident, or adventurous you are. It’s a chance to begin getting to know someone while keeping enough control over your own choices to feel comfortable.

The goal is not to eliminate every possible risk. Nobody can promise that. The goal is to arrange a meeting where you can make decisions freely, ask for what you need, and leave without depending on the person you’ve just met.

The difference between feeling safe and knowing someone

A person can be charming, thoughtful, and easy to talk to without being someone you know well enough to trust in every situation.

You may have exchanged hundreds of messages. You may know their favorite music, their dog’s name, and what happened at their office yesterday. You may have spoken on video.

Those conversations can help you decide whether you want to meet. They don’t tell you everything about how the person will behave face-to-face.

Research offers some reasons for reasonable caution, but the numbers need careful interpretation.

A 2023 Pew Research Center report found that 48% of Americans who had ever used an online dating site or app reported at least one of four unwanted behaviors: receiving unwanted sexual messages or images, continued contact after expressing disinterest, offensive name-calling, or threats of physical harm. Six percent reported threats of physical harm (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

These are experiences involving online dating platforms, not statistics about violence during first dates. You cannot use them to calculate the chance that your own date will be dangerous.

One survey of 1,310 college students also documented victimization associated with people first encountered through social media. But the study involved one university, was based on self-reports, and did not establish how dangerous an individual first date might be (Fansher & Randa, 2019).

There is no reliable first-date danger percentage in the research reviewed for this chapter.

Another important reality is that harm doesn’t come exclusively from strangers. The CDC’s information on stalking shows that people who are known to someone, including current or former intimate partners and acquaintances, can also be responsible (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025).

Knowing someone longer is therefore not a guarantee of safety, either.

The practical lesson is simple: don’t judge safety entirely by how familiar someone feels.

Pay attention to what they actually do, especially when your preferences differ from theirs.

Make the first meeting easy to leave

Consider two arrangements.

In the first, someone you haven’t met offers to drive you to a restaurant outside town. You’ll spend the journey together, have dinner, and rely on them to bring you home.

In the second, you meet at a café near a transit stop. You arrange your own transportation, and you can leave whenever you’re ready.

Both could turn into enjoyable dates. But the second gives you more control over when you arrive, how long you stay, and how you get home.

RAINN, love is respect, and the National Domestic Violence Hotline recommend public meeting places and independent transportation as precautions for early dates (love is respect, n.d.; National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.; RAINN, 2026b).

These recommendations come from organizations experienced in supporting people who have faced violence or abuse. They are professional guidance, not findings from experiments proving that a particular arrangement prevents a certain percentage of assaults.

Still, they offer useful ways to organize your choices.

Choose a public place

For an early meeting, consider a place where other people are present, you can ask for assistance, and you can leave without needing to negotiate with your date.

A staffed café, casual restaurant, or accessible community venue may work. The location should be reachable and practical for you, not merely familiar to the other person.

A public place doesn’t have to be crowded or noisy.

If loud music makes it hard to think, a packed bar isn’t your only option. A quieter café with staff nearby might let you enjoy the conversation while keeping the advantages of a public setting.

Likewise, a secluded trail might sound peaceful, but being alone in an unfamiliar area with somebody you’ve never met introduces dependencies that a public meeting avoids.

This isn’t a rule that nature walks are bad dates. It’s a reason to consider where and with whom you want to take them.

Public does not mean guaranteed safe. Other people being nearby doesn’t ensure that someone will intervene if something goes wrong. It does, however, give you the option of seeking assistance.

Arrange your own way there and back

Having your own transportation doesn’t necessarily mean having your own car.

You might drive, use public transit, arrange a rideshare, or ask someone you trust for a lift.

The important question is whether you have a realistic way home that doesn’t require your date’s cooperation.

Before leaving, consider what happens if the meeting ends earlier than expected. Are buses still running? Can you afford the return trip? Is the pickup location accessible? Do you need to arrange a ride ahead of time?

If you use a mobility aid, need assistance with transportation, or live somewhere with limited transit, your options may be narrower. That’s a practical constraint, not a personal failing.

You might choose a location closer to home without revealing your address, arrange a separate ride through someone you trust, or postpone until a workable arrangement is available.

Marco, 44, is meeting Ben for coffee. Ben offers to collect him from his apartment. Marco would rather drive himself.

He replies:

“I’m looking forward to meeting you. I’ll meet you at the café at two. I prefer to arrange my own transportation at first.”

That’s enough.

Marco doesn’t need to present a history of bad experiences or persuade Ben that the preference is reasonable.

Someone who respects the arrangement has given Marco one useful piece of information about how they respond to his choices.

Keep your home address private for now

You don’t need to share your exact home address with somebody just because you’ve agreed to meet.

Meeting at the destination lets you get acquainted without making your home part of the arrangement.

Similarly, if you’re traveling, you don’t need to disclose the address of your hotel or other accommodation.

This isn’t about hiding who you are forever. It’s about sharing private information gradually, as trust develops.

RAINN and love is respect include location privacy among their dating safety recommendations (love is respect, n.d.; RAINN, 2026b).

You can be friendly and open while keeping some details private.

Let somebody know your plan, if you safely can

Before a first meeting, consider telling someone you trust where you’ll be.

It doesn’t have to be a dramatic announcement.

You might send a friend the meeting location, the approximate time, and when you expect to finish. You can agree on a simple check-in afterward.

For example:

“Meeting someone for coffee at Maple Café at 2. Planning to head home around 3:30. I’ll message you when I’m back.”

This gives someone information they can use if you ask for help or don’t check in as expected.

RAINN recommends informing a trusted person of your date plans and having a way to seek assistance (RAINN, 2026b).

You can also decide together what a missed check-in means. Perhaps your friend will send a message first and call if they don’t hear back.

An optional code word can be helpful if you’d rather request assistance without explaining a situation in front of someone else.

But don’t build a complicated system that you and your friend won’t understand or remember. A simple agreement is easier to use.

What if you don’t have a trusted person?

Not everybody has a friend, roommate, or family member who can safely receive this information.

Some people live alone. Some have limited social support. Others have relatives who monitor their activities or might react badly to learning they’re dating.

You don’t have to disclose your plans to someone who isn’t safe for you.

You can instead put more emphasis on arrangements you control: a familiar public venue, transportation you can access independently, a charged phone, and enough money or another means to cover your trip home.

If a trusted contact becomes available, you can add a check-in. It is one precaution among several, not a requirement for being allowed to date.

Be selective with location sharing

Digital tools can be useful, but they require care.

Sharing a location with a trusted person for a limited purpose is different from giving a new acquaintance ongoing access to your movements.

The National Network to End Domestic Violence’s Safety Net Project addresses how technology and location information can be used both for personal safety and for unwanted monitoring (National Network to End Domestic Violence, n.d.).

Decide who receives your location, why, and for how long. You don’t need to post your plans publicly or turn on permanent tracking to demonstrate that you’re being careful.

If someone you are meeting insists on having access to your live location, that is a request you can decline.

Look at behavior, not just chemistry

Some people try to decide whether a stranger is safe by asking whether they give off a good or bad feeling.

Feelings matter. You don’t need to ignore discomfort.

But feelings are not always clear. You might feel anxious around nearly everybody new. You might feel comfortable around someone who is behaving badly. You might notice that something bothered you only hours later.

You don’t have to solve that uncertainty by becoming an expert at reading facial expressions or body language.

Instead, look for observable behavior.

Suppose you say you’d rather meet in a public place.

Does the person accept that?

If they suggest somewhere else, do they listen when you explain what would work for you?

Do they treat your transportation arrangements as your decision?

Do they accept it if you aren’t ready to meet yet?

The useful distinction is between someone who is awkward and someone who doesn’t respect your choices.

A shy person might make little eye contact. Someone who is nervous might talk too fast or stumble over their words. Someone who communicates directly might sound unusually formal.

None of those behaviors, by themselves, tells you that the person is dangerous.

Repeatedly ignoring your boundary is different.

Professional dating-safety guidance identifies unwanted pressure, controlling behavior, demands for private meetings, and disregard for stated limits as reasons to reconsider an encounter (love is respect, n.d.; RAINN, 2026b).

For example, suppose you propose meeting at a public café.

The other person suggests a private location instead. You repeat that you only want to meet in public.

If they accept your answer, the disagreement may end there.

If they repeatedly push for the private location, belittle your concern, or make your willingness to meet into a test of affection, you can stop arranging the date.

You don’t have to determine their hidden intention.

The behavior itself is enough to decide that the arrangement doesn’t work for you.

And you don’t need to wait for behavior to become obviously threatening before deciding not to meet.

What if you’re the person applying pressure?

Perhaps you are excited about a meeting and keep trying to change somebody’s mind after they’ve told you what they want.

Stop asking.

The other person’s boundary is not a problem you need to solve. Your excitement doesn’t entitle you to an exception.

Accept their answer. If you crossed a boundary, take responsibility without demanding reassurance or another chance.

If respecting other people’s refusals is a recurring difficulty, seek help developing that skill before continuing to date.

A person is free to decline a meeting for any reason, including reasons you don’t understand.

Privacy, alcohol, and consent

A date can change direction unexpectedly.

Someone might suggest another location, offer a drink, ask for physical affection, or want to spend the rest of the evening together.

None of these possibilities creates an obligation.

Agreeing to coffee doesn’t mean agreeing to dinner afterward. Accepting a meal doesn’t mean agreeing to physical contact. Traveling to meet someone doesn’t mean agreeing to go home with them.

Every additional activity is a new choice.

You can say:

“I’d rather stay here.”

“I’m going to head home after this.”

“I’m not comfortable with that.”

These are complete decisions. They don’t require a convincing explanation.

Alcohol and other drugs deserve special attention because intoxication can affect people’s ability to make decisions and consent. RAINN advises being mindful of alcohol and maintaining awareness of your drinks during dates (RAINN, 2026b).

If you choose to drink, think about how you will get home and whether you can still make decisions comfortably. You can also choose not to drink at all.

If somebody pressures you to drink more than you want, that is reason to reconsider continuing the date.

If another person appears too impaired to make a freely informed decision, do not initiate or continue sexual activity. U.S. laws governing sexual consent and incapacity differ by state; RAINN maintains a state-by-state comparison (RAINN, 2026a).

The full discussion of consent belongs earlier in this book. Here, one point is especially important: your ability to leave and your ability to refuse must remain real choices, not merely polite ideas.

Nothing about drinking, accepting a ride, or entering a private location makes a person responsible for someone else’s coercion or assault.

Precautions are options for protecting yourself. They are never a measure of whether someone deserves protection.

Different people, different safety considerations

Safety isn’t experienced from exactly the same position by everyone.

Gender, past experiences, disability, communication differences, and access to transportation can all influence what precautions feel possible or necessary.

The same basic principle still applies: you deserve respect, and the person you’re meeting does too.

Women and harassment concerns

Perspective: Women

In Pew’s 2023 report, 66% of women under 50 who had used dating apps or sites reported at least one of four unwanted online behaviors. Women using online dating also rated its safety less favorably than men did. These are findings about online dating experiences and perceptions, not a measure of how likely a woman is to be assaulted on a first date (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

If previous experiences have made you cautious, you don’t need to minimize that caution to appear agreeable. Meeting in public, arranging your own transport, or asking for more time before meeting are reasonable choices. You don’t need to make yourself more vulnerable to prove that you’re interested.

Men can need protection too

Perspective: Men

Men also experience unwanted attention, coercion, violence, and stalking. The CDC estimates that about one in ten U.S. men have experienced stalking during their lifetime. That figure covers stalking in many circumstances, not specifically dates, and shouldn’t be used to predict your personal risk (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025).

If you feel embarrassed about taking precautions, remember that confidence doesn’t require putting yourself in a situation you cannot leave independently. You can tell a friend where you’re going, decline an unwanted advance, or leave a date that makes you uncomfortable. These choices are available to you regardless of your size, strength, or the gender of the person you’re meeting.

Nonbinary and gender-diverse readers

Perspective: Non-binary

A safety plan should not require you to disclose your gender identity to people you don’t trust. You may not be open about your identity with family, coworkers, or everyone in your social circle. Choose carefully whom you tell about a date and what information you share.

Existing dating-app research provides limited information specifically about nonbinary people’s first-meeting safety. One study of autistic dating-app users included only nine nonbinary and two agender participants, too few to support reliable stand-alone conclusions for those groups (Gibbs et al., 2026).

You can make disclosure decisions at your own pace while still asking for a public location, independent transportation, and respect for your boundaries. Neither your identity nor your relationship structure creates an obligation to accept an unsafe arrangement.

If you prefer clear rules to reading someone’s intentions

If you find it hard to tell whether someone is trustworthy

You might find it difficult to decide whether a person is flirting, joking, impatient, or pressuring you. Some autistic adults also report experiences of online victimization. In one small, self-selected dating-app survey, 40% reported unsolicited sexually explicit messages and 43% reported verbal abuse. That study did not measure danger at first in-person meetings or establish that autism caused those experiences (Gibbs et al., 2026).

A Canadian study found differences in sexual knowledge and reported victimization between its autistic and comparison samples, although it could not establish cause and effect (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

Rather than relying entirely on interpreting someone’s intentions, decide what arrangements you are comfortable with ahead of time. A written location, a time to meet, and a way to leave give you concrete information. If someone repeatedly ignores a clearly stated limit, you don’t have to figure out why before protecting that limit.

If remembering several arrangements is difficult, put the meeting address, transit details, and check-in reminder in your phone or calendar. This is a practical organizational tool, not a requirement associated with any diagnosis.

If you’ve had unsafe relationships before

If saying no feels difficult

If past experiences have made disagreement frightening, you might find it hard to object in the moment. You may agree automatically and only realize afterward that you weren’t comfortable. You don’t need to identify a particular trauma response or diagnosis to use a safety plan.

Choose one or two boundaries before meeting and decide how you’ll act if they aren’t respected. You can arrange independent transportation and give yourself permission to leave without negotiating. The National Domestic Violence Hotline emphasizes moving at your own pace and having access to support when dating after abuse (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

You don’t have to prove that you’re fully healed before you’re allowed to meet people. You can also decide that you’re not ready, or that you need a different arrangement.

If you’re dating someone who needs explicit plans

If you're dating someone who wants everything arranged clearly

Your date might ask for the exact address, prefer written details, want to know when the meeting will finish, or need to arrange transportation separately. They might also prefer a quiet public place or request that you don’t touch them without asking.

Treat these as ordinary preferences and boundaries. They are not evidence that the person dislikes you, doubts your character, or needs to be persuaded to relax.

You can support a comfortable meeting by agreeing on clear details, answering practical questions, and accepting the other person’s choices. You don’t need an explanation of their neurotype to respect what they’ve asked for.

You are allowed to change your mind

Suppose you’ve been looking forward to meeting somebody all week.

On the morning of the date, they change the venue from a public restaurant to their home. You say you’re not comfortable with the change. They continue asking.

You might feel disappointed. You might worry about losing the opportunity. You might think you’ve already agreed to meet, so changing your mind would be unfair.

But agreeing to one arrangement does not require you to accept a different one.

You can reply:

“I’m only comfortable meeting somewhere public. If that doesn’t work, I’ll pass.”

You can also postpone without offering an alternative immediately.

You aren’t required to continue a conversation about plans that no longer work for you.

The same principle applies once the date begins. If you become uncomfortable, you can end the meeting. You don’t owe another person your continued company because they traveled, paid for something, or expected a longer evening.

RAINN’s dating-safety guidance affirms that you can leave a situation that feels unsafe without providing an explanation (RAINN, 2026b).

If your discomfort is difficult to name, you can use a simpler test: Do I still want to be here?

You don’t have to prove that the other person is dangerous to answer no.

If something goes wrong

Sometimes a meeting becomes uncomfortable but not dangerous. Someone may be rude, dismissive, or unwilling to accept your preferences.

You can end the encounter and go home.

If somebody threatens you, refuses to let you leave, or creates an immediate safety concern, focus on getting assistance rather than settling the disagreement.

If possible, move toward other people and seek help from staff or someone you trust. In immediate danger in the United States, call 911.

You can ask a staff member for help, but don’t assume that every worker or bystander will recognize the situation or know what to do.

If somebody follows you, repeatedly contacts you after you’ve asked them to stop, monitors your whereabouts, or appears uninvited at places you visit, take the pattern seriously.

The Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center explains that stalking generally involves a course of conduct that causes fear or substantial emotional distress. Legal definitions differ among states and tribal jurisdictions, so not every unpleasant interaction legally constitutes stalking (Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.).

You don’t need to make that legal determination yourself to seek advice or help.

Keep relevant communications or other records if it is safe to do so. A survivor-support organization can help you consider available options, including reporting and safety planning.

If you’ve experienced sexual assault or coercion, RAINN provides confidential support and guidance about options. The National Domestic Violence Hotline can assist with relationship abuse and related safety concerns (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.; RAINN, 2026b).

U.S. resources include RAINN at rainn.org and the National Domestic Violence Hotline at thehotline.org.

You can seek support even if you are uncertain what to call what happened.

Detailed guidance on stalking, sexual assault, and crisis resources appears in Chapters 32, 35, and 60. This chapter focuses on recognizing when assistance may be needed and knowing that you have options.

And if something harmful happens despite every precaution, the responsibility belongs to the person who caused the harm.

A safety plan is not a contract promising that nothing bad can happen. It is not a test you can fail.

Your two-minute meeting check

Before leaving to meet someone new, run through these questions:

  • Place: Am I meeting somewhere public, accessible, and reasonably comfortable for me?
  • Transportation: Do I know how I’m getting there and how I can get home independently?
  • Information: Have I decided what personal details I want to keep private for now?
  • Support: If it’s safe and possible, does someone I trust know my plans or have a way to hear from me?
  • Exit: Do I have a realistic option for ending the meeting if I want to?

You don’t need an elaborate procedure or a perfect answer to every question. The checklist is a way to notice problems early, while you still have time to change the arrangement.

For an even shorter version, remember three things:

Meet somewhere you can get help. Keep control of your way home. Give yourself permission to leave.

Lena decides to meet Priya at a café instead of arranging a pickup. She tells her roommate when she expects to return and checks the bus schedule. Priya agrees to the location without making it an issue.

That response doesn’t prove Priya is trustworthy in every respect. Lena hasn’t known her long enough to conclude that.

But the arrangement lets them meet without asking either person to surrender independence.

They can concentrate on getting to know each other rather than worrying about how to escape an inconvenient situation.

Safety doesn’t have to turn every first meeting into an investigation. It can simply mean setting up the conditions where two people have room to make their own choices.

You’ve found someone you’d like to meet, and you know how to arrange that meeting with care. The next step is deciding what kind of date will actually work for you.
