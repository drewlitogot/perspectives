Chapter 39: Showing interest clearly

You have been talking to someone for a few weeks. You enjoy their company. You remember little things they tell you. You look forward to seeing them.

You think you have made your interest obvious.

Then you discover they had no idea you wanted to date them.

Or perhaps the situation is reversed. You thought you were simply being friendly, and someone interpreted your attention as romantic interest.

Neither person necessarily did anything wrong. The problem is that the same behavior can communicate different things to different people.

A smile can mean friendliness. A compliment can mean admiration. Spending time together can mean friendship, attraction, curiosity, or simply enjoying the activity. One study of flirting examined several motives that people attributed to apparently similar interactions, including romance, playfulness, exploration, and sexual interest (Henningsen, 2004).

The person standing on the other side of the sculpture may not see what you see.

If you want someone to understand that you are romantically interested, you can make that information available without requiring them to interpret a collection of hints.

Showing interest clearly means making your intentions understandable while leaving the other person free to choose what happens next.

It does not mean confessing every feeling, demanding an answer, or trying to make yourself irresistible.

It means giving someone an honest invitation.

Friendly and romantic are not the same message

Consider three statements:

“You’re really fun to talk to.”

“It would be nice to hang out sometime.”

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you be interested in going on a date with me?”

The first is a compliment. The second suggests spending time together, but does not say what kind of time together. The third communicates romantic interest and asks a question the other person can answer.

All three can be appropriate. They simply communicate different things.

If you are trying to build a friendship, the first two may say exactly what you mean. If you want a date, the third leaves less room for misunderstanding.

This distinction matters especially if you have spent years expressing interest through actions instead of words.

Perhaps you show affection by remembering someone’s favorite food, fixing something that is broken, helping with a task, or sharing information about a subject they love. Those can be meaningful ways of showing care.

But they do not automatically communicate romance.

Someone might appreciate everything you do and still believe you are being a good friend. They have not failed to notice a secret agreement. No agreement was made.

The reverse is also important. If someone offers you help, asks thoughtful questions, remembers your birthday, or wants to spend time together, you cannot conclude from those actions alone that they want to date you.

The next two chapters will examine how to interpret other people’s interest and what happens when friendship and romance are difficult to distinguish.

For now, concentrate on the part you control: making your own meaning clear.

You do not need a clever opening line

Some dating advice makes approaching someone sound like a performance.

You are supposed to be witty, mysterious, confident, surprising, or unusually charming. The opening sentence is treated as an examination where one wrong answer ruins everything.

That is an exhausting way to begin a conversation.

It is also not what the available research suggests you need.

In a 1986 study, Kleinke and colleagues asked three groups of respondents to rate different kinds of opening lines. They compared direct lines, ordinary low-key openings, and clever or flippant pickup lines. Across their samples, direct and ordinary openings were generally rated more favorably than the clever, flippant ones (Kleinke et al., 1986).

A 1989 study, which included actual approaches in bars as well as a laboratory experiment, also found more negative reactions to flippant lines than to direct or ordinary approaches, especially among the women approached (Cunningham, 1989).

These studies are old and largely reflect heterosexual interactions in university, laboratory, and bar settings. They cannot establish what every person prefers today.

Still, they challenge an assumption worth questioning: that you must say something extraordinary to deserve someone’s attention.

“Hi, how’s your evening going?” is a perfectly reasonable way to begin an ordinary conversation.

And after you have gotten to know someone:

“I’ve really enjoyed talking with you. Would you like to go on a date?”

That is enough to make your interest understandable.

Being direct does not require being serious all the time, either. Hall and colleagues studied five overlapping styles of flirting: sincere, polite, playful, physical, and traditional. Their study found different associations between these styles and people’s self-reported dating experiences (Hall et al., 2010).

Someone can be playful and sincere. Humor is not the problem. Pretending to be someone else, relying on a rehearsed performance, or using jokes in place of communicating your actual intentions may leave the other person unsure what you mean.

If humor comes naturally, you can use it. If it does not, you can speak normally.

There is no requirement to become a different personality before asking someone out.

What a clear invitation sounds like

A useful invitation usually communicates three things:

  1. Your intention: You are interested in a date, not merely an unspecified meeting.
  2. Your suggestion: You are proposing a possible activity or occasion.
  3. Their choice: The other person can accept, decline, or take time to decide.

These are practical communication guidelines, not a scientifically tested formula.

For example:

“I’ve enjoyed getting to know you. Would you like to get coffee with me as a date sometime?”

Or:

“I think you’re interesting, and I’d like to get to know you romantically. Would you be interested in going out?”

Or, if you already know each other reasonably well:

“I like spending time with you, and I’m interested in dating you. Is that something you’d want too?”

Notice what these invitations do not contain.

They do not promise an extraordinary future together. They do not ask the other person to judge your worth. They do not require an immediate emotional commitment.

They ask whether the other person wants to explore a possibility.

You can say what you want without declaring everything you feel

Imagine that Isabel, 41, has enjoyed talking with another woman, Renee, at several gatherings.

Isabel finds herself thinking about Renee between meetings. She is attracted to her and wants to see whether they might enjoy dating.

Isabel could make a long declaration about how unusual and important these feelings are.

But Renee does not need Isabel’s entire emotional history to answer the immediate question.

Isabel says:

“I’ve really liked talking with you. Would you be interested in getting dinner together as a date?”

That tells Renee what Isabel wants to know.

It also keeps the decision manageable.

Renee is being asked about dinner, not whether she wants to become Isabel’s life partner.

This is an important distinction if you tend to develop strong feelings before you know someone well. Intensity does not have to become the opening message.

You can have a strong feeling and still make a small invitation.

Be clear about the word “date”

Some people avoid saying date because the word feels too vulnerable.

Instead, they ask whether someone wants to “hang out,” “grab something,” or “do something sometime.”

Those invitations can be perfectly good when the intention is casual or friendly. But when the romantic meaning matters, they can leave both people uncertain.

Imagine that Marcus asks Devin to see a movie.

Devin agrees, thinking they are going as friends.

Marcus believes Devin has accepted a date.

Both arrive expecting different experiences.

One way Marcus could have made his intention clearer is:

“Would you like to see that movie together as a date?”

The word date does not guarantee agreement or attraction. It simply identifies the kind of invitation being offered.

It also gives Devin a chance to decide whether that is what they want.

If saying date feels unfamiliar, you can practice it privately until the word becomes less difficult to use. You do not need to replace it with an elaborate explanation.

Make room for a real answer

A clear invitation is not automatically a comfortable invitation.

There is a difference between understanding what someone wants and feeling free to decline it.

In two experiments, Joel and colleagues found that participants were less willing to reject a potential partner when they believed the opportunity was real rather than hypothetical. Concern about hurting the other person’s feelings contributed to that difference (Joel et al., 2014).

The finding is not a test of particular invitation wording. But it is a useful reminder that turning someone down may feel harder in the actual moment than it does in theory.

You might be prepared to hear no. The other person may not know that yet.

That is one reason an invitation should not make the recipient responsible for your emotional wellbeing.

Compare these two situations.

In the first, someone asks for a date, receives a refusal, and responds respectfully.

In the second, the person receiving the invitation feels responsible for preventing a serious emotional reaction.

The words of the invitation might have been equally clear. The freedom to refuse was not.

A genuine invitation offers a choice. It does not create a debt.

You can help preserve that choice by asking plainly, allowing time to answer, and not treating uncertainty as permission to continue asking.

You do not have to announce, “You are completely free to say no,” every time. That sentence may be sincere, but it cannot cancel an uncomfortable setting, a power difference, or the other person’s concerns about safety.

If the invitation happens in a situation where someone depends on you, cannot comfortably leave, or has reason to worry about consequences, the problem is larger than choosing polite words. Chapter 43 covers when and where an approach is appropriate.

What the answer means

A yes means the person has agreed to what you actually proposed.

If you asked them to have coffee as a date, a yes means they have agreed to have coffee as a date.

It does not establish that they want physical affection, sex, exclusivity, or another date.

RAINN’s sexual-consent guidance is clear that flirting and previous dates do not amount to consent to sexual activity (RAINN, 2026).

A no means the invitation has been declined.

A vague or uncertain answer is not a hidden yes. You can allow the person time to decide without asking repeatedly.

If they do not respond, leave the invitation unanswered rather than continuing to pursue it.

And if someone declines, you do not need to discover the perfect explanation.

“Thanks for being clear. I respect that.”

That is a complete response.

You may feel disappointed, embarrassed, or sad. Those feelings can be real without becoming the other person’s responsibility.

If you notice yourself repeatedly pursuing someone after a refusal or lack of response, stop. Do not search for better wording or another way to reach them. If stopping is difficult, seek support with managing the feelings and behavior rather than involving the person further.

Showing interest before asking for a date

Not every conversation needs to begin with a romantic declaration.

Sometimes you are interested enough to learn more about someone but not yet sure whether you want a date.

That is fine.

You can show ordinary, genuine interest in the person.

Suppose Andre, 36, meets Thomas, 39, through a local walking group.

Thomas mentions that he has been restoring an old bicycle. Andre enjoys hearing about the project.

The next time they meet, Andre asks:

“How’s the bicycle coming along? Did you get the brakes working?”

Thomas tells him about a frustrating afternoon and a successful repair.

Andre listens, asks another relevant question, and shares something about a project of his own.

Nothing in that conversation requires either person to decide whether the interaction is romantic.

Andre is simply getting to know Thomas.

A 2017 paper by Huang and colleagues found, across several studies, that asking questions, particularly follow-up questions, was associated with greater liking and perceived responsiveness in conversations. The research included a heterosexual speed-dating study. A 2025 correction addressed minor reporting errors without changing the paper’s substantive conclusions (Correction to ‘It doesn’t hurt to ask: Question-asking increases liking’ by Huang et al, 2017; Huang et al., 2017).

This does not mean questions are a method for producing romantic attraction.

It means that taking an interest in what someone says can make a conversation more engaging.

A person is not a collection of facts you need to extract. Their answers are part of a conversation, and you can respond with your own experiences and opinions.

Interest can be communicated through listening, remembering, curiosity, and making time for another conversation.

It can also be communicated through a simple compliment:

“You have a really interesting way of looking at that.”

Or:

“I enjoyed talking with you yesterday.”

Those statements are useful because they express something true without assigning a romantic meaning the other person has not agreed to.

But there is a limit to how much ordinary friendliness can communicate.

If you eventually want the person to understand that you are romantically interested, say so.

Someone cannot be expected to assemble years of friendly interactions into a romantic invitation you never actually made.

If being direct feels unusually difficult

For some people, asking clearly is less difficult than interpreting hints.

For others, stating an intention out loud is the hard part.

You might know exactly what you want to say when you are alone, then lose the words when the person is standing in front of you.

You might need extra time to work out what you feel.

You might be comfortable sharing an activity but find face-to-face emotional conversations exhausting.

You might find that a busy room creates overload, making even a simple question difficult to form.

None of these experiences prevents you from communicating interest.

In interviews with 28 autistic young adults in the United States, participants described challenges with implicit social cues alongside strengths in acceptance and empathy. Some wanted more explicit education about dating and flirting (Kohn et al., 2025).

A small UK study involving 16 gender- and sexually diverse autistic adults also described the value of explicit communication, mutual understanding, and defining relationship expectations together (Ciric et al., 2026).

These studies do not establish one correct communication style for autistic people. They show why the assumption that everyone should naturally understand unwritten dating rules deserves questioning.

You can choose a form of communication that works for you.

A written invitation is still an invitation

Lena and Sam are both autistic adults in their early thirties.

They enjoy talking at a weekend art group, but both find it easier to work through important questions in writing.

After several conversations, Lena sends Sam a message:

“I’ve really liked getting to know you. Would you be interested in going to the art museum together as a date?”

Sam replies the next morning:

“Yes. That sounds good. Could we choose a quieter time?”

Lena answers:

“Absolutely. Would a weekday afternoon work?”

There is nothing defective or incomplete about their communication because the invitation happened through messages.

They have made their intentions understandable, expressed their preferences, and reached a mutual decision.

This is an example of an option, not a proven method that works better for autistic people. The research on autistic adults and online dating describes both opportunities for a more manageable communication pace and difficulties with unwritten rules and transitions to meeting in person (Neumann et al., 2024).

You can use writing whether you are autistic, neurotypical, have ADHD, or simply prefer to consider your words.

The important question is whether the other person is comfortable communicating that way too.

You do not have to perform confidence

Direct communication sometimes gets confused with confident presentation.

People imagine that asking someone out requires steady eye contact, a relaxed voice, quick timing, and no visible nervousness.

It does not.

You can speak quietly.

You can look away while thinking.

You can pause.

You can say, “I’m a little nervous asking this.”

You can read a sentence you prepared.

The message can still be clear.

You do not need to spend all your energy masking your natural communication style to qualify for a date.

And if you are the person receiving an invitation, the other person’s manner is not a test they must pass.

If someone expresses interest differently from how you expect

Someone may communicate interest with carefully chosen words, limited eye contact, a quiet voice, or a written message rather than the facial expressions or tone you expect. Those differences do not, by themselves, establish either romantic interest or disinterest.

Listen to what the person is actually saying. If the meaning is unclear, ask. If they need time to find the words, give them time without interpreting the delay for them. You can explain your own preferences as well. Clear communication is a shared responsibility, not a task assigned to whichever partner finds social hints harder.

Who is supposed to make the first move?

Some people are taught that asking someone out is their responsibility.

Others are taught to wait to be chosen.

These expectations can make dating confusing even when two people are genuinely interested in each other.

Hall and colleagues surveyed 5,020 heterosexual adults using an online dating service. Their study identified a traditional flirting style associated with expectations such as men initiating romantic contact. The study also found average differences between men’s and women’s scores on its five flirting-style measures (Hall et al., 2010).

Another study examined a restricted sample of US college students surveyed between 2005 and 2011. Among the qualifying respondents, 88% agreed that women could appropriately ask men on dates, yet 89.1% of the qualifying most-recent dates were reported as initiated by men (Kendrick & Kepple, 2022).

Those dates were drawn from a specific group: unmarried, childless, heterosexual, cisgender students reporting dates that involved sexual activity. The results cannot describe all dating, let alone establish a rule for dating in 2026.

They do illustrate a distinction between approving of a behavior and routinely doing it.

A social expectation can remain influential even when people do not consider it fair or necessary.

Perspective: Men

If you are a man dating women, you may have been taught that initiating is your job. Some heterosexual dating studies reflect that traditional expectation (Hall et al., 2010; Kendrick & Kepple, 2022).

But you do not need to approach every person you find attractive, conceal nervousness, or prove confidence through persistence. Making an invitation is an option, not a test of masculinity. You can also appreciate someone else making the first move. Your responsibility, when you do ask, is to be clear and respectful, not to produce a yes.

Perspective: Women

If you are a woman dating men, you may have encountered an expectation that you should show interest indirectly and wait to be asked. You are allowed to initiate. You do not have to make someone guess what you want.

Safety and comfort still matter. In a US Pew survey reported in 2020, 65% of single women looking to date, compared with 50% of similarly situated men, reported experiencing at least one of six specified forms of dating harassment (Brown, 2020). These are group averages, not descriptions of every woman’s experiences. You can choose when, where, and whether to express interest, and you can decline attention without providing reassurance or explanation.

Perspective: Non-binary

If you are non-binary, familiar dating scripts may not describe your experience at all. You may encounter people who assume you should initiate, people who assume you should wait, or people who try to assign you a role based on how they perceive your gender.

You do not need to accept those assumptions. A small UK interview study of gender- and sexually diverse autistic adults described participants defining relationships and expectations in ways that worked for them rather than relying on fixed scripts (Ciric et al., 2026). That study cannot establish how non-binary people generally initiate dates, but the practical choice is available to you: ask when you want to ask, and discuss preferences rather than assigning roles.

The same principle applies to same-gender couples, bisexual people, and anyone whose relationships do not match a traditional man-asks-woman-waits pattern.

Neither person’s gender determines who must initiate.

A relationship does not need a designated pursuer and a designated person being pursued.

Either person can express interest. Either person can decline.

And both people can contribute to whatever happens next.

Interest is not access

There is an important difference between showing someone that you want to know them and treating their attention as something you have earned.

Perhaps you remember every conversation, help with several projects, and consistently make yourself available.

You may feel that you have demonstrated how much you care.

But the other person has not agreed to a romantic relationship simply because you have invested time or effort.

Your actions cannot substitute for their choice.

Likewise, someone accepting an invitation to dinner does not mean they have agreed to physical affection.

Being attracted to someone does not entitle you to their photographs, private information, time, or attention.

These distinctions matter online as much as they do in person.

A 2026 survey of 102 autistic adults who had used dating apps described meaningful connections alongside unwanted explicit messages, abuse, and stigma. The participants were largely Australian and self-selected, so the results cannot be treated as population rates or as specifically representative of US dating (Gibbs et al., 2026).

The practical standard remains the same: a romantic message should communicate interest without demanding access or an outcome.

You can be enthusiastic without becoming intrusive.

You can be attracted to someone without making sexual comments or sending sexual material they have not invited.

You can hope for a reply without treating silence as a problem you must solve.

And you can keep treating someone respectfully when they do not want what you want.

When you are interested but not completely sure

A romantic invitation does not have to mean you already know that this person is right for you.

Dating is partly a way to find that out.

Consider Priya, 52, who has recently started meeting people after many years without dating.

She enjoys talking with Michael, who is 55. He seems kind, and they share an interest in local history.

Priya does not know whether they would make a good couple.

She also does not know whether she would feel strong romantic attraction after spending more time with him.

She can still decide that a date would be worth exploring.

“I’ve enjoyed our conversations. Would you be interested in going to the history museum together as a date?”

That invitation does not promise that a relationship will develop.

It expresses a present interest in finding out whether one might.

You do not need certainty about the future to ask someone on a date.

You need enough interest to want to spend time together in that context, and a willingness to respect the answer.

What if you only realize your interest afterward?

Sometimes feelings become clear slowly.

You may have enjoyed talking with someone but needed several hours or days to recognize that you wanted to see them again.

You might realize that you were attracted to them only after the conversation ended.

There is no requirement to identify every feeling immediately.

If you have an appropriate, already-established way to contact the person, you can communicate your interest later.

For example:

“I really enjoyed our conversation on Saturday. Would you like to meet for coffee as a date?”

If contacting them would require intruding on their privacy, bypassing a boundary, or using information they did not give you for personal contact, do not do that.

You can miss an opportunity without turning it into a reason to cross someone’s boundary.

A small exercise: Say what you actually mean

You can practice expressing interest without involving another person.

Choose a sentence you might naturally say, then ask whether it communicates the intention you actually have.

Example 1: The friendly compliment

Ambiguous:

“You’re really nice. I enjoy spending time with you.”

Clearer if your intention is romantic:

“I enjoy spending time with you, and I’d be interested in going on a date if you are.”

Example 2: The unspecified invitation

Ambiguous:

“We should get coffee sometime.”

Clearer:

“Would you like to get coffee together as a date next weekend?”

Example 3: The indirect hint

Ambiguous:

“I wish I had someone to go to the concert with.”

Clearer:

“I’m interested in you romantically. Would you like to go to the concert together as a date?”

The clearer versions are examples, not mandatory scripts.

You might never use the words romantically interested. You might find the phrase go on a date much more natural.

The goal is not to memorize a particular sentence. It is to communicate an honest intention in words that fit you.

Write one invitation of your own

Imagine someone you have gotten to know, enjoy talking with, and would genuinely like to date. This can be a fictional person if practicing with a real situation feels too personal.

Write one or two sentences inviting them on a date.

Then check your invitation against four questions:

  1. Is the romantic intention understandable? Could the person reasonably mistake this for an ordinary friendly outing?
  2. Is the invitation manageable? Are you proposing a date rather than asking for a commitment to a relationship?
  3. Can the person freely decline? Does your wording or the imagined situation make them responsible for your feelings or wellbeing?
  4. Will you respect the answer? Are you prepared to stop asking if the answer is no, uncertain, or absent?

If the answer to any question is no, revise the invitation.

For example, you might start with:

“I’d love to spend some time with you.”

After revising it:

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you like to get lunch together as a date?”

Then practice saying it aloud, typing it, or reading it from a note.

Your voice does not have to be steady. The sentence does not have to be impressive.

It needs to communicate what you actually mean.

The goal is understanding, not persuasion

When you express romantic interest, you are doing something useful even if the other person declines.

Before you ask, your intentions may be known only to you.

After you ask, the other person has the information needed to decide whether they want the same thing.

That is meaningful progress in communication, whatever the answer.

A successful invitation is not necessarily one that produces a date.

It is one that tells the truth, respects the other person’s choice, and leaves both people able to move forward without pressure.

You cannot control whether someone is attracted to you.

You can control whether you communicate honestly, whether you treat the other person as an individual, and whether you respect their response.

In the sculpture-and-glasses picture, showing interest clearly means describing what you see instead of requiring the other person to guess.

You might discover they see something similar.

You might discover they do not.

Either answer gives you something more useful than endless guessing: an opportunity to understand each other.

But communicating your own interest is only half of the situation. What happens when someone else appears interested in you? How can you tell what their words and behavior mean without turning an uncertain signal into an assumption?

That is the next skill.
