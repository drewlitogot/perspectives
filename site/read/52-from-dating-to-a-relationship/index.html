Amara and Jules have been dating for two months. They’ve spent weekends together, met a few friends, and started making plans several weeks ahead. Amara has stopped using dating apps. She likes Jules and assumes they’re becoming a couple.

Jules likes Amara too. But Jules still thinks of them as two people getting to know each other. Neither has said anything about exclusivity or becoming partners.

One evening, Amara introduces Jules to a coworker as her partner. Jules looks surprised.

Later, Jules asks, “Are we partners now?”

Amara is confused. “I thought we already were.”

Neither person was trying to mislead the other. They were following different expectations without realizing it.

This is why the transition from dating to a relationship deserves a conversation.

You can enjoy someone’s company, care about them, spend considerable time together, and still have different ideas about what is happening. One person may think exclusivity began weeks ago. Another may believe it starts only when both people agree. Someone might happily call you their partner but prefer to keep the relationship private for now.

These differences do not automatically mean anyone is dishonest, careless, or uninterested. They mean the relationship has reached a point where guessing is no longer enough.

Dating someone is not automatically being their partner

Dating is a process of getting to know someone and finding out whether you want a closer connection.

A relationship is something both people recognize and agree to. That agreement may involve commitment, exclusivity, particular responsibilities, or plans for a shared future. The details depend on the people involved.

There is no universal moment when dating becomes a relationship.

You might feel that it happened naturally. Perhaps you began seeing each other regularly, stopped dating other people, and gradually became an important part of each other’s lives.

But feeling that something happened is different from knowing that the other person understands it the same way.

In a study asking U.S. college students how romantic relationships typically develop, participants described becoming “official,” adopting labels, and often agreeing to exclusivity as recognizable stages. They also distinguished the private understanding between two people from the way the relationship was presented to others. These were descriptions of students’ expectations, not rules that every couple follows (Ogolsky et al., 2025).

The distinction matters because several things that seem connected are actually separate decisions.

  • Being partners: You both agree that you are in a romantic relationship.
  • Romantic exclusivity: You agree not to pursue romantic relationships with other people.
  • Sexual exclusivity: You agree not to have sexual relationships with other people.
  • Public recognition: You agree about whether, when, and how to tell other people that you’re together.
  • Future plans: You discuss what you might want the relationship to become, without assuming that becoming partners settles every future decision.

You can agree on some of these without agreeing on all of them.

For example, two people might agree to date exclusively while they continue deciding whether they want a committed relationship. Another couple might become committed partners while agreeing that their relationship is not sexually exclusive.

Some people prefer not to use conventional relationship labels at all. Others find those labels reassuring and meaningful.

In interviews with 16 autistic adults who were also members of gender or sexual minorities, participants described relationships shaped by individual preferences rather than fixed assumptions about what romance must look like. They emphasized communication, autonomy, and negotiating arrangements that suited the people involved. This was a small U.K. study, not a description of all autistic people or all relationships (Ciric et al., 2026).

The useful question is not whether your relationship resembles someone else’s.

It is whether both people understand what they have agreed to.

Why “what are we?” can feel so difficult

You may find it easy to tell someone that you enjoyed a date but much harder to ask whether you’re becoming partners.

The question carries more emotional weight.

You might worry about discovering that the other person doesn’t feel as strongly as you do. You might fear sounding demanding, being rejected, or ruining something enjoyable.

Perhaps you have never had this conversation before and don’t know what is expected.

Or perhaps you are the person who is uncertain. You like the person you’re dating but aren’t ready to make a commitment, and you worry that admitting this will hurt them.

Uncertainty can make silence tempting.

But silence doesn’t necessarily make the uncertainty disappear.

In a six-week study of 135 romantic couples, researchers found that greater uncertainty about the relationship was associated with viewing conversations about the relationship as threatening and avoiding them. When a person avoided more discussion during one week, they tended to report greater uncertainty the following week. More discussion was generally associated with less uncertainty the following week.

The participants were mostly young, heterosexual couples, many already in serious relationships. The findings were observational, so they cannot prove that talking causes uncertainty to decrease (Knobloch & Theiss, 2011).

A separate study of 244 emerging adults also linked avoiding relationship clarification with uncertainty and later reports of lower dedication and satisfaction. Again, this was an association, not proof that one conversation would fix the relationship (Clifford et al., 2017).

The practical lesson is modest but important: avoiding a question because you dislike the possible answer can leave you making decisions without information you need.

You cannot control how someone feels about you.

You can find out what they are willing to agree to.

Clarity is not pressure

There is an important difference between asking someone what they want and trying to make them want what you want.

You may ask whether someone wants to be your partner. You may explain that exclusivity matters to you. You may decide that continuing to date without commitment does not work for you.

What you cannot reasonably demand is that the other person share your feelings, reach a decision before they’re ready, or agree because you have invested time and attention.

A conversation about commitment is an invitation to make a mutual decision.

It is not a test the other person must pass.

When should you have the conversation?

There is no research-backed rule that says you should become partners after three dates, six weeks, or any other particular interval.

You may encounter advice that insists you should never ask too soon, or that someone who genuinely wants you will make the relationship official by a particular deadline. Those rules are not established by the studies available for this chapter.

Research does suggest that feeling ready for commitment matters.

Across five samples of single people, one research team found associations between perceived readiness for commitment and interest in, pursuit of, and eventual formation of relationships (Hadden et al., 2018). Follow-up research involving people already in relationships also linked readiness with commitment and relationship behavior (Agnew et al., 2019).

Neither study establishes when two particular people should have the “what are we?” conversation.

Instead of counting dates, consider whether the uncertainty has begun affecting your choices.

Maybe you have stopped meeting other potential partners but don’t know whether the person you’re seeing has done the same.

Maybe you’re developing strong feelings and want to know whether the other person sees a possible future together.

Perhaps someone has started introducing you as their partner, and you’re unsure whether you’re comfortable with that.

Or perhaps you simply know that you would like a relationship, and you want to find out whether the person you’re dating wants one too.

These are reasonable reasons to ask.

You do not have to wait until you’re desperately anxious or until the relationship has become confusing.

Equally, you do not have to turn an enjoyable second date into a decision about your entire future.

A useful distinction is between asking about someone’s general intentions and asking for a commitment to you specifically.

Early in dating, you might say:

“I’m interested in finding a long-term relationship eventually. What are you hoping to find?”

That tells you whether your general goals might fit.

Later, once you know each other better, you might ask:

“I’ve really enjoyed getting to know you. I’d like to talk about whether we’re interested in becoming partners.”

The second conversation is about the connection you’ve actually developed, rather than what each of you hopes to find with someone someday.

Neither question requires the other person to give you the answer you want.

First, find out what you want

Before asking what the relationship means to the other person, take a little time to understand what it means to you.

This is especially useful if you tend to agree with people quickly, become intensely excited about a new connection, or have difficulty identifying how you feel until you’ve had time alone.

You might genuinely like someone and still be uncertain about commitment.

You might want exclusivity but not yet want to call the person your partner.

You might want a relationship but not with this particular person.

These are different experiences. It helps to separate them.

Consider four questions.

  1. Do you actually want to keep dating this person?

Not whether you’re attracted to them, whether they’re interested in you, or whether you would feel rejected if they left.

Do you enjoy being with them? Do you like how they treat you? Are you curious about getting to know them further?

A relationship is not a reward for successfully completing enough dates. You still get to decide whether this connection is right for you.

  1. What kind of relationship do you want?

You might want an exclusive long-term partnership. You might prefer a less traditional relationship structure. You might be open to commitment but uncertain about long-term plans.

You do not need a complete plan for the next ten years. You do need enough understanding of your preferences to describe what you’re considering.

  1. What would change if you became partners?

Perhaps you would stop dating other people. Perhaps you would expect to make plans together more regularly. Maybe the most important change would be knowing that both people are intentionally pursuing the relationship.

Be specific about the things that matter to you.

  1. What are you not ready to agree to?

You can want a relationship without wanting to share a home, merge finances, introduce someone to family, or make promises about marriage.

Becoming partners does not require surrendering your independence.

If a question is difficult, you can write down a few thoughts before the conversation. You do not need perfect certainty about your emotions to communicate your current preferences.

One honest answer might be:

“I know I want to keep seeing you, and I’m interested in an exclusive relationship. I’m still learning what a serious relationship would look like for me.”

That gives the other person meaningful information without pretending to know more than you do.

Having the conversation

The conversation does not need a dramatic setting.

Choose a time when both people can pay attention. Avoid beginning it when someone is rushing to work, exhausted, distracted, or dealing with overload.

You can tell the person you’d like to talk, rather than unexpectedly demanding an immediate answer.

For example:

“There’s something I’d like to talk about when we have a little time. I’ve been thinking about where our relationship is going.”

That gives them an opportunity to participate without making the subject sound like an emergency.

When you begin, try to include three things: what you feel or want, what you’re asking, and room for the other person’s answer.

“I really like spending time with you, and I’d be interested in becoming partners. How do you feel about that?”

Or:

“I’ve noticed we’re spending more time together, and I’m starting to think of this as a relationship. I don’t want to assume you see it the same way. What are your thoughts?”

You can also be direct about exclusivity:

“I’d like us to date exclusively. Is that something you want too?”

These are examples, not magic words. There is no demonstrated scientifically optimal script for defining a relationship.

Their purpose is to make your meaning understandable.

Ask one question, then listen

A common temptation is to ask a question and immediately begin explaining, qualifying, or defending it.

Someone might say:

“I’d like us to be exclusive, but only if you want to, and maybe it’s too soon, and I know you’ve been busy, and I don’t want you to think I’m being demanding…”

By the time they stop, neither person may be sure what question needs answering.

You are allowed to express a preference without apologizing for having one.

Say what you mean. Then give the other person space to respond.

If they need a moment to think, that is not automatically a bad sign.

If they ask what you mean by “partners,” that is a useful question, not evidence that they don’t care.

If they disagree, listen to the disagreement rather than treating it as something you must overcome.

The goal is understanding.

If you need it said in words

You can date someone for months and genuinely not know whether you’re in a relationship, because everyone around you treats it as something that becomes obvious. For you it may not.

Ask for the words. “Are we a couple? I’d like to know rather than assume” is a reasonable sentence, and the discomfort of asking is smaller than months of uncertainty.

Needing it explicit isn’t neediness. It’s wanting the same information everyone else believes they already have.

Make the important words explicit

The words dating, exclusive, serious, and partner may feel perfectly clear inside your own head.

But the other person cannot see your private definition.

One study of 573 adults in romantic relationships in the United States and Canada illustrates the problem. Most respondents reported an understanding about romantic or sexual exclusivity, yet only 43% reported having discussed the agreement explicitly. Another 52% described it as implied.

Importantly, the study surveyed individual people, not both members of each couple. It could not establish whether the partners actually shared the same understanding. It also found no statistically significant difference in reported infidelity between people who described their agreement as explicit and those who described it as implied (Muise et al., 2021).

So the evidence does not support promising that an exclusivity conversation prevents cheating.

The reason to clarify is simpler: you should both know what you’re agreeing to.

Consider this exchange.

“I’d like us to be exclusive.”

“I’d like that too.”

“Just to make sure we’re talking about the same thing, do you mean neither of us dates anyone else?”

“Yes. I’d also mean not pursuing romantic connections with other people.”

“That makes sense to me.”

Now both people have described the agreement, instead of relying on one word.

You may also discover differences.

One person might think exclusivity means not having sex with other people, while continuing to date casually is acceptable. Another might mean that neither person pursues other romantic connections at all.

There is no benefit in pretending these meanings are identical.

The same care applies to public recognition.

Imagine Noor and Tessa, who have agreed to become partners.

Noor is excited and wants to share a photograph of them together online. Tessa is happy about the relationship but wants to tell a few people privately first.

Noor’s enthusiasm is real. So is Tessa’s preference for privacy.

They can agree that becoming partners does not automatically mean announcing the relationship publicly.

You might ask:

“Are you comfortable with us telling friends we’re together?”

“How would you like me to introduce you?”

“Would you prefer that I ask before posting photos of us?”

These questions matter regardless of gender, orientation, or neurotype.

For some people, privacy may also relate to safety, family circumstances, or whether they’re comfortable disclosing their relationship or identity. Do not assume the reason. Ask respectfully, and take the answer seriously.

Don’t turn the conversation into a contract negotiation

Being clear doesn’t require planning every future situation.

You do not need an exhaustive list of hypothetical problems or a written agreement covering every social interaction.

Start with what matters now.

If a question becomes relevant later, you can discuss it then.

You can also change an agreement together. For example, two people who initially chose not to be exclusive might later decide they want exclusivity.

What matters is that a change is discussed and freely agreed to, rather than announced as though the other person has already consented.

When your minds work differently

A person who prefers direct communication may ask about relationship status much earlier than someone who expects the relationship to develop through unspoken signals.

One person may regard explicit questions as considerate. Another may initially interpret the same questions as unexpectedly formal.

Neither interpretation is necessarily the only reasonable one.

The double empathy problem, introduced earlier in this book, is useful here. People can experience the same interaction differently because they bring different ways of communicating and understanding relationships.

In a small qualitative study involving 31 autistic and 26 non-autistic adults, both groups identified communication, respect, and safety as important to intimacy. Some autistic participants specifically described relationship uncertainty and communication as barriers (Sala et al., 2020).

That does not mean every autistic person wants identical communication, or that non-autistic people always prefer hints.

It does mean you should not assume that your partner’s preferred way of communicating matches yours.

If you’re someone who finds vague relationship language difficult, you can say:

“I understand things better when they’re stated clearly. Would you mind telling me what being partners means to you?”

If you’re dating someone who asks detailed questions, try answering what they’re actually asking rather than assuming the questions are an interrogation or a sign that romance is missing.

You can also say when you don’t have an answer.

When you need time to process

Some people can recognize and describe their feelings immediately. Others need time to think, particularly when a conversation is unexpected or emotionally important.

You may find speaking difficult during overload or shutdown. You may have trouble identifying what you feel until hours later.

If that’s your experience, you can ask to continue the conversation when you can participate more comfortably.

“I care about this, and I want to give you a thoughtful answer. Can I take some time to think and talk about it tomorrow?”

If speaking is difficult, a message may be easier.

“I’ve been thinking about our conversation. I do want to keep dating you. I’m still deciding whether I’m ready to be exclusive.”

These are practical accommodations, not scientifically tested methods for making a relationship succeed.

If you’re on the receiving end, try not to interpret a request for time as an automatic rejection. Equally, you don’t have to ignore your own need for clarity indefinitely.

You can agree to return to the conversation.

And when you return, both people deserve an honest update, even if the answer remains uncertain.

If you're dating someone who wants it stated explicitly

Being asked to define things directly can feel abrupt if you expected it to settle gradually. It usually means they care about the answer, not that they’re rushing you.

Give the clearest answer you honestly have, including an uncertain one: “Yes, exclusive, and I’m not ready to talk about living together” is more useful than a warm non-answer.

“Let’s see how it goes” is heard as a no by someone who can’t read the implied yes. If you mean it as a yes, say the yes.

When you want different things

Not every relationship conversation ends with two people happily agreeing to become partners.

Sometimes one person wants more commitment than the other.

This can be painful, especially when you’ve started imagining a future together.

But different levels of readiness do not automatically mean that someone is using you, that the relationship was meaningless, or that one person has failed.

Research on unequal commitment is mixed.

One longitudinal study of 315 unmarried, different-sex couples associated larger commitment differences with some poorer relationship outcomes (Stanley et al., 2017).

However, a larger preregistered analysis of 6,960 couples found that differences in commitment did not consistently predict additional problems once each person’s own commitment was taken into account. Much of that larger sample involved established couples, and its main analyses were limited to different-sex relationships (Dobson et al., 2023).

These studies do not establish that partners must feel exactly the same amount of love at exactly the same time.

What you need is agreement about the decisions you’re making together.

If the answer is yes

You can enjoy the moment without immediately making promises about everything that comes next.

You might say:

“I’m really happy we’re on the same page. Can we talk a little about what being partners means to each of us?”

Then clarify the important parts.

You don’t need to turn the evening into an examination. A few clear agreements are more useful than a long discussion that leaves both people exhausted.

If the answer is “not yet”

Suppose Rafael says:

“I really like you, but I’m not ready to call this a committed relationship yet.”

You might feel disappointed. You may also appreciate that Rafael has been straightforward.

A respectful response could be:

“Thank you for telling me. I like you too. I need to think about whether I’m comfortable continuing to date without that commitment.”

You may ask what the other person currently wants, but don’t assume “not yet” means “yes, eventually.”

Someone can genuinely like you and still never want the kind of relationship you want.

If both people are comfortable continuing, they can agree to revisit the question at a later time.

But there is no scientifically established waiting period that makes someone ready, and you do not owe anyone unlimited waiting.

You are allowed to decide that uncertainty no longer works for you.

If the answer is no

A clear no is information you can act on.

Perhaps the other person enjoys dating you but doesn’t want a relationship. Perhaps they want a different relationship structure, or simply do not want to become your partner.

You do not need to investigate whether their reasons are sufficiently convincing.

You can acknowledge the disappointment without making them responsible for fixing it.

“I appreciate you being clear. I’m looking for a committed relationship, so I don’t think continuing to date is right for me.”

That is a boundary about your own participation.

You are not punishing the other person by choosing not to continue.

Similarly, if you’re the one saying no, you can be kind without offering a false promise.

“I’ve enjoyed getting to know you, but I don’t want to become partners. I don’t want to give you a different impression.”

You cannot guarantee that the other person won’t feel hurt. You can avoid adding confusion by saying what you mean.

If your answers are incompatible

Sometimes neither person is uncertain.

They simply want different things.

One wants a monogamous relationship. The other wants a relationship that permits other romantic partners.

One wants to become partners. The other only wants casual dating.

Neither preference is automatically wrong.

But being compatible requires more than respecting the existence of another person’s preference. You need an arrangement that both of you actually want.

A compromise is not meaningful if one person agrees only because they’re frightened of losing the other.

When there is no mutually acceptable agreement, ending the dating relationship may be kinder than continuing under incompatible expectations.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly pushing for an answer after someone has said no, stop the pressure. Respect their answer, and seek support if you find it difficult to do so.

You do not have to win the conversation for it to have been worthwhile.

Becoming partners does not erase boundaries

A new relationship can feel exciting. You may want to spend more time together, share more of your lives, or express affection more openly.

But becoming someone’s partner does not give you unrestricted access to them.

It does not automatically mean you can touch them in ways they haven’t agreed to, have sex with them, read their messages, track their location, demand passwords, or tell other people private information.

Consent remains specific, freely given, and ongoing.

Your partner remains an independent person.

The agreements you make about the relationship should reflect that.

If you’re unsure whether something is welcome, ask rather than treating the relationship label as permission.

And if the other person has a boundary you do not share, you can respect it without needing to experience the world exactly as they do.

A committed relationship is not ownership.

What happens after the conversation?

You have talked. Perhaps you’ve become partners. Perhaps you’ve agreed to continue dating without exclusivity, or to think about the question and return to it.

What now?

First, make sure neither person walked away with a different understanding.

You might briefly summarize what you agreed to:

“So we’re both interested in being partners, and we’re going to stop dating other people. We’d like to tell our close friends, but neither of us wants to post anything publicly yet. Is that right?”

If the other person agrees, you have a shared understanding of the decision you’ve made.

You don’t need to repeat every word back to each other. The point is to notice any important difference while it’s still easy to clarify.

If you communicate more comfortably in writing, a short follow-up message can help:

“I’m happy we talked yesterday. I’m glad we’re together, and I appreciate that we agreed to take things at a pace that works for both of us.”

You can also write down important agreements for your own reference, particularly if you have difficulty remembering conversations.

This is an aid to understanding, not a document you can use to override someone’s later boundaries or consent.

Relationships change over time. An agreement you made willingly today may need to be revisited as your circumstances change.

The purpose of communicating clearly is not to prevent either person from ever changing.

It’s to make those changes something you can talk about rather than discover through assumptions.

A small practice before you need the conversation

If asking “what are we?” feels intimidating, you can prepare without rehearsing every possible outcome.

Take a few minutes and complete these sentences:

I enjoy dating this person because ______.

What I would like next is ______.

When I say “relationship,” I mean ______.

Something I am not ready for is ______.

If the other person does not want the same thing, I can ______.

You can keep the answers private.

Then practice saying the central question aloud, in your own words.

“I like you, and I’d be interested in becoming partners. Is that something you want?”

You don’t need a perfectly polished speech. You need to be understandable, sincere, and prepared to hear a real answer.

If the answer isn’t what you hoped for, that does not mean asking was a mistake.

And if the answer is yes, that doesn’t mean your relationship is suddenly guaranteed to succeed.

In a study of 252 U.S. adults, people who reported approaching relationship transitions more thoughtfully also reported higher dedication and satisfaction and less involvement outside the relationship. Because the study was correlational, it cannot establish that deliberate conversations caused those outcomes (Owen et al., 2013).

Still, deliberately deciding what you’re agreeing to is a reasonable way to approach a decision that matters to both people.

There is no special number of dates that turns two people into partners. No amount of time spent together substitutes for understanding what each person wants.

You can enjoy the uncertainty of getting to know someone without having to remain uncertain about every important decision.

When the time comes, describe what you want, ask what they want, and decide together what happens next.

If you become partners, you’ve reached a new starting point, not the finish line. The next questions are less about whether you’re dating and more about how you want to share ordinary life: your routines, your time, your space, and your independence.

Those are the decisions that begin to shape the relationship you actually live in.
