Chapter 50: On the date

You’ve arrived. The other person is sitting at a table, looking at the menu. They look up. You smile, or try to. Your brain suddenly forgets every conversation you’ve ever had.

Do you shake hands? Hug? Sit down? Say something clever? Ask about their day? What if they ask a question you can’t answer?

Perhaps you’ve been looking forward to this date for a week. Perhaps you nearly canceled three times. Maybe you know how to talk to people at work but have no idea how to talk to someone when the purpose is getting to know each other.

You don’t need a special personality for dating. You need a few ordinary conversation skills, permission to be yourself, and the ability to notice and respect what both people need.

A date is not a performance where one person judges whether the other deserves a relationship. Both people are finding out what spending time together feels like.

That includes whether you enjoy the other person’s company, whether they seem comfortable with you, whether you feel comfortable with them, and whether you can communicate when something isn’t working.

You might have a wonderful conversation. You might have an awkward one. You might discover that someone who seemed exciting online is surprisingly difficult to talk to, or that someone who seemed ordinary is delightful in person.

You don’t have to decide what any of that means while you’re still sitting together.

The first few minutes

Start with something simple.

“Hey, it’s good to meet you.”

“How’s your day going?”

“Did you find the place okay?”

That’s enough. You don’t need an unusual opening line, a rehearsed joke, or an explanation of why you’re nervous.

If you’ve met before, perhaps through friends or an activity group, you can refer to something you already have in common.

“Did you ever finish that painting you were working on?”

“How did your presentation go?”

If this is your first in-person meeting after talking online, you can pick up a conversation you’ve already started.

“You mentioned you were learning to cook. What have you tried lately?”

The opening doesn’t need to be impressive. Its job is simply to help two people begin talking.

If you’re uncertain about something practical, ask.

“Would you rather sit here or by the window?”

“Do you want to order first?”

“Is this table okay?”

Small questions aren’t admissions that you don’t know how dating works. They’re ways of making a shared experience work for both people.

When you feel painfully self-conscious

Maybe you’re thinking about your hands, your posture, your voice, or whether you’ve looked at your date for too long. You’re so busy monitoring yourself that you barely hear what they say.

You may be experiencing masking, which means hiding or suppressing your natural traits to fit in. Trying to maintain an unfamiliar version of yourself can become one more demand on your attention.

You don’t need to force eye contact, laugh at every joke, or pretend you’re comfortable with something you’re not. You can try to be considerate without copying someone else’s mannerisms.

There is also a reason not to treat every awkward moment as a disaster.

Across five studies of getting-acquainted conversations, researchers found that people often underestimated how much their conversation partners liked them. The researchers called this the liking gap. These were primarily non-romantic interactions, so the finding does not mean your date is attracted to you or wants to meet again. It does mean that your anxious estimate of how you’re coming across may not be accurate. (Boothby et al., 2018)

If it helps, you can say something ordinary and true:

“I’m a little nervous. I usually settle in once I’ve been talking for a bit.”

Then continue the conversation. You don’t need to apologize repeatedly for being nervous or ask the other person to reassure you throughout the date.

And if they seem nervous, give them a little room to settle in too.

A conversation isn’t an interview

Imagine two people, Daniel and Marcus, meeting for coffee.

Daniel wants to make a good impression, so he’s prepared questions.

“What do you do for work?”

“Where did you grow up?”

“Do you have siblings?”

“What music do you listen to?”

Marcus answers each one. Daniel moves immediately to the next.

Nothing Daniel asked was inappropriate. But the conversation isn’t developing. Marcus is supplying information, and Daniel is collecting it.

A useful conversation has room for both people to ask, answer, react, and share.

Suppose Marcus mentions that he spent last weekend working on an old bicycle.

Daniel could ask, “Do you ride a lot?”

Marcus says, “I’m hoping to. This one’s been sitting in my garage for years.”

Daniel asks, “What made you decide to fix it now?”

Marcus explains that he wants to start riding with his brother.

Now Daniel has several directions available. He could ask about the rides they’re planning, share a similar experience, or follow whatever part genuinely interests him.

The next question comes from listening, not from a prepared list.

Research supports the value of this kind of curiosity. In experiments, people who asked more questions, particularly follow-up questions about what someone had just said, tended to be liked more. An observational speed-dating analysis also found an association between follow-up questions and agreement to another date. That part of the research cannot establish cause and effect, and it doesn’t provide a formula for getting a second date. A 2025 correction to the paper left its central conclusions unchanged. (Huang et al., 2017)

The useful lesson is not to ask as many questions as possible.

It’s to show that you’ve heard the answer.

Remember to contribute, too

You don’t need to make the other person do all the talking.

If they mention a hobby you share, you can tell them about your experience.

If they ask a question, answer it with enough detail that they can learn something about you.

If you’re telling a story, leave room for them to respond.

For example:

“I started gardening last year. My tomatoes did great, but almost everything else died. Have you ever tried growing anything?”

The question isn’t a requirement. It’s an invitation to participate.

If you tend to talk for a long time about something you enjoy, you can occasionally check whether the other person wants to hear more.

“There’s a lot more to that story, but you might be ready for a different topic.”

They may say they want to hear the rest. They may welcome the change.

Neither answer requires you to be embarrassed about what interests you.

And if your date talks enthusiastically about something unfamiliar, you don’t have to pretend to know it.

“I don’t know much about that. What got you interested?”

Sometimes genuine curiosity is more useful than pretending to have the same interests.

When the conversation gets more personal

You might move from talking about work, movies, or weekend plans to discussing family, ambitions, values, or what life has been like.

There’s no requirement to make a first date deeply personal. But there’s also no rule that you must spend the entire time discussing the weather.

In a series of experiments, people tended to expect deeper conversations with strangers to be more awkward and less connecting than participants later reported experiencing. These were structured conversations, not dates, and the results don’t establish that deeper questions are always welcome. (Kardas et al., 2022)

You can allow personal topics to develop while respecting the other person’s choice not to discuss them.

“Can I ask something a little more personal?”

“Sure.”

“What made you decide to move here?”

Or:

“Would you rather keep things light for now?”

You can also decline a topic yourself.

“That’s a long story, and I’d rather get to know you a little better before talking about it.”

A personal answer is something someone chooses to share, not something you earn by asking the right sequence of questions.

If your date avoids a subject, gives a short answer, or says they don’t want to discuss it, move on. Don’t keep approaching the same question from different directions.

Silence doesn’t need an explanation

At some point, both people may stop talking.

Perhaps you’re eating. Perhaps you’re thinking about what the other person said. Perhaps the conversation has simply reached a natural stopping point.

You don’t have to rescue every silence.

Look at something around you. Take a sip of your drink. Consider what you’d actually like to know.

You can say, “I’m trying to think of how to put that into words.”

You can ask, “What were you saying earlier about your trip?”

Or you can sit quietly for a moment.

The important difference is between silence that feels comfortable and silence that is making one or both people unhappy. You may not know which it is without asking.

If your date looks away, becomes quieter, or needs more time to answer, that doesn’t tell you exactly what they’re thinking.

They could be tired, distracted, uncertain, concentrating, or uncomfortable. They may simply have a different conversational rhythm.

Don’t invent an explanation and treat it as fact.

When communication styles don’t match

Research on autistic and non-autistic interactions gives a useful warning about first impressions.

In three studies, non-autistic observers tended to make less favorable initial judgments about autistic people based on brief visual or audio-visual information. When observers evaluated transcripts containing only what people said, the group difference disappeared. This suggests that social judgments can be influenced by presentation rather than conversational substance alone. (Sasson et al., 2017)

Another US study observed brief conversations between autistic and non-autistic adults. Participants’ evaluations and interest in further interaction varied partly according to the pairing. The results also suggested that autistic adults disclosed more to autistic partners than to non-autistic partners. (Morrison et al., 2020)

These studies don’t tell you whether someone will be a good romantic partner. They do challenge the idea that conventional social presentation is the only acceptable way to communicate.

Consider Priya and Elena, two autistic women in their thirties, meeting at a museum. Priya likes to explain what she’s thinking aloud. Elena prefers to spend several moments studying an exhibit before saying anything.

Priya initially wonders whether Elena is bored.

Instead of guessing, she asks, “Are you enjoying this, or would you rather move to the next room?”

Elena says, “I’m enjoying it. I just like to look carefully before I talk.”

Now Priya understands something that she couldn’t have learned from Elena’s silence alone.

Neither person needed to change their personality. They needed to explain their different experiences.

The double empathy problem, discussed earlier in the book, applies here: misunderstandings between people whose brains work differently can run in both directions.

One person’s familiar way of communicating may be unfamiliar to someone else.

A date gives you a chance to find out whether you can make room for those differences.

It doesn’t require either person to become someone else.

Pay attention to how the date feels

It’s possible to become so focused on whether the other person likes you that you forget to ask whether you enjoy being there.

Every so often, notice your own experience.

Are you enjoying the conversation? Are you comfortable enough to participate? Do you feel listened to? Can you express an ordinary preference without being dismissed?

You don’t have to answer all these questions immediately. They’re simply reminders that your experience matters too.

The person across from you may be kind, attractive, and interesting. You still might not enjoy spending time together.

Or you may feel nervous while genuinely enjoying someone’s company.

Those experiences aren’t identical.

Try not to treat nervousness as automatic evidence of attraction, or quietness as automatic evidence of incompatibility.

Let the encounter give you information instead of trying to force a conclusion.

When too much is happening at once

The restaurant seemed fine when you arrived.

Now the room is crowded. Music is playing. Several conversations overlap. Someone nearby is wearing a strong fragrance.

Your date asks about your weekend, but you can’t follow the question. You can hear their voice without being able to concentrate on the words.

Maybe you become irritable. Maybe you stop talking. Maybe you want to leave so badly that thinking about anything else becomes difficult.

This may be overload: more noise, light, conversation, emotions, or other demands coming in than you can handle at once.

The source of overload is not necessarily the person you’re dating.

In a qualitative study of autistic adults’ accounts of sensory experiences in relationships, researchers found that sound, smell, sight, and touch could influence comfort and connection in different ways. Some sensory experiences were enjoyable, while others made interactions difficult. The findings describe individual experiences, not a universal pattern or a tested method for handling dates. (Gray et al., 2021)

You don’t need an autism label to know that a particular room is too loud for you, or that you need a quieter place to concentrate.

Make one adjustment at a time

If you can recognize overload beginning, you may have options.

“Could we move away from the speakers? I’m having trouble following the conversation.”

“I’m enjoying talking, but I need a few minutes somewhere quieter.”

“Can you give me a moment to think about that?”

“I’m having trouble concentrating. Would you mind asking me one thing at a time?”

You don’t have to give a medical explanation for any of these requests.

You can also decide to use earplugs, move to a different seat, take a short break, or end the date if you need to.

None of these adjustments has been established by the cited research as a universally effective dating intervention. They’re practical options for responding to your own needs.

And remember that your date may have needs of their own.

One person may find a busy room exhausting. Another may feel more relaxed when there’s background activity. The answer isn’t automatically to use whichever environment one person prefers.

You can look for an arrangement that works for both of you, or recognize that the current arrangement isn’t working.

When talking becomes difficult

Sometimes overload progresses to a shutdown, when your brain and body pull inward and slow down or stop. Speaking or responding may become very difficult.

Autistic adults have described shutdowns as experiences of withdrawal, reduced ability to respond, and difficulty keeping pace with demands. These accounts vary, and a shutdown isn’t something you can diagnose from a person’s silence. (Paris et al., 2026)

A meltdown is a different experience. It can involve an intense response to demands that have become too much, and it may be more internal than an observer realizes. Interviews with autistic adults describe varied experiences, including difficulty thinking clearly and a wish to withdraw from the situation. (Lewis & Stevens, 2023)

Neither experience should be treated as a character flaw, deliberate rudeness, or proof that someone isn’t interested.

If you know that speaking sometimes becomes difficult, you might find it useful to have a short message saved on your phone:

“I’m overloaded and having trouble talking. I need some quiet.”

You can show it instead of saying it.

If you cannot communicate easily, you do not have to force yourself through a conversation merely because you’re on a date.

If you need to leave, leaving is enough.

If you're dating someone who suddenly goes quiet

If someone becomes unusually quiet, struggles to answer, or says they need a break, don’t assume they’ve lost interest or are deliberately ignoring you. Some autistic adults describe shutdowns that make responding difficult, even when they know what they want to communicate. (Paris et al., 2026)

You can offer a simple choice: “Would you prefer some quiet, or would you rather head home?” Give the person time to respond. If speaking is difficult, they may prefer to type, gesture, or wait.

Don’t crowd them with questions, insist on touch to comfort them, or make them explain their experience immediately. Ask what helps rather than assuming. They may want company, distance, assistance, or to leave independently. Respect the answer.

What if your date is the one who needs a change?

Suppose you’re enjoying the conversation, but your date says the room is becoming too loud.

You can ask, “Would you like to move somewhere quieter?”

If they say yes, and you’re comfortable doing so, you can look for a different place together.

If they want to end the date, respect that.

If they need ten minutes alone, don’t interpret the request as a challenge to prove you’re caring enough to stay beside them.

You can be considerate without taking control.

And you can express your own needs, too.

Maybe they would prefer to walk outside, but your knee hurts. Maybe they want to stay for another two hours, but you have an early morning.

Neither person’s preferences automatically decide the entire date.

You can say, “A quieter place sounds good, but I need somewhere I can sit.”

A difference is something to discuss, not a reason to decide that one person is being difficult.

Don’t mistake your date for a promise of physical contact

You’ve been laughing together. The conversation is going well. Your date has been smiling.

Would a hug be appropriate? Holding hands? A kiss?

You don’t have to guess.

“Would you like a hug?”

“Can I hold your hand?”

“Would you be comfortable with a kiss?”

These are ordinary questions. They give the other person room to answer.

They can say yes, no, or that they’re not sure.

If the answer is uncertain, wait. Don’t treat silence, hesitation, or the absence of resistance as permission.

RAINN’s guidance on consent emphasizes that consent to sexual activity must be freely given, specific, and ongoing, and that it can be withdrawn. Prior agreement doesn’t establish consent to something new or later. (RAINN, 2026a)

Those principles also offer a respectful way to approach everyday physical affection, although laws governing sexual contact vary across US states and the earlier consent chapters explain those issues in greater detail.

If your date says they don’t want a hug, the response can be as simple as “Okay.”

Don’t ask them to justify the decision.

Don’t turn an ordinary physical boundary into an argument about whether they like you.

And don’t interpret a yes to one kind of touch as permission for another.

Your consent matters, too

Suppose your date asks whether they can kiss you.

You like them, but you’re not ready.

You can say, “Not tonight.”

You can also say, “I’m not sure yet.”

You don’t have to accept physical contact because the other person paid, traveled a long distance, spent hours talking, or seems disappointed.

A date isn’t an exchange in which attention, money, or effort purchases access to another person’s body.

If you want to change your mind about contact you’ve already agreed to, you can.

If someone continues pressuring you after you’ve expressed uncertainty or refusal, take that seriously. You don’t need to remain in the situation to avoid upsetting them.

If you recognize yourself pressuring someone, stop. Respect their decision and don’t demand reassurance, an explanation, or another chance to ask.

Ending the date

Every date eventually ends, including the good ones.

Sometimes this is straightforward. You finish your drinks, stand up, and say goodbye.

Other times you reach the planned ending but can’t tell whether you’re supposed to suggest another activity. Perhaps you’ve had a pleasant time and don’t know whether that means anything romantic. Or perhaps you’re exhausted and the other person wants to keep talking.

You don’t need to solve the future of the relationship before leaving.

A simple, ordinary goodbye

If you’ve had a pleasant time, you can say:

“Thanks for meeting up. I had a good time.”

“I’m glad we got to talk.”

“Thanks for showing me this place.”

If the conversation was neutral, or you aren’t sure how you feel, you can simply say:

“Thanks for meeting. I’m going to head out now.”

You don’t need to exaggerate how wonderful the date was.

You also don’t need to announce a detailed evaluation of the other person’s personality, appearance, or compatibility.

A goodbye is not a performance review.

If you genuinely want to express interest in seeing them again, you can, but a decision about another date can wait. Chapter 51 covers what happens afterward.

When one person wants to continue

Suppose you planned to spend an hour together. The hour has passed, and your date suggests going somewhere else.

You can accept if you genuinely want to and feel comfortable.

“Sure, I’d enjoy that.”

You can also decline.

“I had a good time, but I’m ready to head home.”

Or:

“I’d rather stick with the plan and finish here.”

You don’t owe someone extra time because you were enjoying yourself earlier.

That is what a boundary means in practice: you can decide what you’re willing to do and communicate that decision.

Your date can have a different preference without either person being wrong.

What matters is whether that difference is respected.

When you need to leave early

Maybe the conversation isn’t going well. Maybe you’re tired, uncomfortable, or dealing with overload. Maybe you expected to stay an hour and discover that twenty minutes is all you can manage.

You can end the date early.

“I’m getting tired, and I need to head home.”

“I’m not feeling up to continuing tonight.”

“I need to leave earlier than planned.”

You can be kind without giving a long explanation.

If the other person seems disappointed, you can acknowledge that without changing your decision.

“I know we’d planned to stay longer, but I need to go.”

You don’t need to invent an emergency to justify leaving.

You also don’t have to stay until the other person agrees that your reason is good enough.

Of course, your date may have planned something around the agreed ending time. Basic consideration matters. But consideration does not mean surrendering control of your time or ignoring your own limits.

If leaving early becomes necessary, communicate as clearly as you reasonably can, then leave.

When the date feels unsafe

Politeness is not more important than safety.

If someone is behaving in a way that makes you feel unsafe, you do not need to finish the meal, explain your concerns, or complete a goodbye conversation.

RAINN advises maintaining the ability to leave a date and emphasizes that you may end an encounter when you feel uncomfortable. These are safety precautions, not guarantees, and responsibility for harmful behavior rests with the person causing it. (RAINN, 2026b)

Move to a safer, populated area if possible. Ask staff or a trusted person for help. Contact emergency services if you are in immediate danger.

You do not need to get the other person’s permission to leave.

Research on hypothetical romantic-rejection situations found that perceived safety concerns could increase people’s willingness to reject indirectly rather than communicate a direct refusal. The findings don’t describe actual date departures, and differences by gender were uncertain. They do reinforce that safety concerns can be relevant to how someone chooses to end an interaction. (Freedman et al., 2022)

If you feel safe, straightforward communication is usually a reasonable choice.

If you don’t feel safe, prioritize leaving safely. You are not required to deliver the perfect rejection speech.

When your date decides to leave

The same freedom belongs to the other person.

If your date says they’re ready to go, take the answer seriously.

You can say, “Okay. Thanks for meeting me.”

If they explain that they’re tired or overloaded, you can acknowledge it without demanding more information.

If they don’t explain, you can still respect their decision.

Don’t block their departure, follow them to continue the discussion, repeatedly ask them to reconsider, or insist that they owe you more time because you arranged or paid for the date.

You may feel disappointed, confused, or hurt. Those feelings are real, but they aren’t permission to pressure someone.

If you recognize yourself beginning to argue with a person’s decision to leave, stop. Let them go. Address your feelings away from the encounter, and seek support if respecting other people’s boundaries is a recurring difficulty.

A date should be something both people are free to participate in and free to end.

What if you think you ruined it?

Perhaps you told a joke that didn’t land.

Perhaps you talked for too long about your work.

Maybe you misunderstood a question, knocked over your water, forgot someone’s name, or needed an unexpected break.

Not every awkward moment requires an apology or explanation.

If you’ve actually hurt someone’s feelings or crossed a boundary, acknowledge what happened, apologize appropriately, and respect their response. The earlier chapter on repairing misunderstandings explains that process.

But being nervous, needing time to think, asking for clarification, or having different communication needs isn’t wrongdoing.

You may notice that your date is also making small mistakes. They might search for words, lose their train of thought, or accidentally interrupt you.

A useful question isn’t whether either person behaved perfectly.

It’s whether you could be imperfect around each other without being treated unkindly.

That doesn’t mean ignoring serious disrespect or pressure. It means allowing ordinary human awkwardness to be ordinary.

One awkward conversation also cannot tell you everything about how two people might connect.

Research on brief interactions indicates that first impressions depend partly on the observers and the communication styles involved. Those studies do not predict whether a specific date will become a relationship. (Morrison et al., 2020; Sasson et al., 2017)

You can learn from an uncomfortable experience without declaring yourself incapable of dating.

A few sentences worth remembering

You don’t need to memorize an entire conversation. If having prepared words makes dating easier, keep a few that serve different purposes.

For starting:

“Hey, it’s good to see you.”

For showing interest:

“Tell me more about that.”

For clarification:

“I’m not sure I understood. Can you explain what you meant?”

For a pause:

“Give me a moment to think.”

For overload:

“I’m having trouble concentrating. Could we find somewhere quieter?”

For a physical boundary:

“I’m not comfortable with that.”

For checking consent:

“Would a hug be okay?”

For ending:

“Thanks for meeting. I’m going to head out now.”

These are examples, not special words that produce particular results. Change them to sound natural coming from you.

And remember that listening to the answer matters as much as asking the question.

The date doesn’t have to answer everything

Maybe you leave feeling excited.

Maybe you’re unsure.

Maybe you know you didn’t enjoy yourself.

Maybe you were comfortable and interested, but there wasn’t an obvious romantic feeling. Or perhaps you enjoyed yourself so much that you’re already imagining what a relationship could look like.

You don’t have to resolve all of that during the date.

For now, the useful things are simple: you showed up, spent time with another person, learned something about them, and had an opportunity to notice your own experience.

You may have discovered that you like direct conversation. That you need a quieter setting. That you enjoy talking to someone who shares your interests, or that you appreciate someone who asks thoughtful questions about things they don’t know.

You may have learned what you’re willing to try next time, or what you don’t want to repeat.

The next chapter looks at what happens after you go home: how to decide what you feel, whether you want another date, and how to communicate that decision.

You don’t have to make a relationship happen during one encounter.

You only have to give both people a fair opportunity to find out whether they enjoy being together, while respecting each other’s choices.

That’s enough for one date.
