Chapter 56: Mixed-neurotype and neurodivergent couples

Jordan thinks the evening went well. They made dinner, watched a movie with their partner, Alex, and spent a comfortable hour sitting together without talking much.

Alex thinks something is wrong.

Alex had a difficult day at work. They wanted to talk about it, hear some reassurance, and feel that Jordan was interested. Jordan listened for a few minutes, offered a practical suggestion, and then returned to watching the movie.

Jordan, who is autistic, thought the suggestion was helpful. Sitting quietly together afterward was Jordan’s way of offering comfort.

Alex, who is not autistic, interpreted the silence as a lack of interest.

Neither person intended to hurt the other. But by bedtime, Alex felt lonely and Jordan felt unfairly criticized.

The difficulty wasn’t that one person cared and the other didn’t. They had different ideas about what caring looked like.

If you're dating someone who shows care in practical ways

A suggestion instead of sympathy isn’t always a refusal to engage with your feelings. Your partner may be offering the kind of help they would most want to receive.

You can say what you need without treating their response as a failure: “I’m not looking for a solution yet. Could you just listen for a few minutes first?” Most people can do that once they know it’s what’s wanted.

Sitting quietly beside you may also be care rather than absence. Ask what it means to them instead of deciding, and tell them what reads as closeness to you.

This is one of the challenges in a relationship between people whose brains work differently. Something one partner considers obvious may not be obvious to the other. A gesture meant as kindness may not be received that way. A request that sounds simple to one person may involve considerable effort for the other.

These differences can appear between autistic and non-autistic partners, between someone with ADHD and someone without it, or between two neurodivergent people with different needs.

They can also appear between people with the same neurotype.

The goal isn’t to make both partners think alike. It’s to understand what each person needs, communicate those needs, and build a relationship where both people can participate without having to become someone else.

Is dating someone whose brain works like yours easier?

Sometimes. But sharing a neurotype isn’t a guarantee of compatibility.

One study of 229 autistic adults found that participants with autistic partners reported greater relationship satisfaction than those with non-autistic partners. That suggests shared ways of experiencing the world may help some couples connect (Strunz et al., 2017).

A newer US study surveyed 106 autistic adults whose partners were autistic, otherwise neurodivergent, or neurotypical. It found no statistically significant difference in overall relationship satisfaction between those groups. Participants with autistic partners often described a sense of immediate understanding. Those with partners of other neurotypes frequently described understanding that developed through effort, communication, and accommodations (Khaw & Vernon, 2026).

The studies don’t establish the same conclusion. They used different samples and methods, and neither can show that a particular partner neurotype causes a better relationship.

A smaller UK study interviewed 16 autistic adults with diverse gender identities and sexual orientations. Some described strong connections with autistic partners. Others described successful relationships with non-autistic partners, or experiences where sharing a neurotype hadn’t made a relationship work (Ciric et al., 2026).

Taken together, these findings point toward a practical distinction:

Having similar needs can make understanding easier. Having different needs doesn’t make understanding impossible.

Imagine two people who both dislike crowded restaurants. Choosing somewhere quiet might be effortless because neither person needs to explain why.

Now imagine one person enjoys busy restaurants and the other finds them exhausting. They might need to plan quieter dates, alternate activities, or sometimes go out separately.

The second couple has more to negotiate on that particular issue. That doesn’t tell you whether their relationship is better or worse overall.

Two autistic people might agree about sensory needs but disagree about how much time to spend together. Two people with ADHD might understand each other’s distractibility but struggle to maintain a shared routine. An autistic person and a partner with ADHD might have a wonderful sense of humor together while finding planning difficult.

The label doesn’t tell you enough.

Compatibility is about the two people and how their needs fit together, not simply whether their diagnoses match.

You’re learning a person, not a diagnosis

When people describe a mixed-neurotype relationship as speaking different languages, they usually mean something broader than words.

They mean that people may communicate affection, attention, discomfort, enthusiasm, and care in different ways.

But there isn’t one autistic language, one ADHD language, and one neurotypical language. Each person has their own habits, preferences, and expectations.

Consider what the word support might mean.

For one person, support means being listened to without interruption.

For another, it means someone helping solve the problem.

Someone else might prefer to be left alone until they’re ready to talk.

None of these preferences is automatically wrong. But they can create misunderstandings when partners assume their own preference is universal.

Remember the double empathy problem from Chapter 19: people who experience and interpret the world differently can misunderstand each other in both directions.

One person might have difficulty recognizing what their partner wants. Their partner might have equal difficulty recognizing what they are trying to communicate.

In interviews with autistic adults in mixed-neurotype relationships, participants described learning how non-autistic partners communicated while also wanting their own communication styles to be understood. They valued reciprocal effort rather than one person always adapting (Ciric et al., 2026).

That word, reciprocal, matters.

It means both people contribute.

It doesn’t mean both people make exactly the same adjustments. One partner might find verbal conversations easier while the other communicates better in writing. Their contributions may look different.

But the relationship shouldn’t permanently require one person to translate everything, explain everything, and abandon their own needs while the other makes no effort.

Learn what an action means to your partner

Return to Jordan and Alex.

Instead of deciding that the other person’s behavior means something negative, they could compare their interpretations.

Alex says:

“I know you were trying to help with my work problem. When you gave me a solution and stopped talking, I felt like you didn’t want to hear any more.”

Jordan answers:

“I thought the solution would help, and I thought sitting together quietly would be comforting. I didn’t realize you wanted to keep talking.”

Alex explains:

“When I’m upset, I usually want to talk before thinking about solutions.”

Jordan asks:

“Would it help if I asked whether you wanted advice or just someone to listen?”

Alex agrees. Then Jordan adds something of their own:

“Sometimes I need quiet after work too. Can we make room for that without assuming I’m upset with you?”

Now they have something useful.

They haven’t decided that one partner is too emotional or the other is too distant. They’ve discovered what their behavior communicates to each other.

The specific arrangement is theirs to choose.

One couple might use a simple question before giving advice. Another might agree that serious conversations happen after dinner. Another might prefer discussing difficult subjects through messages because writing gives both people time to think.

These are practical options, not scientifically proven formulas for mixed-neurotype couples.

The point is to replace assumptions with information.

The partner who isn’t neurodivergent has needs too

Books about neurodivergent relationships sometimes concentrate so heavily on understanding the neurodivergent partner that the other person becomes almost invisible.

Their job appears to be understanding, accommodating, reminding, forgiving, and remaining patient.

That’s not a complete picture of a relationship.

A non-autistic partner might love someone deeply and still struggle with aspects of their life together.

They might want more conversation about feelings. They might find it difficult when affection is expressed in ways they don’t immediately recognize. They might feel unsure whether their partner wants closeness, solitude, reassurance, or practical help.

They may become tired of initiating every difficult conversation.

These aren’t necessarily unreasonable needs. They’re experiences that deserve to be discussed.

If you're dating someone who needs more solitude than you do

Wanting more conversation, or more visible reassurance, doesn’t make you demanding. It isn’t something to quietly give up in order to count as understanding.

Say it as a preference rather than a complaint: “I know you need quiet evenings. I need to talk things through sometimes. Can we find a shape for the week that gives us both some of that?”

A relationship where only one person’s needs are treated as real will eventually exhaust whoever is doing the accommodating. Yours belong in the conversation too.

In a small 2025 study involving six non-autistic partners of autistic people, participants described confusing interactions but also positive and happy relationships. Understanding autism helped some make sense of their experiences, while participants emphasized that autism was only one aspect of the person and relationship (Edge & Parker, 2025).

A separate interview study found that differences in desired social interaction could become frustrating. Some non-autistic participants wanted more emotional conversation or responsiveness, while some autistic participants needed more solitude or experienced repeated requests for interaction as difficult (Wilson et al., 2023).

These studies are small and selective. They don’t describe every non-autistic partner, and they don’t establish that all autistic people prefer less interaction.

But they identify a problem worth taking seriously.

Understanding why something is difficult for your partner doesn’t automatically remove your own need for it.

Suppose Maya needs to spend an hour alone after coming home from work. Her wife, Denise, enjoys talking about their day as soon as they’re together.

Maya’s need for quiet is legitimate.

Denise’s desire for connection is legitimate.

Denise shouldn’t insist that Maya talk through overload. Maya shouldn’t insist that Denise stop wanting conversation.

They could agree that Maya takes an hour to recover and that afterward they spend some time together talking, provided both are willing and able.

If that arrangement doesn’t work, they can revisit it.

The goal isn’t to find out whose need wins. It’s to determine whether both needs can be respected.

Sometimes a workable arrangement exists. Sometimes it doesn’t.

Understanding shouldn’t become self-erasure

There is a difference between being flexible and continually suppressing yourself.

If you’re the partner who communicates more easily in conventional social settings, you may find yourself doing most of the explaining. That can become exhausting.

If you’re the partner whose communication is often misunderstood, you may find yourself constantly monitoring your expressions, tone, eye contact, and behavior to prevent misunderstandings. That can also be exhausting.

Masking, hiding or suppressing your natural traits to fit in, shouldn’t be the price of being loved.

Neither should abandoning every reasonable preference because your partner experiences the world differently.

A more useful question than “Who’s being difficult?” is:

“What is each person being asked to give up, and can that arrangement last?”

When forgetfulness is mistaken for not caring

Attention and memory can create a different kind of misunderstanding.

Imagine that Marcus, who has ADHD, promises his boyfriend, Luis, that he’ll arrange an appointment to get their car serviced.

He intends to do it.

He forgets.

Luis reminds him. Marcus apologizes and says he’ll do it tomorrow.

Tomorrow comes. He forgets again.

Luis begins to feel that their shared responsibilities aren’t important to Marcus.

Marcus feels awful because he does care. He just keeps failing to act on something he genuinely intends to do.

If you keep meaning to do the thing you promised

Saying “I’ll do it tomorrow” costs your partner something even when you mean it sincerely each time. The apology stops landing long before the intention fades.

Change the system rather than the promise. Do the task while you’re saying you’ll do it, put it somewhere that interrupts you, or agree out loud who owns it. “I’ll book it now, while we’re talking about it” is worth more than a better apology.

Caring and remembering are different skills. Your partner is reacting to the second one, not doubting the first.

After several repetitions, Luis is doing the task himself, along with most of the reminding.

Both are upset, for different reasons.

In a 2026 Irish study analyzing 335 usable responses from people with ADHD, participants described forgetfulness being interpreted as a lack of love or interest. Some described intense attention early in relationships that became harder to sustain over time. Others reported feeling ashamed, burdensome, or dependent on their partners (O’Brien et al., 2026).

Importantly, the survey asked participants specifically about negative relationship impacts. It tells us what some respondents found difficult, not how common every difficulty is among people with ADHD or how their partners would describe the same situations.

A 2021 review also found that ADHD was associated with greater relationship difficulties in the literature, while noting that research on what specifically helps couples was limited (Wymbs et al., 2021).

The distinction Marcus and Luis need is simple, though not always easy to act on:

An explanation is not an excuse, and an unmet responsibility is not automatically evidence of a lack of love.

Marcus isn’t necessarily forgetting because he doesn’t care.

But Luis shouldn’t have to accept indefinitely doing everything Marcus forgets.

Both truths can exist at once.

The next step isn’t another apology followed by exactly the same arrangement. It’s choosing a different arrangement.

Marcus could put the appointment in a calendar with a reminder he controls. He could make the call while they’re already discussing it. They could redistribute responsibilities if another task is a better fit for him.

None of these methods is guaranteed to work. Marcus needs to participate in finding something he can reliably use.

Luis doesn’t need to become his supervisor.

When a partner becomes a manager

Sometimes one person gradually becomes responsible not only for their own tasks but also for monitoring everything the other person is supposed to do.

They remember appointments, check whether bills were paid, notice missing groceries, arrange social plans, and remind their partner about unfinished work.

Eventually, they may feel less like an equal partner and more like a household manager.

Meanwhile, the person receiving reminders may feel criticized, controlled, or incapable of doing anything correctly.

A study of autistic and non-autistic people’s close relationships described how increasing reminders and prompts could become part of a frustrating interaction cycle. One person kept prompting, while the other increasingly relied on or avoided those prompts (Wilson et al., 2023).

The ADHD study also included participants describing caregiver-like roles and uneven household responsibilities (O’Brien et al., 2026).

Research involving both spouses has identified strain as well. In a Turkish study comparing 28 married couples with an ADHD partner against 28 comparison couples, the ADHD couples reported more conflict and lower marital adjustment. Both partners participated, and their perceptions weren’t always identical (Kahveci Öncü, 2022).

An Israeli comparison study similarly found lower reported intimacy and marital satisfaction among spouses of adults with ADHD than among comparison spouses (Ben-Naim et al., 2017).

These findings don’t mean that relationships involving ADHD inevitably become unequal. The studies were observational, and they don’t establish that ADHD alone caused the difficulties.

They do show why taking both partners’ practical experiences seriously matters.

If you're dating someone who forgets what you asked

Being the person who remembers everything is tiring, and resenting it doesn’t make you unkind. Say so plainly, early, before it hardens into contempt.

What usually helps is moving a task out of memory altogether rather than improving the reminders: a shared calendar, a standing day, or doing it immediately instead of promising it. Reminding harder tends to turn you into a supervisor, which is corrosive for both of you.

Forgetting isn’t a message about how much you matter. It is still a problem you’re allowed to want solved, and solving it is a joint job.

Share responsibility, not just reminders

Consider three different questions about a household task:

“Who remembers this needs doing?”

“Who actually does it?”

“Who makes sure it gets finished?”

Sometimes all three responsibilities fall on one person, even when the couple believes the task itself is shared.

A useful conversation might sound like this:

“Right now, I’m remembering when the bills are due, reminding you to pay them, and checking whether they were paid. I’m getting tired of managing the whole process. Can we agree on a system you can manage yourself?”

The answer might involve automated payments, an agreed calendar, a redistribution of tasks, or another arrangement suited to their circumstances.

If automation or another tool creates financial or practical risks, both people should agree on how those risks will be handled.

The measure of success isn’t whether the arrangement looks conventional.

It’s whether responsibilities get handled and both partners consider the arrangement fair.

One person might enjoy planning meals while the other enjoys cooking. One might handle paperwork while the other handles most cleaning. Someone with limited energy might contribute differently during a difficult period.

Fairness doesn’t always mean splitting every individual task in half.

It does mean that neither person’s time, effort, or capacity is treated as limitless.

And supporting a partner’s difficulty should never become permission to control their phone, monitor their movements, or demand access to their private information.

If a reminder system is useful, it should be agreed upon, not imposed.

What if both partners are neurodivergent?

It’s tempting to imagine that if both partners are neurodivergent, they’ll automatically understand each other.

They might understand some experiences immediately.

But their needs can still conflict.

The US survey of 106 autistic adults included 46 participants whose partners were described as non-autistic but otherwise neurodivergent. Overall satisfaction wasn’t statistically different across the study’s partner categories. However, the researchers grouped different neurotypes together and collected responses only from the autistic partners. The results don’t tell us specifically how autistic-ADHD couples compare with other couples (Khaw & Vernon, 2026).

Some participants in the Irish ADHD study described feeling more understood by neurodivergent partners, including partners with ADHD. Those were individual accounts, not a comparison showing that such pairings consistently work better (O’Brien et al., 2026).

The research is still too limited to rank these combinations.

What matters in practice is understanding the specific differences between the people involved.

When one person needs predictability and the other wants variety

Nico is autistic and likes knowing what the weekend will look like by Thursday.

His girlfriend, Priya, has ADHD and enjoys deciding what to do when Saturday arrives. She doesn’t want every free hour planned in advance.

Nico interprets last-minute suggestions as disruption.

Priya interprets a detailed schedule as a restriction.

Neither is trying to make the other unhappy.

Their different preferences create a practical problem.

They might agree to plan Saturday morning in advance and leave the afternoon open for optional activities. Or they could establish which plans are firm and which can change.

Nico could decline spontaneous outings without being treated as unloving. Priya could occasionally choose spontaneous activities with friends without being treated as disloyal.

The important point is that both people retain choices.

A compromise doesn’t require one partner to become spontaneous or the other to enjoy rigid schedules.

And if one partner repeatedly changes important agreed plans, the other person is allowed to object. A preference for novelty doesn’t cancel an agreement.

When both people forget

Imagine two partners who both have trouble remembering appointments, paying bills on time, or beginning unpleasant tasks.

They may have tremendous empathy for each other’s difficulties.

But empathy alone won’t make the rent get paid.

They might need a shared calendar, automated reminders, clearly assigned responsibilities, or help from outside the relationship.

They should also consider what happens when both have a difficult week.

An arrangement that depends on one person always being able to compensate for the other’s attention difficulties may not be dependable when both experience them.

Neither person has to become the designated organized adult.

They do need a realistic way of handling the responsibilities they’ve accepted.

When the same label hides different needs

Two autistic partners might both experience sensory overload, but in different ways.

One finds music soothing. The other needs complete quiet.

One wants physical closeness after a difficult day. The other doesn’t want to be touched when overloaded.

One finds long conversations enjoyable. The other prefers shorter conversations separated by breaks.

They may recognize each other’s experiences without wanting the same things.

Knowing that a partner is autistic doesn’t tell you whether they enjoy hugs, how much solitude they need, or whether they prefer written communication.

You still have to ask.

The same applies to any shared diagnosis.

A label can provide useful information about possible experiences. It can’t replace learning the individual.

If you and your partner have the same diagnosis

Sharing a label can make the early part of a relationship unusually easy. You may not have to explain why you want to leave early, or apologize for how you talk.

That ease can hide where you differ. Two autistic people can still disagree about touch, noise, plans and how much time alone is normal, and a shared label can make those ordinary differences feel like betrayal.

Keep asking the specific questions you would ask anyone. Being understood in general is not the same as being known in particular.

Learn what your partner means by care

You don’t need to understand every detail of your partner’s brain to have a useful conversation about your relationship.

You do need to know what specific behaviors mean to each other.

Research interviewing mixed-neurotype couples has described the importance of genuine care, support, and recognizing each person’s strengths (Smith et al., 2021). The newer US study also found that support, acceptance, communication, and authenticity appeared in descriptions across partner groups (Khaw & Vernon, 2026).

Those findings provide useful themes, but they don’t prove that a particular conversation exercise will improve a relationship.

The following exercise is simply a way to make those themes concrete.

A two-sided translation check

Choose a calm time when neither person is already upset. Each partner answers the same four questions.

1. When do you feel cared for?

Try to name an observable action rather than a vague quality.

Instead of “When you’re thoughtful,” you might say, “When you remember something important to me and ask how it went.”

Or: “When you sit with me quietly, even if neither of us is talking.”

2. What do you do to show you care that I might not notice?

One person might prepare food when their partner is busy.

Another might share articles about subjects their partner enjoys.

Someone else might give their partner space because that’s the kind of kindness they personally appreciate.

These gestures are invitations to understand each other. They aren’t debts that automatically require appreciation or repayment.

3. What makes communication difficult for you, and what helps?

Answers might include needing time before answering, becoming overloaded by several questions at once, forgetting the original topic during a long discussion, or struggling to identify a feeling immediately.

The other partner should answer too, even if they don’t identify as neurodivergent.

4. What’s one change you’d like us to try?

Keep this manageable.

“Could you tell me when you need some quiet time, instead of leaving me guessing?”

“Could we put appointments somewhere both of us can see them?”

“Could you ask whether I want advice before suggesting a solution?”

“Could we spend twenty minutes together without our phones after dinner?”

Each person should be free to say that a proposed change doesn’t work for them.

Choose something both partners agree to try. Afterward, check whether it actually helped.

If the change only makes one person’s life easier by making the other’s much harder, revisit it.

The purpose is to discover what works for these two people, not to pass a relationship test.

A diagnosis can explain something without settling an argument

Learning about autism or ADHD can change how partners interpret past misunderstandings.

Someone who thought their partner was deliberately ignoring them might recognize that attention was difficult to sustain.

Someone who thought their partner disliked affection might learn that certain kinds of touch are uncomfortable.

Someone who had spent years blaming themselves for struggling with ordinary tasks might finally have a useful explanation for those difficulties.

Participants in the Irish ADHD study described self-understanding as important, including relief from some of the blame they had placed on themselves (O’Brien et al., 2026).

Understanding can make a difference. But it doesn’t settle every disagreement.

Consider these two statements:

“I sometimes forget commitments because remembering future tasks is difficult for me.”

“You aren’t allowed to be upset when I forget a commitment because I have ADHD.”

The first explains a difficulty.

The second attempts to determine what the other person is permitted to feel.

Likewise:

“I need time alone after social situations because I get overloaded.”

“You aren’t allowed to ask for time together because I’m autistic.”

One describes a need. The other dismisses the partner’s need.

Your neurotype matters. Your partner’s needs still matter too.

And this principle goes both ways.

A non-autistic partner shouldn’t say, “You’re autistic, so you don’t understand relationships.”

A partner without ADHD shouldn’t say, “If you cared enough, you’d remember everything.”

Those responses turn differences into judgments about character.

Instead, describe the specific problem, its consequences, and what you need to change.

You can be understanding without accepting an arrangement that isn’t working.

You can take responsibility without pretending a genuine difficulty will disappear through effort alone.

Don’t make one person’s way of living the standard

In some relationships, the partner whose behavior fits more conventional expectations is treated as the default.

Their preferred amount of conversation becomes the normal amount.

Their comfort with social gatherings becomes the normal level of social activity.

Their way of expressing emotion becomes the correct way.

The other person is expected to adapt.

But being more conventional doesn’t automatically make a preference more important.

The reverse can happen too. A person may become so focused on making life suitable for their own sensory, communication, or attention needs that they overlook what their partner needs.

Neither approach creates equality.

Think back to the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book.

You and your partner are looking at the same relationship from different positions, through different experiences.

The purpose isn’t to prove which view is correct.

It’s to describe what each person sees and decide what to do with that information.

This doesn’t mean every disagreement is merely a difference in perspective. Sometimes someone has failed to keep an agreement, acted unfairly, or crossed a boundary.

In those situations, understanding the reason doesn’t eliminate responsibility.

But when the disagreement concerns legitimate differences in preferences or needs, deciding that one partner’s way is inherently normal won’t help.

What equal effort actually means

Equal effort isn’t necessarily identical behavior.

One person may find it easy to make phone calls but extremely difficult to organize complicated paperwork.

The other may find paperwork straightforward but become exhausted by phone conversations.

They can contribute differently.

One person may need more time to answer difficult questions. The other may need more reassurance that a difficult conversation hasn’t been abandoned.

They can agree on an approach that makes room for both needs.

The important questions are whether each person is participating, whether expectations are realistic, and whether the arrangement feels fair over time.

You shouldn’t have to demonstrate love by continually pushing through overload.

Your partner shouldn’t have to demonstrate love by silently accepting that their needs will never be met.

Learning each other’s language is supposed to expand understanding, not erase a person.

When understanding isn’t enough

Some differences can be accommodated. Others may reveal a serious incompatibility.

If one person wants to spend nearly every evening together and the other wants substantial time alone, there may be several possible arrangements.

But if neither person can accept the amount of togetherness the other genuinely needs, recognizing that mismatch is more useful than blaming either partner.

A couple may understand each other perfectly well and still want fundamentally different lives.

A relationship also isn’t healthy simply because each partner has a good explanation for their behavior.

Autism, ADHD, trauma, or another difference doesn’t excuse intimidation, coercion, humiliation, controlling behavior, or violations of consent.

If a partner repeatedly crosses your boundaries, you don’t have to keep searching for a better way to explain them.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly disregarding your partner’s boundaries, the next step is to stop that behavior and take responsibility for changing it, not to ask your partner for unlimited patience.

These are questions of behavior and safety, not competing communication styles. The earlier chapters on boundaries, consent, and harmful behavior provide the fuller guidance.

There is also a difference between someone struggling with a skill and someone refusing to participate in solving a shared problem.

A person may sincerely need help with planning, initiating conversations, remembering tasks, or recognizing their own emotions.

They can still be expected to acknowledge the impact of their behavior and participate, within their capacity, in finding workable solutions.

If repeated attempts haven’t helped, that doesn’t automatically mean either person has failed.

It may mean the couple needs support beyond what they can provide each other, or that a particular arrangement cannot meet both people’s needs.

A relationship is bigger than its difficulties

Research on neurodivergent relationships often focuses on problems.

A 2025 review examining 42 articles published between 2013 and 2024 identified gaps in research on strengths, diverse couples, and studies that collect information from both partners (Neu & Bradford, 2025).

That matters because understanding relationships requires more than cataloging what goes wrong.

People also choose partners because they enjoy each other’s company.

They may appreciate a partner’s honesty, creativity, humor, reliability, curiosity, affection, or enthusiasm.

They may like spending quiet mornings together, taking long walks, building things, discovering restaurants, or discussing a subject neither person ever gets tired of.

In small interview studies of mixed-neurotype couples, participants described love, care, support, and positive relationships alongside their difficulties (Edge & Parker, 2025; Smith et al., 2021).

Being neurodivergent doesn’t make a person incapable of offering those things. Having a neurodivergent partner doesn’t require the other person to accept a relationship without them.

And having a successful relationship doesn’t require eliminating every misunderstanding.

What matters is whether the people involved can recognize a problem, take each other’s experiences seriously, make changes where possible, and remain themselves.

You don’t need a partner who experiences the world exactly as you do.

You need someone whose differences you can respect, whose needs you can take seriously, and who offers you that same respect.

Sometimes that person shares your neurotype. Sometimes they don’t.

Either way, a healthy relationship is built by two people, not by a diagnosis.

There will also be times when communication tools and good intentions aren’t enough. Past experiences can affect what feels safe, threatening, or familiar in a relationship, even when your current partner means no harm. That’s the subject of the next chapter.

And when the same difficulties keep returning despite your efforts, seeking outside support is a reasonable option. A partner doesn’t have to be your only source of understanding, and you don’t have to solve every problem together without help.
