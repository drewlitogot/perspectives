Content note: This chapter discusses unwanted contact, harassment, stalking, and personal safety. You can skip the examples and go directly to “If someone is doing this to you” if you need practical help.

Mara and Elise have been on two dates. Mara wants to keep seeing Elise, but Elise says she doesn’t feel a romantic connection.

Mara is disappointed. She thought their conversations went well. She wonders whether Elise was nervous, whether the timing was wrong, or whether another date might change things.

None of those questions changes Elise’s answer.

Mara can feel disappointed. She can even wish Elise would reconsider. But if Mara keeps pursuing Elise after that refusal, her interest has become pressure.

This distinction matters because dating advice sometimes celebrates persistence. Stories about romance often show someone refusing to give up until the other person finally returns their feelings. The audience knows how the story ends, so the pursuit looks worthwhile.

In real life, nobody knows how someone else’s story ends. And the person being pursued gets to decide whether they want to be part of it.

Strong feelings explain why you want contact. They do not give you permission to continue it.

When persistence stops being romantic

Persistence can be a valuable quality. It helps you learn skills, keep commitments, and work toward goals.

But another person’s romantic interest is not a goal you can achieve through determination alone.

Think about the difference between two situations.

In the first, two people like each other. They have busy schedules and keep trying to arrange a date. Both want to meet. The persistence is mutual.

In the second, one person wants to date and the other does not. The interested person keeps trying because they believe their sincerity, patience, or effort will eventually change the answer.

That is not mutual persistence. It is pressure.

The earlier chapters explained how to say boundaries out loud, respect someone else’s boundaries, and understand consent. The same principles apply here.

A person may communicate disinterest by explicitly declining, asking to be left alone, ending contact, or choosing not to engage. Silence can have many explanations, but it is not permission to intensify your efforts.

You do not need to understand why someone has lost interest before respecting that decision.

They might have found you attractive but incompatible. They might be seeing someone else. They might be uncomfortable. They might simply not want another date.

You may never know.

You can ask someone to date you. You cannot require them to keep considering you.

What about showing someone you’re serious?

Showing genuine interest means being honest about what you want while leaving the other person free to choose.

It does not mean demonstrating how much rejection you can tolerate.

If someone declines, you have received the information needed to make your next decision: stop pursuing them.

A person is allowed to change their mind about dating you. But that possibility does not justify waiting for them, monitoring their life, or repeatedly presenting yourself as an option.

If they freely decide to reconnect someday, that decision belongs to them. It is not something you can engineer.

There is no special number of attempts after which persistence suddenly becomes wrong. Respect begins with how you respond to the other person’s wishes, not with counting how many times you’ve contacted them.

Harassment and stalking are not the same thing

These words are related, but they do not mean exactly the same thing.

Harassment, in everyday language, refers to unwanted behavior that disturbs, intimidates, pressures, or interferes with another person. Its legal definition depends on the situation and the applicable law.

Stalking generally involves a pattern of unwanted conduct directed at a particular person that causes fear or serious distress.

The US Department of Justice describes stalking as a course of conduct directed at a specific person that would cause a reasonable person to fear for their safety or someone else’s safety, or experience substantial emotional distress. (Office on Violence Against Women, 2023)

A course of conduct means a pattern of behavior, rather than an isolated event.

From the receiving end, stalking may involve unwanted contact, intrusions into someone’s physical or digital life, or a sense that another person is continually watching or pursuing them. These experiences can occur within a current relationship, after a breakup, or between people who never dated. (RAINN, 2026; Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.a)

Not every uncomfortable encounter or unwanted message legally qualifies as stalking.

That distinction matters, especially when deciding how to describe behavior to police, an employer, or a court.

But the reverse matters just as much:

You do not need to prove that something meets the legal definition of stalking before you are allowed to feel unsafe, end contact, or ask for help.

A behavior can cross your boundary before it crosses a criminal threshold.

What about online stalking?

Online spaces are part of people’s lives, so unwanted pursuit does not have to happen face-to-face.

Someone experiencing online harassment or stalking might receive repeated unwanted messages, discover that another person is intruding into their private accounts, or become concerned that their activities or location are being monitored without permission.

These behaviors may be part of stalking, technology-facilitated abuse, or another form of harassment, depending on the circumstances. (National Network to End Domestic Violence, Safety Net Project, n.d.; Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.a)

The fact that something is technically visible online does not make it an invitation to use that information to pursue a person.

Likewise, having previously shared a password, location, or account access does not mean someone has unlimited permission to monitor you.

The earlier chapter on boundaries covered digital privacy. Here the important extension is that repeatedly violating digital boundaries can become part of a larger pattern of harm.

What does US law say?

Stalking is a crime throughout the United States, including the District of Columbia. However, the requirements for proving it, the available legal protections, and the penalties differ across jurisdictions. (RAINN, 2026; Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.b)

Harassment laws also vary. Some situations can involve federal law, while others fall under state or local law.

For example, a particular pattern may be distressing and clearly unwanted without meeting every element of a criminal stalking statute. In another situation, conduct may meet more than one legal definition.

This book cannot determine which laws apply to an individual situation.

The Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center (SPARC) maintains information about US stalking laws. A local victim advocate or qualified legal professional can help explain what applies where you live. (Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.b)

Stalking happens to people of different genders

Stalking is not limited to one kind of victim, one kind of relationship, or one gender.

The CDC’s 2023–2024 National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey estimated that 22.5% of US women and 9.7% of US men had experienced stalking during their lifetimes. Approximately 5.5% of women and 3.0% of men reported it within the previous 12 months. (Smith et al., 2025, pp. 1–4)

These are survey estimates, not predictions about whether a particular person will be targeted.

The survey’s findings also show that the consequences can be serious. Among people classified as stalking victims, 85.2% of women and 71.6% of men reported mental or emotional harm. (Smith et al., 2025)

There are important limits to these numbers. The CDC survey relied on self-reports, had a 16.5% response rate, and reported these figures using woman and man categories. They cannot establish a separate stalking rate for nonbinary people. The survey also used definitions and methods that should not be directly compared with earlier CDC survey cycles. (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025; Smith et al., 2025)

The Bureau of Justice Statistics conducted a different US survey in 2019 and found that only 29% of people identified as stalking victims reported their experience to police. That finding comes from a separate survey with different methods and should not be used to calculate a trend alongside the newer CDC figures. (Morgan & Truman, 2022)

These findings make two points worth remembering.

First, stalking is a serious problem that affects more than one gender. Nobody should assume a man cannot be a victim, that a woman cannot be responsible, or that a nonbinary person’s experiences are less important because survey data are limited.

Second, police reports do not represent everyone who experiences stalking. A person may seek support without immediately involving law enforcement.

The problem with romantic stories about never giving up

Many familiar romantic stories reward the person who keeps pursuing someone after being turned down.

The pursuer’s effort is treated as evidence of love. The other person’s refusal becomes something temporary, perhaps even a test of sincerity.

This story has an uncomfortable implication: that the person who says no does not really know what they want.

One experiment investigated how films portraying persistent romantic pursuit affected beliefs about stalking. A frightening portrayal reduced support for stalking-related beliefs. Romanticized pursuit did not increase those beliefs across all participants, but it did among certain viewers who experienced the film as especially realistic or immersive. (Lippman, 2018)

That is one study about beliefs, not proof that watching a romantic movie causes stalking.

Still, it offers a reason to examine the stories people absorb about romance.

Consider a film in which someone rejects an invitation repeatedly but ultimately agrees to a relationship. The ending can make the earlier pursuit seem justified.

Real people do not owe others that ending.

Someone may have a wonderful personality, sincere intentions, and a long history of feeling rejected. None of those things changes another person’s right to refuse contact.

The lesson is not that romantic effort is wrong. Effort becomes meaningful when another person wants to receive it.

When a crush becomes fixation

Chapter 16, Crushes, infatuation and fixation, discussed what happens when your thoughts keep returning to someone.

You may imagine conversations, replay interactions, or feel that meeting this person has changed your entire future.

Those experiences can be intense without becoming harmful behavior.

The dividing line is what you do with them.

Suppose Ari, a 30-year-old nonbinary person, develops a strong crush on a friend, Jordan. Ari spends months imagining what dating Jordan would be like.

Eventually, Ari learns that Jordan does not want a romantic relationship.

Ari feels hurt. The imagined future is difficult to give up.

Those feelings are real. But Jordan’s refusal is real too.

Ari can acknowledge the disappointment, make space for other activities, and seek support without making Jordan responsible for resolving the feelings.

The problem would be treating Jordan’s refusal as unfinished business that Ari has a right to keep pursuing.

You can be deeply attached to an imagined future while the other person has never agreed to share it.

If you need an explanation before you can move on

Sometimes rejection leaves questions that feel impossible to put down.

You might wonder what you misunderstood, what changed, or whether the other person would feel differently if you could explain yourself better.

Not having answers can be uncomfortable.

But the other person does not have to provide a detailed explanation for their decision to count.

You can recognize that something is unresolved in your mind while accepting that the relationship, or the possibility of one, has ended.

That can mean living with an unanswered question instead of trying to get another answer from the person who wants no contact.

If indirect messages are difficult to interpret

If hints are difficult for you to understand, you may prefer direct communication about romantic interest. That preference is reasonable. In one small, older Australian study, parents of autistic adolescents and adults reported differences in romantic learning and pursuit after negative or absent responses. However, the study involved only 25 autistic participants, relied on parent reports, and did not establish criminal stalking or generalizable rates. (Stokes et al., 2007)

A useful personal rule is to treat a clear refusal as final and a lack of engagement as a reason not to increase contact. You may ask for clarification in ordinary conversations where the other person is willing to communicate. But someone who has declined contact does not owe you another explanation, regardless of your communication needs.

If your thoughts will not leave the subject alone

You might find yourself returning to the same person mentally, even when you have decided to stop pursuing them.

Perhaps concentrating on work is difficult. Perhaps the possibility of rejection brings up much older feelings. Perhaps your attention keeps returning to one imagined conversation.

This can happen without making you a dangerous person.

What matters is recognizing that a strong impulse does not require action.

You can feel the urge to seek reassurance and choose not to contact the person. You can notice yourself imagining a different outcome without acting as though that outcome has been promised.

The sources reviewed for this chapter do not establish that ADHD, autism, or a history of trauma causes someone to stalk. Nor do they establish that intense fixation inevitably becomes harassment.

A diagnosis or explanation may help you understand your experiences. It does not remove your responsibility for what you do.

If someone is doing this to you

Imagine that Theo, a 42-year-old man, ended a relationship with his boyfriend. Theo has clearly communicated that he does not want further contact.

Afterward, he experiences unwanted attention that makes him increasingly concerned about his privacy and safety.

Theo wonders whether he is making too much of it.

Perhaps his former partner is lonely. Perhaps he should explain himself once more. Perhaps other people will not take the situation seriously because both people are men.

None of those thoughts means Theo has to tolerate continued unwanted contact.

If someone is repeatedly intruding into your life, you can seek help.

You do not have to wait until you are certain that a crime has occurred.

Start with your safety

If you are in immediate danger in the United States, call 911 if you can do so safely.

If the danger is not immediate, consider speaking with someone trained to help people experiencing stalking or abuse.

A victim advocate can help you think through risks involving your home, workplace, school, social connections, and online accounts. Safety planning is personal because different situations carry different risks. (Office on Violence Against Women, 2023; Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.c)

You can also tell someone you trust what is happening.

For example:

“Someone keeps contacting me even though I don’t want it. I’m uncomfortable, and I’d like help figuring out what to do.”

You do not need a perfect explanation or a legal label before asking for support.

Keep a record when it is safe

Stalking can involve incidents that appear separate until someone looks at the larger pattern.

Specialist organizations recommend documenting incidents and preserving available communications or other evidence when it is safe to do so. This information may help an advocate understand the situation or support a later report or request for protection. (National Network to End Domestic Violence, Safety Net Project, n.d.; Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.c)

A basic record can include when something happened, what you experienced, and any evidence you already have.

But documentation is not more important than your immediate safety.

If someone may have access to your phone, computer, or accounts, keeping a record there could expose information you do not want them to see.

The National Network to End Domestic Violence’s Safety Net Project provides guidance about documenting technology-related abuse and protecting that information. (National Network to End Domestic Violence, Safety Net Project, n.d.)

You also do not need to collect evidence personally if doing so would expose you to additional danger. An advocate can help you decide what is practical.

You do not have to confront the person

Some advice about unwanted attention tells people to be firmer, give a final warning, or explain their feelings one more time.

That is not always safe.

If you have already communicated that you do not want contact, you are not responsible for making the other person understand.

If you have not communicated it directly, you still do not owe someone a confrontation when doing so feels unsafe.

RAINN explains that avoiding contact is generally advisable, but questions such as whether to block someone depend on the circumstances. Blocking may stop some unwanted communication, while other safety considerations, including evidence preservation, may matter. (RAINN, 2026)

You do not have to choose between blocking and not blocking based on a universal rule. A stalking-informed advocate can help you consider the safest option for your circumstances.

Take digital monitoring concerns seriously

If you suspect someone is monitoring your phone or online accounts without permission, making immediate technical changes may not always be the safest first action.

The Federal Trade Commission warns that suspected surveillance software can create risks when a person changes or removes it without a safety plan. The FTC advises considering a safer device when seeking help and getting advice before taking steps that could alert the person responsible. (Federal Trade Commission, 2026)

This does not mean you must continue using a device you distrust. It means your safety matters more than following a generic technical checklist.

An advocate familiar with technology-facilitated abuse can help you consider appropriate options.

You are allowed to respond differently from other people

Some people who experience stalking are frightened. Others become angry, exhausted, irritated, numb, or uncertain.

SPARC cautions that victims do not always display the emotions outsiders expect. A person’s outward reaction should not determine whether their experience is taken seriously. (Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.a)

You might freeze when asked to describe what happened. You might struggle to explain why a particular interaction felt threatening. You might only understand the pattern after several incidents.

You are still entitled to seek support.

You also do not have to make an immediate decision about reporting to law enforcement. An advocate can explain options without making that decision for you.

Where to find help in the US

Several organizations can help you identify appropriate support:

  • VictimConnect: 855-4-VICTIM (855-484-2846), for information about victim assistance and referrals.
  • National Domestic Violence Hotline: 800-799-SAFE (7233), particularly when the person involved is a current or former partner.
  • RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline: 800-656-HOPE (4673), when sexual violence is part of the situation.
  • SPARC: stalkingawareness.org, for educational information, including links to stalking laws and safety resources.

These services and resources are identified in US victim-support and stalking guidance. SPARC provides information rather than direct case services. (Office on Violence Against Women, 2023; RAINN, 2026; Stalking Prevention, Awareness, and Resource Center, n.d.c)

A local advocate can also discuss whether a protective order or police report may be appropriate. Laws and procedures differ by jurisdiction.

No single action guarantees that unwanted pursuit will stop. The aim is to make informed decisions with support rather than face the situation alone.

If you recognize yourself doing the pursuing

This section may be uncomfortable to read.

Perhaps you have continued contacting someone after they asked you to stop. Perhaps you have treated silence as an invitation to try harder. Perhaps you have been paying more attention to another person’s life than they have agreed to share.

Recognizing these behaviors does not require deciding that you are permanently a bad person.

It does require recognizing that what you do next matters.

Stop the unwanted contact and monitoring.

Do not make another attempt to explain your intentions. Do not ask the person to reassure you that you are forgiven. Do not use mutual friends to obtain contact or persuade the person to respond.

If someone has made it clear that they want to be left alone, leave them alone.

An apology is not always yours to deliver

Earlier chapters described how apologies can help repair ordinary misunderstandings.

But not every situation allows for another conversation.

If someone has ended contact, sending an apology may mean ignoring the very boundary you are supposed to be respecting.

Your desire to apologize does not create an obligation for the other person to receive it.

You can take responsibility without asking them to participate.

That may mean accepting that they will never hear your explanation, agree that you have changed, or offer forgiveness.

You cannot control how they remember the experience.

You can control whether you continue the behavior.

Get help with the behavior, not with winning the person back

If you repeatedly feel driven to contact, monitor, or pursue someone who does not want contact, consider speaking with a qualified mental-health professional or appropriate behavior-change service.

Describe the behavior accurately.

It is possible to seek help with unwanted pursuit while also discussing the loneliness, fixation, rejection, or distress you are experiencing.

But those feelings should not become excuses.

The goal is not to become more convincing or get the person back. The goal is to stop causing harm and learn to respect other people’s decisions, including decisions that hurt.

You can also ask a trusted person to support your accountability, provided they do not contact the person you pursued or become involved in pressuring them.

If a court order or legal restriction applies, follow it. Seek qualified legal advice about your obligations rather than relying on advice from friends or social media.

The research reviewed for this chapter does not identify a proven treatment program for every person who engages in stalking behavior. Seeking appropriate professional help is a practical recommendation, not a promise that any particular approach will succeed.

A short exercise: feelings and actions

If you are struggling after rejection, try separating what is happening inside you from what you choose to do.

Write two headings on a private page:

What I’m feeling

This might include disappointment, jealousy, confusion, loneliness, anger, longing, or uncertainty.

What I’m responsible for

This includes respecting the other person’s decision, stopping unwanted contact, managing your actions, and seeking support without involving the person who wants distance.

Then ask yourself:

  • Am I respecting what this person has decided, even though I dislike the answer?
  • Am I treating my feelings as a reason they should give me something?
  • What can I do today that does not involve contacting or monitoring them?

Your feelings may take time to settle.

Respectful behavior does not have to wait for that process to finish.

If you’re dating someone whose communication works differently

Not everyone communicates interest, discomfort, or rejection in the same way.

Some people prefer direct statements. Others avoid confrontation. Someone may need extra time to understand their feelings or may have difficulty speaking when under stress.

Those differences matter in a relationship.

But they cannot turn consent into an obligation.

For example, if you are dating someone who appreciates explicit communication, it can help to state your preferences clearly when you feel comfortable doing so.

That does not mean you are required to repeat a refusal, explain private feelings, or continue contact after deciding to stop.

Likewise, if your partner becomes intensely focused on a person or situation, their experience may deserve understanding. Their responsibility for respecting boundaries remains unchanged.

The double empathy problem reminds you that different people can misunderstand each other without either person intending harm.

But once a boundary has been communicated, the responsibility to respect it is not a matter of interpreting someone’s brain correctly.

Understanding someone’s reasons for a behavior is different from accepting that behavior.

A person’s neurotype can be relevant to how they learn social expectations or manage difficult feelings. It does not make another person’s consent optional.

You do not have to solve someone else’s feelings

There is a difficult reality on both sides of unwanted pursuit.

If someone is pursuing you, you might feel responsible for their disappointment. You may fear that rejecting them firmly will hurt them, or that ignoring them makes you cruel.

You are allowed to care about another person’s feelings without giving them access to you.

You do not owe someone a romantic opportunity because they have invested time, attention, or hope in you.

If you are the person who wants a relationship, you may feel that stopping means abandoning something important.

But mutual relationships cannot be built through one person’s refusal to stop trying.

Respecting a no is not an admission that your feelings were foolish or meaningless. It is recognizing that the other person has feelings and choices too.

Dating inevitably involves uncertainty. People sometimes want different things. Sometimes a connection ends before it begins. Sometimes you never receive the explanation you hoped for.

The aim is not to become someone who never feels rejected, never misreads interest, or never develops a difficult crush.

It is to become someone who can experience those feelings without crossing another person’s boundaries.

That is what makes the next part of dating possible.

You can show interest clearly. You can ask someone out. You can be enthusiastic, vulnerable, and hopeful.

And because you understand that the other person is free to say no, your interest can remain an invitation rather than a demand.
