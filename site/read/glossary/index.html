Use this glossary to look up unfamiliar words, then follow the chapter references for fuller explanations and examples.

A

ADHD
A way attention, activity, and task management can work that may affect planning, memory, time, and focus. Someone with ADHD may find certain tasks difficult while being deeply focused on others. See chapters 1 and 6.
Alexithymia
Difficulty identifying and describing your emotions. You may know something feels different without knowing whether you are sad, angry, excited, or afraid. See chapter 4.
Anus
The external opening through which stool leaves the body. See chapter 33.
Aromantic
Describes someone who experiences little or no romantic attraction. Aromantic people may still want companionship, commitment, or sexual connection. See chapter 7.
Arousal
Physical or psychological responses associated with sexual excitement. Arousal is not the same as desire or consent. See chapter 33.
Asexual
Describes someone who experiences little or no sexual attraction. Asexual people can have romantic relationships, and their interest in sexual activity varies. See chapter 7.
Attachment anxiety
Being especially concerned about whether someone will remain available, loving, or supportive. It describes a pattern of feelings, not a fixed identity. See chapters 2 and 17.
Attachment avoidance
Being uncomfortable with emotional dependence or closeness. Wanting time alone does not automatically mean someone has attachment avoidance. See chapters 2 and 17.
Attachment patterns
Ways you tend to respond to closeness, distance, and uncertainty in relationships. These patterns can change and do not place everyone into a fixed category. See chapter 2.
Attraction
Finding someone appealing in a romantic or sexual way. Attraction can take different forms and does not necessarily mean two people are compatible. See chapter 11.
AuDHD
An informal term for someone who is both autistic and has ADHD. The two can affect attention, sensory experiences, communication, and everyday life in overlapping ways. See chapter 1.
Autism
A way of experiencing and processing the world that can shape communication, sensory needs, interests, and routines. Autistic people differ widely, and you do not need a label to use this book. See chapter 1.
Autistic burnout
A pattern of prolonged exhaustion, reduced ability to manage everyday tasks, and lower tolerance for demands or sensory input. It can occur when a person’s needs and available energy have been strained over time. See chapter 5.

B

Boundary
A limit on what you will do or accept. A request asks something of someone else, who can say no. See chapter 26.

C

Catfishing
Presenting a false or misleading identity online. Someone may use false photos or personal details to mislead people about who they are. See chapter 47.
Coercive control
An ongoing pattern of behavior that restricts someone’s freedom, choices, or independence in a relationship. It can involve fear, pressure, or isolation. See chapter 36.
Commitment
Making an agreement about your ongoing relationship. What that agreement includes depends on what the people involved freely choose. See chapters 7 and 52.
Companionship
Having someone whose company you enjoy. It can be part of friendship, dating, or a long-term relationship. See chapter 7.
Compatibility
How well two people’s needs, values, goals, and ways of living can work together. You can feel strongly attracted to someone without being compatible. See chapter 13.
Consent
Freely agreeing to a specific activity. Consent is ongoing, can be withdrawn, and cannot be assumed from silence or an earlier yes. See chapter 30.
Consensual non-monogamy
Relationships in which the people involved knowingly and freely agree that certain romantic or sexual connections with others are permitted. The agreements depend on the people involved. See chapter 7.
CPTSD (complex post-traumatic stress disorder)
A term for difficulties that can develop after prolonged or repeated traumatic experiences, including problems with feeling safe, managing emotions, and relationships. A professional can help you explore whether it fits your experiences; the label is not required to understand your needs. See chapter 2.
Crush
A feeling of romantic or sexual interest in someone, often before you know them well. A crush tells you how you feel, not how the other person feels. See chapter 16.

D

Dating
Getting to know someone to find out whether you both want a romantic relationship or another kind of connection. Its purpose is to discover whether there is a mutual fit, not to win someone’s approval. See chapter 9.
Dealbreaker
A condition that makes a relationship unacceptable or unworkable for you. It is different from a preference, which you may be willing to compromise on. See chapter 13.
Desire
Wanting sexual activity. Desire and physical arousal do not always happen together, and neither automatically means consent. See chapters 33 and 55.
Diagnostic disclosure
Telling someone about a diagnosis. You can choose whether, when, and how much to share, including whether to explain your needs without naming a diagnosis. See chapters 5 and 25.
Direct communication
Saying what you intend in words that make your meaning clear. It reduces the need for another person to guess what you mean. See chapter 20.
Disclosure
Choosing to tell someone personal information about yourself, such as your neurotype, diagnosis, or support needs. You can decide what is relevant and what remains private. See chapter 25.
Double empathy problem
Misunderstandings between people with different ways of experiencing the world go both ways. Neither person automatically has the more accurate view; both may need to explain what they mean. See chapter 19.

E

Ejaculation
The release of semen from the penis. It may happen during sexual activity or at other times. See chapter 33.
Emergency contraception
Methods used after intercourse to prevent pregnancy. It is different from medication used to end an existing pregnancy. See chapter 33.
Erection
The physical firmness of the penis. An erection can occur without a person wanting sexual activity. See chapter 33.
Executive functions
Mental processes that help you organize and carry out what you intend to do. They include planning, starting tasks, remembering steps, and adjusting when plans change. See chapter 6.

F

Fawning
A pattern of trying to stay safe or avoid conflict by pleasing others, agreeing, or putting their needs ahead of your own. It can make recognizing and expressing your boundaries difficult. See chapter 2.
Fixation
Becoming so focused on a person that your thoughts or actions keep returning to them, even when you want to focus elsewhere. Strong feelings do not give you a right to someone’s attention or contact. See chapters 16 and 32.
Flirting
Communicating possible romantic or sexual interest through words, humor, or behavior. Because flirting can be misunderstood, you can ask plainly rather than treating a signal as consent. See chapter 42.

G

Games
Deliberately creating confusion, insecurity, jealousy, or emotional pressure to influence another person’s feelings or choices. This is different from playful flirting that both people enjoy. See chapter 42.
Gaslighting
A pattern of undermining someone’s trust in their own memory, perceptions, or judgment. An ordinary disagreement about what happened is not automatically gaslighting. See chapter 36.
Ghosting
Ending contact without explaining the withdrawal. It can be painful to experience, although ending contact without explanation may be necessary for safety. See chapter 51.
Green flag
An observable behavior that suggests someone may be capable of building a respectful, healthy relationship. Examples include respecting a no and taking responsibility for mistakes. See chapter 37.

H

Harassment
Unwanted behavior that pressures, threatens, or repeatedly targets someone. It may include continued contact after the person has made it clear they do not want contact. See chapters 31 and 32.
Hyperfocus
Becoming deeply absorbed in something so that other things receive less attention. It may involve a hobby, task, or person, but intense attention does not automatically mean a good relationship fit. See chapters 16 and 22.

I

Indirect communication
Conveying an idea without stating it completely. The other person may need to use context or hints to understand what you mean. See chapter 20.
Info-dumping
Sharing a large amount of detailed information about a subject, often because you find it interesting or important. Sharing enthusiasm is fine; a conversation also makes room for the other person’s interests and responses. See chapter 22.
Interoception
The ability to detect and interpret signals from inside your body. These signals may help you notice hunger, tiredness, tension, or feelings. See chapter 4.
Intimacy
A sense of closeness or connection with someone. It can be emotional, physical, romantic, or sexual, and does not always involve sex. See chapter 55.

L

Limerence
An intense form of romantic infatuation involving repeated thoughts about someone and a strong desire for their attention or affection. It can feel powerful even when you barely know the person. See chapter 16.
Love bombing
Intense attention or affection that becomes concerning when it is tied to pressure, control, or demands. Affection alone is not evidence of manipulation; the surrounding behavior matters. See chapter 36.
Lubricant
A product used to reduce friction during sexual contact. See chapter 33.

M

Manipulation
Influencing someone through deception, emotional pressure, or other unfair means rather than allowing them to make an informed, free choice. See chapter 36.
Masking
Hiding or suppressing your natural traits to fit in. See chapter 5.
Match
On many dating apps, a match means both people have indicated some interest. It does not guarantee a conversation, date, or relationship. See chapter 46.
Meltdown
An intense, involuntary reaction that can happen during overload. It may involve crying, difficulty speaking, or losing control of ordinary responses, and is different from shutdown. See chapters 50 and 54.
Menstrual cycle
A recurring process involving hormones, egg development, and changes to the uterine lining. See chapter 33.
Monogamy
A relationship agreement in which people choose romantic or sexual exclusivity with one partner. The people involved still need to discuss what exclusivity means to them. See chapter 7.

N

National Sexual Assault Hotline
A US support service operated by RAINN for people affected by sexual assault. It offers information, support, and help exploring options. See chapters 35 and 60.
Neurodivergent
A broad term for people whose neurological development or functioning differs from what is considered typical. It can include autistic people and people with ADHD, among others. You do not need to use this label to describe your experiences. See chapter 1.
Neurotypical
A term for someone whose neurological development or functioning is generally considered typical. It does not mean someone has no struggles, needs, or differences. See chapter 1.

O

Orgasm
An experience of sexual climax. Not everyone experiences it in the same way, and it is not required for sexual intimacy to be meaningful. See chapter 33.
Overload
More coming in (noise, light, people, questions, feelings) than you can handle at once. See chapters 1 and 3.
Ovulation
When an ovary releases an egg. See chapter 33.

P

Partner responsiveness
The experience of feeling understood, valued, and cared for by a partner. It involves how someone responds to your needs and feelings, not simply how often they contact you. See chapter 18.
Personal space
The physical distance someone wants between their body and another person’s body. Comfortable distances vary, and someone being nearby does not automatically mean they welcome touch. See chapter 31.
Preference
Something you would like in a partner or relationship but might be willing to compromise on. It is different from a dealbreaker, which makes a relationship unworkable for you. See chapter 13.

R

Rape
Sexual penetration without consent. Legal definitions and how particular acts are classified vary across US states. See chapter 35.
Reciprocity
Both people participating in sustaining a connection. They do not have to contribute in identical ways, but neither person should be expected to carry the relationship alone. See chapter 18.
Red flag
An observable behavior that raises concern about disrespect, control, coercion, or harm. A red flag is a reason to pay attention to someone’s actions rather than dismiss your concerns. See chapter 37.
Rejection sensitivity
A tendency to anxiously expect rejection, perceive it readily in unclear situations, and react strongly when it seems to happen. Feeling rejected does not always mean someone intended to reject you. See chapter 44.
Repair
Taking steps to address a misunderstanding or hurt after something has gone wrong. It may involve listening, apologizing, clarifying what happened, and changing your behavior. See chapter 24.
Request
Asking something of someone else, who can say no. A boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept. See chapter 26.
Response time
How long someone takes to answer a message or other communication. Different habits, responsibilities, and needs can affect response times, so the delay alone does not tell you someone’s feelings. See chapter 23.
Responsive desire
Sexual desire that may develop in an already wanted, comfortable intimate context rather than appearing beforehand. It does not mean someone should begin unwanted sexual activity to see whether desire develops. See chapter 55.
Romance
Wanting a kind of closeness you experience as romantic. What feels romantic can differ from person to person. See chapter 7.
Romance scam
A form of fraud in which someone uses the appearance of romantic interest or a relationship to obtain money, valuable information, or other benefits dishonestly. See chapter 47.
Romantic attraction
Experiencing another person as someone you are romantically drawn to. It does not always come with sexual attraction. See chapter 7.
Romantic self-sabotage
Behaviors that undermine a relationship a person may otherwise want. These can include pulling away from someone kind or testing their feelings instead of discussing concerns openly. See chapter 17.

S

Semen
The fluid released during ejaculation that may contain sperm. See chapter 33.
Sensory profile
The pattern of sounds, lights, smells, textures, touch, and other sensations you find comfortable or difficult. Knowing your sensory profile can help you choose dates and settings that work for you. See chapter 3.
Sexting
Exchanging sexual messages or images through a phone or online service. Both people need to agree freely to the exchange, and receiving one message does not mean agreeing to more. See chapter 34.
Sextortion
Threatening to expose someone’s intimate images or other sexual material unless they meet a demand. It is a form of exploitation, not a normal relationship disagreement. See chapter 47.
Sexual assault
A broad everyday term for nonconsensual sexual activity. It can include unwanted sexual touching or penetration, although legal definitions vary. See chapter 35.
Sexual assault forensic examination (SAFE)
A medical examination that can include injury assessment and collection of evidence after sexual assault. Medical care and evidence collection are options even when someone is uncertain about reporting to police. See chapter 35.
Sexual attraction
Experiencing another person as someone you are sexually drawn to. It is different from deciding to have sex or wanting a relationship. See chapter 7.
Sexual coercion
Pressure, threats, or controlling behavior that interferes with someone’s ability to choose sexual activity freely. Agreement obtained through coercion is not freely given consent. See chapter 35.
Sexual connection
Wanting some form of sexual intimacy. People can differ in how important this is to them and what kinds of activity they want. See chapter 7.
Sexual consent
Freely agreeing to a specific sexual activity. It must remain voluntary and can be withdrawn at any time, including during the activity. See chapter 34.
Sexually transmitted infection (STI)
An infection that can pass between people through certain types of sexual contact. Testing, protection, and sexual health care help people make informed choices. See chapter 33.
Shared daily life
Deciding how closely your routines and practical lives will connect. This can include time together, household responsibilities, finances, or living arrangements. See chapters 7 and 53.
Shutdown
When your brain and body pull inward and slow down or stop, often after overload; speech may become very hard. See chapters 1 and 54.
Small talk
Brief conversation about everyday subjects, often used to begin an interaction or get comfortable talking. It does not have to be deeply personal to serve a purpose. See chapter 21.
Special interest
An interest someone pursues with unusual depth, enjoyment, or personal importance. It can be a source of pleasure and connection without needing to become a shared interest. See chapter 22.
Spontaneous desire
Becoming interested in sex before sexual activity or closeness begins. It is one way desire can arise, not a requirement for healthy sexuality. See chapter 55.
Sperm
Reproductive cells that can fertilize an egg. See chapter 33.
Stalking
A pattern of unwanted attention, following, or contact that causes fear or concern for safety. US laws define stalking differently by state, and the behavior can happen online or in person. See chapter 32.
StopNCII.org
A resource for adults seeking to prevent the spread of certain intimate images shared without consent on participating online services. See chapter 47.
Subtext
Meaning underneath the literal words someone says. Understanding it may depend on context, tone, or shared expectations, and asking for clarification is reasonable. See chapter 20.

T

Take It Down
A service from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children that helps address the spread of intimate images taken when someone was under 18. Its website is takeitdown.ncmec.org. See chapter 47.
Time blindness
Difficulty sensing how much time has passed or estimating how long something will take. It describes an experience, not a lack of caring or effort. See chapter 6.
Trauma
Experiences that are deeply harmful or threatening, or the lasting effects those experiences can leave. Trauma can affect what feels safe or familiar in relationships, but people respond differently. See chapters 2 and 57.
Trigger
Something that brings up a strong response connected to a past harmful experience. A trigger may be a sound, situation, action, or reminder that another person does not recognize as significant. See chapter 57.

U

Unmasking
Allowing more of your usual behavior or reducing efforts to hide your traits. You can decide when and with whom it feels appropriate to unmask. See chapter 5.

V

Vagina
The internal muscular canal leading from the vaginal opening to the cervix. It is different from the vulva, which is external. See chapter 33.
Vulva
The external genital area, including the labia and clitoris. It is different from the vagina, which is internal. See chapter 33.
