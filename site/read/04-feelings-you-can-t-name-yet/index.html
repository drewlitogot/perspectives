The date went well. At least, you think it did.

You talked for two hours. There were no awkward arguments. The other person seemed interested, laughed at your jokes, and asked whether you’d like to meet again.

You said yes.

Now you’re home, sitting on the couch, and something feels wrong.

Maybe you’re exhausted. Maybe you’re anxious. Maybe you didn’t enjoy yourself as much as you thought. You replay parts of the conversation, looking for an explanation.

Then you remember something.

Halfway through dinner, your date made a joke about your appearance. You laughed because everyone was laughing. It didn’t bother you at the time, or at least you didn’t recognize that it bothered you.

Now it does.

Or perhaps the opposite happens. You finish a date feeling almost nothing. You tell a friend it was fine, but you’re not particularly excited. Two days later, you catch yourself smiling about a conversation you had. You realize you’d like to see that person again.

Why couldn’t you tell sooner?

Sometimes the difficult part of dating isn’t understanding another person’s feelings. It’s understanding your own.

You can experience an emotion without immediately recognizing what it is. You might recognize it but struggle to describe it. You might know exactly what you’re feeling but need time to decide what it means.

Those are different experiences. Understanding the difference can change how you approach dating, especially when someone expects an answer before you have one.

Feeling something and knowing what it is

Imagine someone asks, “How do you feel about me?”

There are several things your mind might need to do before you can answer.

First, there is the experience itself. Something is happening inside you, whether that’s excitement, affection, anxiety, irritation, or something harder to identify.

Then comes recognizing the experience. You notice that something has changed and begin to distinguish one feeling from another.

Next comes finding words for it.

Finally, you decide what, if anything, to communicate or do.

These steps don’t always happen together.

You might feel uncomfortable without knowing why. You might know you’re uncomfortable but have no idea whether you’re anxious, angry, or simply tired. You might know you’re angry but decide that discussing it while you’re upset wouldn’t be helpful.

Or you might know that you don’t want another date, even though you can’t explain your feelings neatly.

You don’t need to identify every emotion before you can make a decision.

Consider Jordan, 34, who has been on three dates with Marcus. Jordan enjoys the conversations but feels increasingly reluctant to arrange another meeting.

A friend asks why.

“I don’t know,” Jordan says. “He’s nice. Nothing bad happened.”

The friend suggests giving Marcus another chance because there’s no obvious reason to stop.

But Jordan doesn’t need to prove that Marcus did something wrong. Not wanting to continue is enough.

The feeling might become clearer later. Perhaps Jordan realizes the conversations felt one-sided. Perhaps the attraction simply isn’t there. Perhaps Jordan never finds a satisfying explanation.

None of those possibilities creates an obligation to keep dating.

At the same time, difficulty naming a feeling doesn’t always mean the answer is no. Sometimes the most accurate answer really is, “I’m not sure yet.”

That answer deserves a place in dating, too.

When emotions are difficult to name

There’s a word for a particular kind of difficulty identifying and describing emotions: alexithymia.

Alexithymia isn’t the absence of emotions. It describes difficulty recognizing what you’re feeling or putting those feelings into words. Researchers generally study it as a trait that varies between individuals, rather than something a person either completely has or doesn’t have (Bird & Cook, 2013; Kinnaird et al., 2019).

You might recognize some of these experiences:

  • Someone asks whether you’re upset, and you genuinely don’t know.
  • You describe yourself as “fine” because you can’t find a more accurate answer.
  • You notice that you’re crying, laughing, or withdrawing before you understand the emotion behind it.
  • You can explain what happened during an argument but struggle to explain how it affected you.
  • You know you care about someone, but questions about your emotional experience leave you searching for words.

These experiences aren’t exclusive to alexithymia. Having one or several doesn’t establish that you have the trait.

They also don’t mean you’re dishonest, uncaring, or incapable of love.

There is an important difference between not having a feeling and not being able to identify it.

Suppose your partner says, “You never tell me how you feel about us.”

You might genuinely care about the relationship. You enjoy being together, look forward to seeing them, and make room for them in your life.

But when someone asks you to explain your feelings, you struggle.

From your perspective, your affection may seem obvious through your choices. From your partner’s perspective, the missing words may leave them uncertain.

Both experiences matter.

The difficulty isn’t automatically a lack of love or a lack of effort. But neither does having trouble with emotions make your partner’s need for clarity disappear.

Understanding the difference gives both people something useful to discuss.

Is this connected to autism, ADHD, or trauma?

Alexithymia appears more frequently in some groups, but it doesn’t belong to any one diagnosis.

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 15 studies of alexithymia in autistic people. Across the subset of studies that reported questionnaire cut-off scores, approximately half of autistic participants scored above the threshold, compared with about 5% of nonautistic comparison participants (Kinnaird et al., 2019).

That finding is striking, but it needs context.

The studies were generally small, some included only men, and the questionnaire relied on people reporting their own difficulties identifying feelings. These were research samples, not a representative survey of every autistic person.

Most importantly, the findings mean that many autistic people in these studies did not score above the threshold.

Alexithymia and autism are not interchangeable.

Researchers have proposed that alexithymia may explain some emotion-processing difficulties sometimes attributed directly to autism (Bird & Cook, 2013). But that explanation isn’t complete. One laboratory study still found differences in recognizing anger from moving faces after accounting for alexithymia (Keating et al., 2022).

Studies of adults with ADHD have also found elevated alexithymia scores in some participants. One German clinical study of 73 adults found that about 22% scored above a high-alexithymia threshold. A Turkish study of 101 adults with ADHD reported 41.5% (Edel et al., 2010; Kiraz et al., 2021).

Those figures are not reliable estimates for everyone with ADHD. The studies differed in their samples and methods, and neither establishes that ADHD causes alexithymia.

Childhood experiences may matter, too. A meta-analysis covering 99 samples and more than 36,000 participants found a modest association between childhood maltreatment and alexithymia. Emotional neglect and emotional abuse were among the experiences associated with greater difficulty identifying and describing feelings (Ditzer et al., 2023). Another meta-analysis found a similar general association (Kick et al., 2024).

An association is not a diagnosis or a life story.

Difficulty recognizing feelings does not prove that you experienced childhood trauma. Having experienced trauma does not mean you’ll necessarily have difficulty identifying emotions.

Chapter 2 explored how early experiences can affect relationships. Here, the useful question is simpler: Whatever the reason, what happens when I need to understand a feeling and don’t have the words?

You can work with that question without first discovering why your mind works this way.

Your body may notice something before you can name it

Emotions aren’t experienced only as thoughts.

You might notice a racing heart, tense shoulders, a warm face, a heavy feeling in your chest, or an uncomfortable stomach.

The ability to detect and interpret signals from inside your body is called interoception.

It includes sensations such as hunger, heartbeat, and physical discomfort. These signals can sometimes provide clues about emotions, although they don’t identify emotions by themselves (Klein et al., 2025; Van Bael et al., 2024).

For some people, those clues are noticeable.

For others, they’re faint, confusing, or difficult to separate.

Imagine you’re sitting across from someone you find attractive.

Your heart is beating quickly. You’re speaking faster than usual. Your hands feel restless.

Are you excited?

Nervous?

Uncomfortable?

All three?

Your heartbeat can’t answer that question.

You might feel physically activated because you’re interested in someone. You might feel that way because you’re worried about making a mistake. You might be tired, hungry, or reacting to something unrelated to the person sitting across from you.

A body sensation is a clue, not a verdict.

This matters because dating advice sometimes treats physical feelings as if they come with exact translations.

“Trust your gut.”

“You’ll just know.”

“If there’s chemistry, you’ll feel it.”

Those statements might make sense to someone who easily recognizes their emotional responses. For someone who doesn’t, they can be frustrating.

What if your stomach feels uncomfortable around nearly every new person? What if attraction and anxiety feel similar? What if you don’t notice much of anything until later?

You don’t have to treat your body as a machine that delivers perfect answers.

What research does and doesn’t say

Research on interoception in autistic people is more mixed than popular explanations sometimes suggest.

A 2025 systematic review examined 31 studies. Its meta-analysis of five comparable adult studies found no statistically significant difference between autistic and nonautistic adults on tasks measuring accuracy in detecting heartbeats. Other studies examining self-reported body awareness produced inconsistent results (Klein et al., 2025).

A separate meta-analysis found that different aspects of reported body awareness relate differently to alexithymia. Noticing sensations, feeling confused by them, and trusting them are not the same thing (Van Bael et al., 2024).

This doesn’t mean everyone detects internal signals equally well. It means the evidence doesn’t support a simple rule that autistic people are less aware of their bodies.

Your own experience is more useful here than an assumption based on a label.

And your body isn’t your only source of information.

You can also notice your thoughts, actions, preferences, and reactions to what happened.

For example:

You might not notice tension during a date, but you realize you keep hoping the other person will leave.

You might feel physically calm but notice that you’re interested in nearly everything someone says.

You might enjoy a conversation yet feel relieved when it ends.

You might not know whether you feel attracted to someone, but you know you would enjoy spending another afternoon together.

These observations won’t necessarily produce an immediate emotional answer. They can, however, help you make sense of your experience.

A small experiment

The next time you notice something emotionally important, try starting with observation rather than interpretation.

Instead of asking, “What emotion am I feeling?” try:

“What happened just before I noticed something change?”

“What do I notice in my body, if anything?”

“What am I thinking about?”

“What do I want to do right now?”

You can answer only the questions that help.

Perhaps the answers are:

They interrupted me three times. My shoulders feel tight. I’m thinking about going home. I want some quiet.

You still might not know whether the feeling is irritation, anxiety, disappointment, or fatigue.

That’s fine.

You’ve identified something useful: the interaction affected you, and you want to leave.

You can act on that information without giving the emotion a perfect name.

When you understand a feeling afterward

Sometimes the moment when something happens and the moment when you understand your reaction are far apart.

Consider Priya, 42, who goes on a date with Elena.

During dinner, Elena asks whether Priya wants children. Priya answers honestly. Elena then makes a dismissive comment about people who choose not to become parents.

Priya smiles, changes the subject, and finishes dinner.

On the drive home, she begins replaying the conversation. She’s irritated but can’t quite explain why.

The next morning, she realizes that Elena’s comment made her feel judged. She hadn’t recognized that reaction while they were sitting together.

Priya isn’t required to disregard the feeling just because it became clear the next day.

She also doesn’t have to decide that Elena deliberately meant to hurt her. She can distinguish what happened from what she knows about Elena’s intentions.

She can decide not to meet again. Or, if she’s otherwise interested, she can ask Elena what she meant.

The same uncertainty can happen with positive feelings.

Andre, 29, meets Sam for coffee. Their conversation is pleasant, but Andre doesn’t feel especially excited afterward.

Later that evening, he remembers how comfortably they talked about their hobbies. The following day, he realizes he’s curious about Sam and would enjoy another date.

Neither response proves that someone has a particular emotional-processing trait.

They’re examples of how a person might recognize the significance of an experience after it happens.

Does the research explain delayed feelings?

Not very well.

One small study asked autistic and nonautistic adults to report their emotions repeatedly during everyday life. The autistic participants more frequently reported having an emotion they couldn’t name (Costache et al., 2024).

That offers evidence that uncertainty about current emotions can occur in everyday situations. It doesn’t establish that the participants identified those emotions hours or days afterward.

Another study involving more than 1,200 autistic and nonautistic adults found broadly comparable accuracy but slower responses among autistic participants on tasks involving recognition of other people’s facial and vocal emotions (Jertberg et al., 2026).

That is a different process.

Taking longer to recognize an emotion on someone’s face in a computer task isn’t evidence that you recognize your own feelings a day after a date.

Research included here doesn’t establish how common that longer delay is, why it occurs, or whether it is more frequent in a particular neurotype.

So there’s no need to turn a recognizable experience into a scientific rule.

The practical point remains useful: if you sometimes understand your reactions later, you can make room for that possibility when dating.

You don’t have to invent an explanation or diagnose yourself.

And you don’t need to decide that a delayed feeling is automatically more accurate than your original reaction.

Sometimes reflection reveals something you missed. Sometimes repeated thinking makes an event seem more significant than it was. A new interpretation is worth considering, but it isn’t automatically the final answer.

Look at what happened, what you know, and what you want to do now.

If you only notice discomfort later

You may get home from a date, or wake up the next morning, and only then realize that something bothered you. That delay is not the same as inventing a problem afterwards, and it doesn’t make the reaction less real.

It does mean “I don’t know” is an honest answer in the moment, and a better one than a guess you’ll have to take back. “I’m not sure yet. Can I tell you tomorrow?” is a complete sentence.

Give yourself a way to catch the information: a note on your phone on the way home, or a check the next day. You’re not trying to feel faster. You’re making sure the feeling still counts when it arrives.

You don’t owe anyone an instant emotional answer

Dating creates many situations where someone wants to know what you’re feeling.

“Are you having a good time?”

“Do you like me?”

“Did I upset you?”

“Do you want to keep seeing each other?”

Sometimes those questions are easy.

Sometimes your most accurate answer is simply that you don’t know.

You might worry that saying so will disappoint the other person. You might feel tempted to guess, especially if they seem excited, anxious, or impatient.

But giving an answer just to end an uncomfortable conversation can create a bigger problem.

Suppose someone asks whether you want a committed relationship.

You’re uncertain, but you say yes because you like them and don’t want to lose the connection.

A month later, you realize you aren’t ready.

Now the other person has made decisions based on an answer you weren’t confident about.

You can’t guarantee you’ll never change your mind. Nobody can. But you can avoid presenting uncertainty as certainty.

A more accurate response might be:

“I’m interested in getting to know you, but I’m not sure yet whether I want a serious relationship.”

Or:

“I enjoy spending time with you. I need more time before I can answer that.”

Or simply:

“I don’t know yet.”

Those answers aren’t promises. They tell the other person what is known and what remains undecided.

And they allow the other person to decide whether that uncertainty works for them.

You can know what you want before you know why

There’s another important distinction.

You might not know what emotion you’re feeling, but you can still know what action you want to take.

Maybe you want to leave.

Maybe you want another date.

Maybe you don’t want to be touched.

Maybe you want to continue the conversation tomorrow.

You do not need to produce a complete explanation before expressing those preferences.

“I’d like to go home now” is enough.

“I don’t want to kiss” is enough.

“I’d like to see you again” is enough.

An explanation may help someone understand you. It isn’t a requirement for your preferences to matter.

This becomes especially important when you feel uncomfortable.

If someone asks you to do something and you aren’t sure whether you want to, you can stop and take time to decide.

Uncertainty doesn’t create an obligation to agree.

What to do when a date is hard to understand

After a date, it’s tempting to answer one enormous question:

Did I like them?

That question can be difficult even when you recognize your emotions easily.

Instead, try separating what happened from how you interpret it.

You can make a few short notes, privately, without turning the evening into an investigation.

Use four prompts:

  1. What happened? Write two or three things you remember. “We talked about work. They asked thoughtful questions. They interrupted me once.”
  2. What did I notice? Record anything about your experience. “I laughed a lot. I felt tired near the end. I was comfortable sitting beside them.”
  3. What am I unsure about? Name the uncertainty instead of forcing a conclusion. “I can’t tell whether I felt attracted or just enjoyed the conversation.”
  4. What do I want next? Choose only what you know. “I’d be interested in another short date,” or “I’d rather not continue.”

You can write a sentence for each question, or just a few words.

Don’t search for a hidden meaning in every detail.

The point is to gather information, not to produce a verdict.

If you’re still uncertain later, it’s reasonable to leave the answer open when no immediate decision is required.

A small experimental study involving 82 participants found that using exhaustive emotion labels wasn’t automatically better than simpler labels for emotional clarity or planning a response (Vine et al., 2019). That study wasn’t about dating, and it doesn’t establish this note-taking exercise as a treatment for alexithymia.

The exercise is simply a way to reduce the demand for a perfect emotional explanation.

Look for patterns, not one decisive moment

A single date may not tell you everything.

Suppose you meet someone three times.

The first time, you feel uncertain.

The second time, you feel comfortable but tired.

The third time, you notice that you’re looking forward to seeing them again.

You might gradually recognize an interest you couldn’t identify initially.

Alternatively, you might notice that you repeatedly dread the date, feel relieved when it ends, and struggle to think of a reason to continue.

Repeated observations can help you understand your preferences.

But there is a limit to this approach.

You do not need to keep dating someone merely to gather enough evidence.

If you already know you don’t want to continue, that’s a decision you can make now.

And if someone treats you disrespectfully or ignores a boundary, you don’t need multiple examples to justify leaving.

Your emotional uncertainty doesn’t cancel your ability to choose.

When your partner needs an answer sooner than you can give one

Trouble naming feelings doesn’t affect only the person experiencing it.

It can also affect a partner who’s trying to understand what’s happening.

One longitudinal study found that higher self-reported alexithymia was associated with poorer subsequent reports of relationship satisfaction, commitment, affectionate communication, and relationship-maintenance behavior (Hesse & Gibbons, 2019).

That doesn’t prove alexithymia causes unhappy relationships.

Another study of 410 Italian adults in relationships found that the relationship between alexithymia and relationship adjustment became less straightforward after accounting for attachment-related factors (Romeo et al., 2024).

Neither study establishes what will happen in a particular relationship, or what happens specifically in couples where one or both partners are autistic or have ADHD.

Still, the difficulty is easy to imagine.

Taylor asks their partner, Morgan, “Are you upset with me?”

Morgan says, “I don’t know.”

Taylor feels worried. They ask again.

Morgan still doesn’t know.

Taylor interprets the lack of an answer as avoidance. Morgan feels pressured to provide an answer they don’t have.

Soon, both are frustrated.

Neither person’s experience has to be dismissed.

Morgan may genuinely be uncertain.

Taylor may genuinely need reassurance or clarity about what’s happening between them.

The solution isn’t for Taylor to guess Morgan’s feelings or for Morgan to invent an answer.

They can communicate what they know.

Morgan might say:

“I’m having trouble identifying what I’m feeling. I know I need some quiet time. Can we talk after dinner?”

Taylor might respond:

“Okay. I can give you some time. I do want us to talk about what happened tonight.”

Now the uncertainty has a practical shape.

There’s space to reflect, and there’s an expectation that the conversation will continue.

That matters because “I don’t know” should be allowed to mean “I don’t know.”

But it shouldn’t become a permanent substitute for addressing something important.

If you’re the person waiting for an answer

Your partner may genuinely need time to understand a feeling.

That doesn’t mean you have to suppress your own questions or wait indefinitely.

You can ask about something smaller and more immediate.

“Do you want some space right now?”

“Would tomorrow be a better time to talk?”

“Can you tell me whether you want to continue this relationship, even if you’re unsure exactly how you feel?”

Your partner might be able to answer one question without answering every question.

If they can’t, you can decide what uncertainty you’re willing to live with.

You don’t need to become their emotional interpreter, and you shouldn’t assume that hesitation means rejection or agreement.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from earlier in the book. Your partner’s experience may not look the way yours does. Describing what you each know is more useful than assuming either perspective tells the complete story.

The same applies if both partners need additional time.

Two autistic partners, for example, may both have difficulty putting feelings into words, or one may answer quickly while the other needs longer. The diagnosis alone doesn’t tell you which experience either person will have.

The useful information comes from what each person actually says and does.

If you're dating someone who can't answer how they feel yet

“I don’t know” is often the literal truth rather than avoidance. Pressing for an answer tends to produce a guess, and a guess is worse information than a wait.

Ask in a way that allows the delay: “You don’t have to answer now. Would it help to tell me tomorrow?” Then let the subject rest, and keep the offer open rather than circling back to it every hour.

If you need something for yourself in the meantime, ask for the smallest version: whether they want to continue seeing you, rather than how they feel about everything. A narrow question is far easier to answer honestly.

When you’re not sure whether you want physical intimacy

There is one situation where uncertainty requires particular care: physical intimacy.

Suppose you’re on a date and the other person asks whether you’d like to kiss.

You like them. You’re curious. But you’re not sure you want to kiss them right now.

You don’t have to decide yes because you might eventually want to.

You can say, “I’m not ready.”

Or, “I’m not sure. Let’s wait.”

You also don’t need to determine whether your hesitation comes from anxiety, attraction, unfamiliarity, or something else before choosing to pause.

The same principle applies to your date.

If they seem uncertain, stop and check rather than assuming they want to continue.

RAINN’s US consent guidance emphasizes that consent must be freely given, clear, and ongoing. Silence, freezing, confusion, or hesitation should not be interpreted as agreement (RAINN, 2026).

Consent is not a puzzle where the other person has to guess the correct answer.

If either person is unsure about an intimate activity, the appropriate choice is to pause or wait until there is clear, voluntary agreement.

Someone else’s frustration does not create an obligation to continue.

And if you notice that you’re pressuring a person to answer, or treating their uncertainty as a yes, stop. Respect their decision and their space.

Legal definitions of consent vary across US states, but you don’t need to understand every legal distinction to follow the practical rule: don’t proceed without clear, freely given consent.

What if you realize afterward that you were uncomfortable?

You might agree to something and only later recognize that you didn’t enjoy it.

That later understanding is still relevant.

It may affect what you want to do next, what you communicate, and whether you want to continue seeing the person.

You are allowed to change your mind about future intimacy, even if you previously agreed to something similar.

At the same time, a delayed feeling alone cannot establish what another person knew or intended during an earlier interaction.

You don’t have to resolve that question to take your present discomfort seriously.

If someone pressured you, ignored your refusal, or continued when you couldn’t freely agree, that behavior matters regardless of how quickly you recognized your feelings.

Support and safety resources are addressed in the later chapters devoted to those topics.

For now, remember that you don’t need a perfectly named emotion to stop an activity, set a limit, or ask for help.

You can learn to describe your experience without explaining everything

Some people can easily say, “I’m disappointed because I expected you to call.”

Others know only that something feels wrong.

If you struggle with emotional language, try starting with what you can describe.

Instead of “I’m angry,” you might say:

“I didn’t like what happened.”

Instead of “I’m anxious about our relationship,” you might say:

“I keep thinking about our conversation, and I’m not ready to make a decision.”

Instead of “I’m feeling emotionally disconnected,” you might say:

“I’ve noticed that I don’t feel like talking much today. I’m not sure why.”

These statements may be incomplete, but they can still be accurate.

And they’re more useful than claiming certainty you don’t have.

There’s also a difference between recognizing a feeling and assigning responsibility for it.

Suppose your date makes an innocent comment and you feel embarrassed.

You can acknowledge the embarrassment without deciding the other person deliberately humiliated you.

Or suppose someone repeatedly makes jokes about your body and dismisses your discomfort.

You don’t need to find an alternative explanation that makes their behavior acceptable.

You can describe what happened and decide what you will accept.

Your feelings provide information about your experience. They don’t automatically reveal another person’s intentions.

This distinction can prevent misunderstandings without asking you to ignore your reactions.

When the words still won’t come

You don’t have to force a conversation while you’re struggling to understand yourself.

You can explain the difficulty in ordinary language.

“I know this matters. I can’t explain what I’m feeling yet.”

“I need time to sort out my thoughts.”

“I’m not ignoring the question. I just don’t have an answer right now.”

The important part is to communicate whatever you can honestly communicate, especially when another person is directly affected.

If you need time, say so. If you know a decision, state it. If you change your mind later, communicate that change.

Don’t use uncertainty to avoid an answer you already know.

And don’t let someone convince you that needing time makes your feelings less legitimate.

If difficulty identifying emotions regularly interferes with your relationships, decisions, or sense of safety, you may find it helpful to speak with a qualified mental health professional. That can be an option without requiring you to adopt a diagnostic label.

You can also use practical observations on your own, whether or not you seek professional support.

What matters more than the perfect word

Imagine going on a date and realizing afterward that you can’t explain how you feel.

You know the conversation was interesting, but you don’t know whether you’re attracted to the person.

You know they seemed kind, but you aren’t certain whether you want to see them again.

You notice yourself thinking about something they said, but you can’t decide whether that means curiosity, concern, or affection.

It’s possible to leave those questions unanswered for a while.

You can pay attention to what becomes clearer, rather than trying to force an immediate conclusion.

You can also make practical decisions while some feelings remain uncertain.

Maybe you agree to another date because you’re genuinely curious.

Maybe you decline because you don’t want to continue.

Maybe you tell someone you’re interested but need more time.

The important thing isn’t whether you can produce a sophisticated description of your emotional life.

It’s whether you’re making room for what you actually experience, communicating as honestly as you can, and respecting the choices of the person you’re dating.

You don’t need to be perfectly understood to deserve respect.

You don’t need to understand yourself perfectly to treat someone else with respect.

And you don’t need to know the name of every feeling before deciding what to do with it.

Sometimes “I don’t know yet” is the most accurate answer available.

That is an answer you can build from.
