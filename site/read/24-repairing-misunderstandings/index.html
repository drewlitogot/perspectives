Sam and Ellis have been dating for a few weeks. They’re enjoying dinner when Sam says, “I’m getting tired. I think I’ll head home.”

Ellis hears: You’re boring me. I don’t want to be here.

Sam means: The restaurant is loud, I’m running out of energy, and I want to leave before I get exhausted.

Ellis replies, “Fine,” and starts looking at the check.

Now Sam thinks Ellis is angry. Ellis thinks Sam has lost interest. Neither understands what happened, and both are wondering whether the date went badly.

A few minutes ago, they were having a perfectly good evening.

This is a misunderstanding: two people assign different meanings to the same exchange. The words themselves may be clear, but the assumptions surrounding them are different.

Misunderstandings are part of getting to know someone. You cannot expect another person to know everything happening inside your head, and you cannot reliably guess everything happening inside theirs.

What matters is what you do when those differences become visible.

Repairing a misunderstanding means finding out what went wrong in the communication, correcting what you can, and taking responsibility if your words or actions caused harm.

Sometimes that takes thirty seconds. Sometimes it takes an apology and a change in behavior. Sometimes you discover that the other person understood you perfectly but disagrees with you.

The goal is not to make every conversation comfortable. It is to make understanding possible.

The difference between what you meant and what they heard

Every conversation has at least two sides: what one person intends to communicate and what the other person understands.

There is also a third piece: how the interaction affects each person.

Consider these statements:

  • “I was only joking.”
  • “I thought you wanted advice.”
  • “I didn’t know you were waiting for me.”
  • “I thought you were asking why, not telling me to stop.”

Each might be a completely truthful explanation. None automatically tells you how the other person experienced the interaction.

Suppose you make a sarcastic comment about someone’s cooking. You intend to be playful. They hear criticism.

Your intention matters because it explains what you were trying to communicate. Their experience matters because it explains what happened when they received it.

You can acknowledge both without pretending you intended something you did not.

For example:

“I meant that as a joke. I can see it sounded like criticism. I’m sorry I made fun of something you worked hard on.”

That explanation provides context and takes responsibility.

Compare it with:

“It was obviously a joke. You’re taking it too seriously.”

The second response offers no room for the other person’s interpretation. It treats your intention as the only thing that counts.

But the opposite extreme is unhelpful, too. If someone misunderstands an ordinary, respectful statement, you do not necessarily need to confess wrongdoing.

You can say:

“I wasn’t upset with you. I was trying to explain that I needed some time alone.”

That is clarification, not an apology.

A misunderstanding does not automatically mean someone behaved badly. And a good intention does not automatically mean no harm occurred.

Learning to tell those situations apart makes repairing them much easier.

Notice when the conversation changes

Sometimes a misunderstanding announces itself.

Someone says, “That’s not what I meant.”

Sometimes it is less obvious.

Your date was talking enthusiastically and suddenly becomes quiet. They answer a question that seems unrelated to yours. They sound irritated by something you thought was neutral. Or they respond to an invitation as though you had criticized them.

These moments are worth noticing, but not diagnosing.

A short reply does not prove someone is angry. Silence does not prove rejection. A serious expression does not mean you have done something wrong.

You have noticed a change. You have not yet learned what caused it.

Research on conversations involving autistic adults offers an important reminder: people can work together and enjoy one another’s company while occasionally operating with different assumptions about what has been understood. Not every small communication glitch destroys the connection (Heasman & Gillespie, 2019).

Instead of deciding immediately what the other person’s behavior means, try checking.

“Did something I said come across differently than I intended?”

“I noticed the conversation changed after that. Did I miss something?”

“I think I may have misunderstood your question.”

Sometimes the answer is simple.

“No, I’m just tired.”

Sometimes the person says something you didn’t expect.

“I thought you were making fun of me.”

Now you know what needs attention.

You don’t have to investigate every pause or facial expression. Constantly checking whether someone is upset can make an ordinary conversation exhausting for both people.

Look for moments when clarification would genuinely help, particularly when a misunderstanding seems to be affecting the interaction.

And if the other person says nothing is wrong, you generally need to allow that answer rather than interrogating them until they provide a different one.

Ask what happened before explaining yourself

When you realize somebody misunderstood you, the urge to explain can be immediate.

You may want to correct every detail, prove you weren’t being unkind, or defend the exact words you chose.

That makes sense. Being misunderstood can feel deeply uncomfortable.

But if the other person is trying to tell you they were hurt, launching into a long explanation may leave them feeling unheard.

Try to understand their experience first.

Imagine Jordan tells Priya, “You always have to make things complicated.”

Jordan intended to express frustration about an unexpectedly difficult plan. Priya hears a criticism of her personality.

When Priya says the comment hurt, Jordan could respond:

“That’s not what I meant. You know I’ve had a terrible day, and the reservation changed, and then there was traffic, and I wasn’t talking about you.”

Jordan might be telling the truth. But the response asks Priya to absorb an explanation before her experience has even been acknowledged.

A more useful beginning would be:

“That came out badly. What did it sound like I was saying?”

Priya answers:

“That I’m difficult to be with.”

Jordan now has information that matters.

“I was frustrated with changing the plans. I shouldn’t have made it sound like you were the problem. I’m sorry.”

The important shift is from defending the sentence to understanding the interaction.

Research on autistic communication suggests that misunderstandings sometimes involve different judgments about what information is relevant or what background the other person already knows. Those differences can operate in both directions (Marchena et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2021).

Clarification helps because it makes the missing information available.

You can approach an ordinary misunderstanding with four questions in mind:

  1. What actually happened? Identify the particular words, action, or moment.
  2. What did each person understand? Ask rather than guessing.
  3. Is something missing? Explain context, intention, or information that wasn’t shared.
  4. Does anything need to change? An apology, correction, practical adjustment, or simply an acknowledgment may be enough.

These are questions to guide your thinking, not a script you must recite.

You might find that you disagreed about what happened. You might discover the other person lacked information. You might realize that you were wrong.

All three are useful discoveries.

Different communication styles need room to meet

Imagine two people looking at the same sculpture from different positions, each wearing glasses that affect what details stand out.

That familiar picture from earlier in this book applies to misunderstandings particularly well.

What seems obvious from your position may not be visible from someone else’s.

For example, one person might ask:

“Why did you put the dishes there?”

They genuinely want to understand the arrangement. Perhaps they’re trying to learn where everything belongs.

Their partner might hear:

“Why would you do something so stupid?”

The same question has been understood as either a request for information or an accusation.

If the listener immediately becomes defensive, the questioner may be baffled. If the questioner insists that their wording could not possibly sound critical, the listener may feel dismissed.

Both can contribute to restoring understanding.

The questioner might say:

“I was genuinely asking where the dishes go. I wasn’t criticizing you.”

The listener might say:

“I heard it as criticism because of how the question was phrased. Thanks for explaining.”

Neither must pretend their first interpretation was the only reasonable one.

This matters particularly when people have different communication styles.

A review of research on autistic adults describes how questions intended to clarify meaning may sometimes be interpreted by non-autistic listeners as challenges or rudeness. The review also emphasizes that adapting communication should not be solely the autistic person’s responsibility (Marchena et al., 2025).

Someone who frequently asks, “What exactly do you mean?” may be trying to understand you accurately, not dispute your intelligence or honesty.

Likewise, someone who interprets your tone differently may be drawing on habits of communication you have not learned.

The double empathy problem is a reminder that understanding across different experiences takes participation from both people.

It is not proof that different neurotypes cannot understand each other.

In a small qualitative study of natural conversations, researchers observed substantial mutual understanding alongside moments when people differed about which information mattered (Williams et al., 2021).

You can respond to those differences without requiring anyone to abandon their natural communication style.

Try making the intention behind a question visible:

“I’m asking because I want to understand, not because I think you’re wrong.”

Or explain your interpretation without declaring it to be fact:

“When you said that, I heard it as criticism. Was that what you meant?”

If you are dating someone who regularly asks for clarification, consider giving a straightforward answer before assuming the question contains an accusation.

If you regularly need clarification yourself, you can explain what information is missing.

“Do you mean you want to leave now, or that you want to leave after dinner?”

There is nothing wrong with wanting a precise answer.

And there is nothing wrong with needing a moment to work out what you meant.

The aim is not perfect communication. It is being able to correct mistakes without making either person feel defective for communicating differently.

How to apologize without making it about yourself

An apology is appropriate when you recognize that something you did was wrong, careless, or hurtful.

It does not have to be elaborate.

It does need to acknowledge the actual behavior.

In two experiments involving 755 participants evaluating hypothetical workplace wrongdoing, researchers tested six elements of an apology: expressing regret, explaining what happened, accepting responsibility, expressing an intention to change, offering repair, and asking for forgiveness.

Apologies containing more of these elements were generally rated more effective. But the importance of individual elements varied between the two experiments. Acknowledging responsibility was especially important in one experiment, while offering repair and expressing an intention to change ranked higher in the other. Asking for forgiveness contributed relatively little (Lewicki et al., 2016).

Those studies did not test apologies between romantic partners. They cannot tell you exactly what words will restore someone’s trust.

They do provide a useful starting point.

A meaningful apology usually answers three questions:

What did you do?

Name the behavior rather than giving a vague apology for the whole situation.

“I’m sorry I interrupted you repeatedly.”

Why does it matter?

Show that you understand what happened to the other person, without arguing about whether they should have felt that way.

“You were trying to explain something important, and I didn’t let you finish.”

What will you do differently?

Offer a change that is specific enough to mean something.

“Next time, I’ll let you finish before I respond. If I interrupt, I’ll stop and give you the floor.”

You do not need to promise perfection. You need to make a realistic commitment and take it seriously.

Explanation is not the same as an excuse

Suppose Alex forgets a planned video call with his boyfriend, Mateo.

Alex had a difficult afternoon, lost track of time, and didn’t look at his calendar until much later.

He can explain that.

But an explanation alone doesn’t repair the missed commitment.

A useful response might be:

“I’m sorry I missed our call and didn’t let you know. I lost track of time, but I know you set aside the evening. I’m putting our calls in my calendar with an alert. I understand if you’re frustrated.”

The explanation helps Mateo understand what occurred. It does not make Mateo responsible for Alex’s mistake.

Alex also offers something observable: a change in how he remembers their plans.

Now imagine Alex adds:

“Please tell me you forgive me. I feel terrible, and I can’t stand knowing you’re upset.”

The conversation has changed.

Mateo is being asked to comfort Alex before he has had time to process what happened.

Feeling guilty or embarrassed is understandable. But an apology should not create a new obligation for the person who was hurt.

You can apologize sincerely and allow the other person to remain disappointed.

They may appreciate the apology without being ready to forgive. They may want time. They may decide that repeated problems have affected their willingness to continue dating.

Their response is theirs to choose.

An apology is an act of responsibility, not a way to purchase forgiveness.

Don’t apologize for something that isn’t wrong

Some people become so uncomfortable with disagreement that they apologize whenever another person seems upset.

They may say sorry for having preferences, asking reasonable questions, needing time to think, or declining something they do not want to do.

You can care about someone else’s disappointment without deciding that your legitimate needs are mistakes.

For example:

“I’m sorry I didn’t explain that clearly. I really do need a quiet evening tonight.”

That is different from:

“I’m sorry I’m so difficult. I’ll come out even though I don’t want to.”

The first acknowledges a communication problem while preserving the original decision.

The second attempts to settle discomfort by surrendering a need.

If you and your date wanted different things, and neither behaved badly, you may need understanding rather than an apology.

You are allowed to have different preferences. So are they.

The apology is only the beginning

Imagine your date repeatedly makes jokes about something you’ve said is sensitive.

The first time, they apologize and tell you they hadn’t realized the jokes bothered you.

The next time, they apologize again.

By the fifth time, the words may not mean much to you.

The problem is no longer that the person hasn’t found the perfect apology.

The problem is that their behavior has not changed.

Research on what couples do after conflict supports paying attention to actions beyond the immediate exchange.

Across three studies, researchers examined several responses after disagreements, including active repair, gaining a different perspective, avoidance, and letting go. In a two-week diary study involving 226 couples, active repair and gaining perspective were associated with better emotional recovery, while avoidant responses were associated with poorer recovery (Parsons et al., 2020).

Another study, involving 217 cohabiting heterosexual couples, found that attentive listening during everyday conflict predicted how helpful later reconciliation efforts were perceived to be (Wilder et al., 2024).

These are associations, not proof that one particular method will repair every relationship. The research also concerns broader couple conflict, not just misunderstandings.

Still, it suggests an important distinction: what happens after the words matters.

If you apologize for repeatedly talking over someone, let them finish the next conversation.

If you apologize for forgetting plans, use whatever reliable system helps you keep those plans.

If you discover that your sarcasm is regularly misunderstood, consider using it less often with that person or explaining when you are joking.

And if you tell someone that a remark hurt, notice whether they make room for that information next time.

Repair is not simply reaching the moment when everyone stops feeling uncomfortable.

It includes learning enough from the interaction that the same problem has a better chance of being handled differently.

Taking a break is not the same as avoiding repair

Sometimes neither person is ready to keep talking.

You may have too many feelings or questions competing for your attention. You may be close to overload. You may know what happened but not yet understand how you feel about it.

Continuing immediately might make the conversation less useful.

You can say:

“I want to understand this, but I need some time to think before I can give you a useful answer.”

If you intend to return to the conversation, it can help to agree on when or how.

“Could we talk about this tomorrow afternoon?”

Then follow through if the other person agrees.

A mutually understood break is different from ignoring someone to punish them, refusing every attempt to address a serious concern, or disappearing without explanation when you have made a commitment to discuss it.

You cannot demand that another person process feelings on your schedule. You also do not have to continue an emotionally difficult conversation at a moment when you cannot participate meaningfully.

Does a misunderstanding have to be repaired face-to-face?

Not necessarily.

Text messages are convenient, but they leave room for ambiguity about tone.

In experiments on email communication, senders overestimated how accurately recipients could recognize intended humor and sarcasm (Kruger et al., 2005).

A separate study of 276 young adults in romantic relationships found different associations between affectionate and hurtful texting patterns and relationship outcomes. It did not establish that apologies delivered by text are inherently less effective (Schade et al., 2013).

These findings suggest some caution about relying on tone alone.

Consider:

“Well, that was great.”

Depending on the circumstances, that could mean genuine enjoyment, sarcasm, irritation, or an attempt to end the conversation.

When something has already gone wrong, there is little benefit in adding another ambiguous remark.

A clearer text might say:

“I realized my last message could sound sarcastic. I meant that I enjoyed spending time with you. I’m sorry I wasn’t clear.”

But this does not mean that everyone should move difficult conversations to a phone call.

A UK study of 245 autistic adults found that many participants preferred written communication in certain situations. Writing could provide time to think, organize thoughts, and communicate from a comfortable environment. Phone calls were particularly unpopular, although preferences varied depending on the relationship and purpose of the conversation (Howard & Sedgewick, 2021).

For some people, face-to-face conversation makes it easier to understand emotion. For others, the pressure of responding immediately makes understanding harder.

A person’s preferred method may also change with fatigue, trust, or overload.

The practical question is not, “Which method is supposed to be best?”

It is, “Which method will let both of us understand and respond?”

You might say:

“I think we’re talking past each other in these messages. Would you rather keep writing, talk in person, or come back to this later?”

The other person might prefer a method that isn’t your favorite.

You can discuss what works, but neither person is entitled to force a conversation through a particular channel.

And if someone has declined further contact, the task is no longer to find a better communication method. Their decision needs to be respected.

If you can't discuss it while it's still happening

Needing to leave a difficult conversation is not avoidance, and it isn’t a refusal to repair. Past a certain point you may genuinely not have language available, and continuing produces worse outcomes for both of you.

Leave with a return attached. “I want to sort this out and I can’t do it right now. Can we talk at eight?” is very different from walking out, and most people can wait when they know they aren’t being abandoned.

Then keep the appointment, even if you still don’t have perfect words. Coming back on time is most of what makes the pause trustworthy.

When your brain needs a different pace

Not everyone can identify a misunderstanding, understand their emotions, choose accurate words, and respond immediately.

Some people need time to process spoken language. Others realize an interaction bothered them several hours afterward.

Some lose their train of thought when asked multiple questions in quick succession. Others find it difficult to remember a conversation accurately once it ends.

These experiences can affect how a repair conversation unfolds, regardless of whether someone has a diagnosis.

If you only realize later that something hurt

You are allowed to revisit an earlier exchange.

For example:

“I’ve been thinking about what happened at dinner yesterday. At the time, I couldn’t explain why I was quiet. I realize now that the joke about my job embarrassed me.”

You don’t need to pretend you understood your feelings immediately.

If you’re dating someone who processes their reactions more slowly, an answer that arrives later is not automatically less sincere or less important.

Give each other reasonable time to respond without assuming that every delay is deliberate avoidance.

If you forget things you meant to do

Forgetting a commitment can become a misunderstanding about priorities.

One person experiences the missed plan as evidence that they aren’t important. The other may have genuinely lost track of it.

A research review describes greater average relationship difficulties among adults with ADHD, while emphasizing gaps in understanding the processes involved and which supports work best (Wymbs et al., 2021).

That research does not establish a particular apology method for forgotten commitments.

A practical approach is to distinguish your reason from your responsibility.

You can explain that you forgot without expecting the other person to be unaffected.

You can also choose supports that fit your actual difficulties: recording plans immediately, using reminders, or checking arrangements beforehand.

Those tools are not proof of affection. They are ways to make your intentions more consistent with your actions.

And if someone repeatedly forgets what matters to you without trying to address the problem, you can take that pattern seriously without deciding that their neurotype makes them a bad person.

If a small misunderstanding feels frightening

Sometimes an ordinary disagreement carries more emotional weight than either person expected.

A raised voice, a particular phrase, or an abrupt change in tone may be especially difficult for someone whose earlier relationships or family experiences involved fear, humiliation, or unpredictability.

A meta-analysis of 31 studies found a moderate association between PTSD measures and problems in intimate relationships. The authors could not establish which caused which, and the findings do not describe how every trauma-affected person responds to an ordinary misunderstanding (Taft et al., 2011).

You do not need to diagnose your reaction, or someone else’s, to respond respectfully.

You may need time before continuing.

You might say:

“I know you want to explain, but I need a little time before I can listen properly.”

If your partner asks for time, you can respect that without insisting on immediate reassurance.

Neither person’s history requires the other to tolerate harmful behavior. But recognizing that someone may need a different pace can make ordinary clarification more possible.

If you're dating someone who needs to leave the conversation

Someone stepping away mid-argument may be preventing a worse version of it rather than refusing to engage. Following them to finish the discussion usually guarantees the worse version.

Agree the pattern in advance, when nothing is wrong: either of you can call a pause, and whoever calls it names when you’ll resume. That turns leaving into a shared tool rather than something done to you.

Waiting is hard when you process by talking. Saying “I find the gap difficult, so a time helps me” is a fair thing to ask for in return.

Sometimes you understood each other perfectly

Not every uncomfortable conversation is a misunderstanding.

Suppose you want to see someone three evenings a week. They want to meet once a week.

You explain your preferences clearly. They explain theirs.

Nobody has misunderstood.

You simply want different things.

Or imagine you tell someone that a particular comment is hurtful. They understand exactly what you mean and say they intend to keep making it.

That is not a communication problem you can fix by choosing more precise words.

Research reviews of romantic conflict suggest that different communication approaches can help or hurt depending on the seriousness of the issue and whether change is possible. There is no universally effective style for every disagreement (Overall & McNulty, 2017).

Being clear is useful. Being endlessly accommodating is not always a solution. And repeatedly explaining something does not guarantee that another person will agree or change.

There are three different outcomes worth recognizing:

A misunderstanding: “You thought I meant something I didn’t mean.”

Clarification may solve it.

A disagreement: “You understand what I mean, but you want something different.”

The issue is whether a mutually acceptable decision is possible.

A harmful pattern: “You understand that this hurts me, but the behavior continues.”

The issue is no longer just the message. It is the behavior and whether the relationship remains good for you.

Recognizing the difference can save you from endlessly rewriting a perfectly clear sentence.

When an apology is not enough

The advice in this chapter assumes that two people are trying to understand each other respectfully.

It is not a method for handling coercion, intimidation, or abuse.

The CDC’s definition of intimate partner violence includes physical violence, sexual violence, stalking, and psychological aggression, including conduct intended to harm or control another person (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026).

Repeated humiliation, threats, unwanted pressure, or controlling behavior should not be dismissed as ordinary communication differences.

Nor does the double empathy problem mean that every harmful interaction is equally the responsibility of both people.

A misunderstanding can be mutual. Responsibility for harmful conduct belongs to the person who engages in it.

If someone repeatedly makes you frightened or pressures you after you have made your wishes clear, you do not owe them another discussion to prove that you communicated correctly.

Your safety matters more than repairing the conversation. Trusted support may be more useful than another attempt to explain.

If you recognize yourself pressuring, intimidating, or controlling someone, stop the behavior and seek appropriate professional help. An apology is not permission to resume unwanted contact.

And if someone has said they do not want further communication, respect that decision. Do not keep contacting them to obtain forgiveness, defend your intentions, or secure one more conversation.

Later chapters address boundaries, consent, harmful persistence, and abuse in greater detail.

A small practice: what happened, what I heard, what I need

You don’t have to wait for a painful disagreement to practice repair.

Think about a minor misunderstanding from a conversation, friendship, family relationship, or date.

Choose something ordinary rather than a frightening or unsafe interaction.

Write down three things.

1. What happened?

Try to record the words or actions without adding motives.

Instead of:

“They deliberately ignored me.”

Write:

“They didn’t answer my question during dinner.”

2. What did I think it meant?

Be specific about your interpretation.

“I thought they were annoyed with me.”

Then ask yourself: What other explanations might fit?

Perhaps they didn’t hear. Perhaps they were distracted. Perhaps they were annoyed. You don’t know yet.

3. What information would help me understand?

Choose one short, respectful question.

“When I asked you about Saturday, did you hear me?”

Or:

“I wasn’t sure whether you wanted to make plans. Are you interested in getting together?”

Notice the difference between asking for information and demanding a particular answer.

You can also practice from the other direction.

Think of a time when someone interpreted your words differently than you intended.

What context did they lack? Could you explain that context simply? Did you cause any harm that needs acknowledgment?

You may find that one accurate sentence does more than ten minutes of defending yourself.

This practice is not a research-tested repair technique. It is a way to build the habit of separating observation, interpretation, and the information you still need.

Returning to the conversation

Sam and Ellis, from the beginning of this chapter, are sitting outside the restaurant.

Sam notices that Ellis has become quiet.

“Did I upset you when I said I wanted to leave?”

Ellis hesitates.

“I thought you weren’t having a good time.”

Sam answers:

“I was having a good time. The noise was getting hard for me, and I needed to get somewhere quieter.”

Ellis looks relieved.

“I didn’t realize. I thought you wanted to get away from me.”

“I can understand why it sounded that way. Next time I’ll explain what I mean.”

Ellis considers this.

“And I can ask instead of assuming.”

Nothing extraordinary has happened. Neither person produced a perfect speech. They didn’t discover a secret relationship rule or solve every future disagreement.

They learned something about how the other person communicates.

Maybe next time Sam will mention the noise earlier. Maybe Ellis will ask whether Sam wants to leave the restaurant or end their time together.

Either way, they have more information than they had before.

That’s what an ordinary repair can accomplish.

It gives two people a way to return to understanding after their words have gone in different directions.

You will sometimes say things badly. You will sometimes hear things that weren’t intended. Other people will misunderstand you, and you will misunderstand them.

You can ask. You can listen. You can explain. When you’ve caused harm, you can apologize and change what you do next.

You cannot guarantee the other person’s response. You can make your own communication more honest and responsible.

And sometimes, after the misunderstanding is cleared up, you will still want different things.

That is important information too.

The skills you’ve learned about communicating clearly become particularly important when the subject is not merely what someone meant, but what they are comfortable with, what they want, and what they will or will not accept.

Understanding someone’s limits begins with taking their words seriously.
