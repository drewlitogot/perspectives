You have had the same argument with three different partners. Or you have never been in a relationship, but the thought of dating makes you so anxious that you keep putting it off. Perhaps you are dating someone kind, yet small misunderstandings turn into exhausting conversations that neither of you knows how to finish.

You might wonder whether you need therapy, whether your partner needs it, or whether you simply need to learn a few things nobody taught you.

Sometimes the answer is more than one of those.

Getting help does not mean something is fundamentally wrong with you. It also does not mean every difficulty requires a diagnosis, a therapist, or years of treatment. Some problems improve when you learn a skill, change an arrangement, or find people who understand your experiences. Others need more specialized support.

The useful question is not “Am I broken enough to need therapy?” It is “What am I having trouble with, and what kind of help would actually address it?”

You do not have to reach a breaking point

You do not need to wait until a relationship ends or your everyday life becomes unmanageable before asking for help.

Consider seeking support if:

  • You keep encountering the same painful relationship pattern and cannot figure out how to change it.
  • Anxiety, fear, or memories of past experiences interfere with dating or closeness.
  • You have trouble recognizing or expressing your own needs, even when you want to.
  • Arguments repeatedly leave you distressed, exhausted, or unable to resolve anything.
  • You are struggling with a breakup, rejection, loneliness, or the possibility of getting close to someone.
  • You want help understanding attention, sensory, emotional, or communication differences that affect your relationships.

These are reasons to consider support, not a diagnostic checklist. One difficult date does not mean you need treatment. Neither does needing time alone, communicating directly, or having an unusual interest.

You can also seek therapy while single. There is no requirement to bring a partner, have relationship experience, or know exactly what is wrong.

And you can be doing reasonably well while still wanting help with something specific.

Different problems call for different kinds of help

“Get therapy” is common advice, but it leaves out an important question: therapy for what?

Individual therapy gives you a place to explore your own distress, experiences, reactions, or patterns. You might want to understand why disagreement frightens you, why you struggle to recognize your feelings, or why a past relationship still affects you.

Couples counseling, also called couple therapy, focuses on difficulties within a relationship. It provides a structured setting in which both partners can describe what is happening, understand each other’s experiences, and work on changes they both choose.

Peer support involves connecting with people who have relevant lived experiences. A support group might offer recognition, practical ideas, or companionship. It is not necessarily treatment.

Practical support addresses concrete difficulties. You might need help organizing appointments, managing daily responsibilities, accessing services, or finding a workable communication system rather than spending every session analyzing feelings. In a synthesis of autistic adults’ experiences with mental-health services, participants valued support that was individually tailored, accessible, and useful for practical concerns (Andoni et al., 2026).

Some people benefit from several forms of support at once.

Imagine Taylor, 34, who wants to date but keeps forgetting to reply to messages and feels ashamed afterward.

Taylor might benefit from a reminder system, clearer expectations about texting, or help with everyday organization. If the difficulty also involves significant distress, an individual therapist might help. A therapist would not necessarily be the person to build every reminder or solve every scheduling problem.

Now consider Evelyn, 61, who has started dating again after a painful divorce. She becomes frightened whenever someone expresses disappointment, even respectfully. Individual therapy might help her understand why those moments feel threatening and develop different ways to respond.

Neither person needs to prove that their problem is serious enough. They need to identify what they want help changing.

What therapy can and cannot promise

Therapy is not one treatment. Different approaches are designed for different problems, and their evidence varies.

For example, when someone has post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), US Department of Veterans Affairs guidance strongly supports particular trauma-focused treatments: Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, usually called EMDR (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, National Center for PTSD, 2026).

That does not mean everyone with a painful childhood has PTSD, or that those treatments are automatically the right choice for someone who finds dating difficult.

Likewise, therapy that helps a person manage anxiety has not necessarily been shown to improve romantic compatibility.

It is reasonable to ask a therapist what they propose treating, how the approach is supposed to help, and how you will know whether it is working.

Therapy also has limits.

It cannot make another person love you, guarantee that someone will stay, or turn an incompatible relationship into a compatible one. It cannot replace another person’s consent.

You might understand your own behavior better and still discover that a relationship is not right for you.

That is not automatically a failure of therapy. A useful outcome can be making a clearer decision.

Finding a therapist who understands how your mind works

If you have ever tried to explain an experience and been told you are thinking about it incorrectly, the prospect of explaining yourself to a stranger may not sound appealing.

Some people have already had disappointing experiences with professionals. Others worry that a therapist will see their communication differences as problems that must be corrected.

Those concerns deserve to be taken seriously.

A study involving 200 autistic adults found that participants reported difficulties accessing mental-health support, including professionals who did not adequately understand autism. Individually tailored support was something participants valued and wanted (Camm-Crosbie et al., 2019).

A systematic review of 30 studies examined ways mental-health care had been adapted for autistic people. These included changes to communication, session structure, treatment content, and the physical environment. The evidence for whether adaptations were feasible and acceptable was more consistent than the evidence that they improved clinical outcomes (Loizou et al., 2024).

That distinction matters. An adjustment can make therapy easier to access and participate in without being a separately proven treatment.

What does “neurodivergent-affirming” actually mean?

You may encounter therapists describing themselves as neurodivergent-affirming or neurodiversity-affirming.

In practical terms, an affirming approach should respect that people think, communicate, process information, and experience the world differently. The aim is not automatically to make someone behave more like a neurotypical person.

But the phrase alone is not proof of competence. The research reviewed for this chapter does not establish an independently validated credential that guarantees a therapist is neurodivergent-affirming.

Look at what the therapist actually does.

For example, does the therapist:

  • Ask how you prefer to communicate rather than assuming one way is best?
  • Allow extra time to answer difficult questions?
  • Explain unfamiliar terms and describe what will happen during sessions?
  • Accept written notes, concrete examples, or questions sent in advance when appropriate?
  • Take sensory discomfort, overload, and shutdown seriously?
  • Recognize that avoiding eye contact or needing time alone does not automatically mean hostility, dishonesty, or lack of caring?
  • Help you address difficulties you actually want to change, rather than deciding what a “normal” relationship should look like?

These are questions about fit, not a validated scoring system.

In one survey of 130 autistic adults who had experienced therapy, participants rated therapist understanding of autism and an affirming approach particularly highly. Participants also valued practical accessibility measures, including easier appointment booking. Preferences varied, however, and the sample was mostly Australian women, limiting how broadly its findings can be applied (Paynter et al., 2025).

One person may want a detailed written agenda. Another may prefer an open conversation. One may find remote therapy less tiring; another may find it difficult to connect through a screen.

An affirming therapist should be curious about the individual rather than assuming everyone with a particular label needs the same thing.

Understanding you does not mean agreeing with everything

A therapist who respects neurodivergence should not treat every difficulty as a defect.

But affirming care is not the same as being told every choice you make is healthy.

If you repeatedly ignore a partner’s boundaries, become controlling when afraid of losing someone, or use distress to excuse hurtful behavior, a helpful therapist should be able to address that directly.

The same applies to someone who is neurotypical, autistic, ADHD, or unsure of their neurotype.

Your experiences may help explain why something is difficult. They do not erase another person’s right to safety and autonomy.

Good support respects your differences while helping you take responsibility for your choices.

How to find help in the United States

Finding a therapist can feel strangely complicated for a service that is supposed to help people who are already struggling.

The National Institute of Mental Health identifies several starting points, including primary-care providers, insurance networks, school or college health services, employee assistance programs, and public mental-health services (National Institute of Mental Health, 2026).

You do not have to search everywhere at once.

If you have health insurance, start with the insurer’s provider directory and verify any promising result directly. If you already see a primary-care provider, you can ask for a referral.

If you do not have insurance or need public treatment services, the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) maintains information about treatment locators, including FindTreatment.gov for mental-health and substance-use facilities (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2026).

Useful starting pages are:

  • NIMH: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/find-help
  • SAMHSA treatment locators: https://www.samhsa.gov/find-help/locators
  • FindTreatment.gov: https://findtreatment.gov

These resources are starting points, not endorsements of every provider listed. A facility directory is not necessarily a directory of individual therapists or couples counselors.

Check qualifications, not just descriptions

Therapists may be psychologists, licensed professional counselors, clinical social workers, marriage and family therapists, or other appropriately qualified providers.

Their training and professional responsibilities can differ.

If you are seeking couples counseling, ask about training and experience in working with couples, not just individual therapy. If you need help with trauma, ask about the therapist’s experience with your particular concerns.

You can also check a clinician’s active license through the relevant state licensing board. For psychologists, the Association of State and Provincial Psychology Boards recommends verifying licensure and authorization to provide services online (Association of State and Provincial Psychology Boards, n.d.).

This matters especially if you are considering remote therapy with someone located in another state. Permission to provide telehealth services across state lines varies by jurisdiction.

A polished website, an appealing specialty label, or a listing in a directory should not replace these questions.

What to ask before your first appointment

You are allowed to interview a prospective therapist.

This is not a test in which you must give the correct answers to earn their approval. You are deciding whether their services meet your needs.

You may be able to ask questions during a short consultation, through email, or during the first appointment. There is no need to reveal your entire history just to ask about a therapist’s approach.

You could say:

“I’m looking for help with relationships and communication. I sometimes need extra time to process questions, and I prefer direct explanations. Is that something you can accommodate?”

Or:

“I have had difficulty with therapists misunderstanding how I communicate. What experience do you have working with autistic adults or people with ADHD?”

If you are seeking couples counseling:

“My partner and I communicate differently, and neither of us wants the other person treated as the problem. How would you approach that?”

Other useful questions include:

  1. Experience: What experience or training do you have with the concerns I am bringing?
  2. Approach: What kind of therapy would you suggest, and why?
  3. Adjustments: Can sessions include written notes, a predictable agenda, or additional processing time?
  4. Goals: How would we decide what to work on and recognize progress?
  5. Practical details: What do sessions cost, what insurance do you accept, and what are your cancellation rules?
  6. Privacy: What are the limits of confidentiality, and who can access my records?

NIMH encourages people to ask about treatment methods, provider experience, costs, and what to expect, and to raise concerns if treatment is not helping (National Institute of Mental Health, 2026).

You do not need to ask every question in one conversation. Choose the ones that matter most to you.

If speaking on the phone is difficult, a written inquiry may be easier. If forms take considerable energy, completing them over several sittings or asking whether assistance is available may make the process more manageable.

The objective is to make help accessible, not to prove you can navigate an inconvenient system without assistance.

What if you are unsure about a diagnosis?

Some people have spent years being told that they are anxious, depressed, inattentive, socially awkward, or too sensitive. Others have been given explanations that do not seem to fit their experiences.

You might suspect autism, ADHD, trauma-related difficulties, some combination, or none of them.

You do not have to solve that question before asking for help.

You can describe what happens:

“When someone asks how I feel, I often don’t know until hours later.”

Or:

“I lose track of plans even when they’re important to me.”

Or:

“When someone raises their voice, I become frightened and have difficulty speaking.”

These descriptions give a professional something concrete to explore without requiring you to diagnose yourself.

If you want an assessment, ask what kind of evaluation the professional is qualified to provide and whether a referral would be appropriate.

If a label is useful to you, explore it. If you are not ready to pursue one, you can still ask for support with a particular difficulty.

If you don't know what to call what you're dealing with

You do not need a diagnosis, or a settled view about whether one fits, before asking for help. “Here is what is hard and here is what I’ve tried” is enough to start with.

Describe the experience rather than the label: trouble in busy places, exhaustion after socializing, going blank in conflict, losing track of time. That is more useful to a decent clinician than a term you’re unsure about.

If someone treats the absence of a diagnosis as a reason not to help, that’s information about them. It is reasonable to look for someone else.

What a useful therapy session might look like

Therapy does not require you to sit in a chair and spontaneously produce a perfect explanation of your feelings.

You can bring notes.

Suppose you are trying to explain a conflict in which your partner said you seemed uninterested. You remember the conversation clearly but cannot explain why it upset you.

Instead of beginning with an abstract question about your emotional life, you might bring a written account:

“My partner told me I seemed distant. I was exhausted and needed quiet. I didn’t know they interpreted my silence as anger. I want to understand what happened and how to explain myself sooner.”

That is enough to begin.

You can also ask for concrete examples instead of general statements.

If a therapist says, “You need to be more emotionally available,” it is reasonable to ask:

“What would that look like in a specific situation? Can you give me an example?”

If the discussion becomes difficult to follow:

“I need a moment to think before answering.”

And if you disagree with an interpretation:

“That explanation doesn’t match my experience. Could we consider another possibility?”

You are allowed to disagree respectfully. A therapist’s professional training does not mean every interpretation is correct.

At the same time, therapy sometimes involves examining uncomfortable possibilities. Feeling challenged is not automatically evidence of a poor therapist. The important question is whether the challenge is understandable, relevant, respectful, and open to discussion.

How to tell whether therapy is helping

After several sessions, consider what has changed.

You might ask yourself:

  • Do I understand what I am working toward?
  • Can I explain my experiences without spending most of the session correcting misunderstandings?
  • Does the therapist listen when I say an approach is not helping?
  • Am I learning something I can use outside the appointment?
  • Do I feel able to ask questions or disagree?
  • Have we discussed how to tell whether the work is useful?

Progress is not always immediate or comfortable. But therapy should not become an indefinite process that you feel unable to question.

If something is not working, bring it up. You can ask to change the approach, clarify goals, or seek another professional opinion.

NIMH explicitly recognizes that finding effective help may involve discussing treatment concerns or looking for a different provider (National Institute of Mental Health, 2026).

Changing therapists is not automatically giving up. Neither is staying with a therapist after a difficult session automatically the right choice.

Couples counseling: help for a relationship, not a contest

Couples counseling is sometimes imagined as a room where two people present their arguments and a professional decides who is right.

That is not a useful way to approach it.

The purpose is not to convince a therapist that your partner is unreasonable. It is to understand the relationship difficulty, identify what each person can choose to change, and decide whether the relationship can meet both people’s needs.

Does it work?

There is meaningful evidence that couples counseling can help with relationship distress.

A meta-analysis combining 58 studies, representing 40 independent samples and 2,092 couples, found substantial average improvements in relationship satisfaction among couples receiving therapy. Gains generally persisted at follow-up. The studies included only opposite-sex couples, and improvements measured before and after treatment should not be confused with the full effect attributable to therapy (Roddy et al., 2020).

A separate research review covering studies published from 2010 through 2019 identified several established approaches to treating couple distress, including behavioral, cognitive-behavioral, emotionally focused, and integrative behavioral couple therapies (Doss et al., 2022).

These approaches differ in their methods, but the broader finding is useful: couples counseling is not simply two people talking while a stranger watches.

There are organized forms of treatment with research behind them.

However, evidence for couples therapy in general is not a guarantee of success for any particular couple. It also does not establish that one approach is best for every combination of personalities, cultures, sexual orientations, or neurotypes.

When might a couple try it?

Imagine Mateo and Ben, both in their thirties.

Mateo needs quiet after work. Ben feels connected through conversation and looks forward to discussing the day immediately.

They have tried explaining their preferences, but the same problem returns. Ben interprets Mateo’s withdrawal as rejection. Mateo experiences the questions as demands when he has little energy left.

Neither needs to be the villain for the disagreement to matter.

They might decide to seek couples counseling to find a workable arrangement and understand why their previous attempts failed.

A possible goal could be:

“We want to find a way to reconnect after work that respects both our needs.”

That is more useful than:

“We need the therapist to explain why my partner is wrong.”

A couple does not have to be married to seek relationship counseling. The important starting point is that both people freely agree to participate and understand what they want help with.

If one partner does not want to attend, the other can seek individual support. Counseling cannot create genuine participation through pressure.

What about couples with different neurotypes?

The book has already explored how differences in attention, sensory experience, emotional processing, and communication can affect relationships.

The same differences can affect therapy.

A conventional session might involve rapid questions, interpreting facial expressions, or expecting both people to describe feelings immediately. That format may suit one partner better than the other.

A therapist who is unfamiliar with neurodivergence might misunderstand what is happening.

For example, one partner may describe needing time alone after an argument. The therapist should not automatically interpret that need as an attempt to punish or reject the other person. Nor should the therapist assume that the partner who wants reassurance is being unreasonable.

Both experiences deserve investigation.

A review by Stafford examined nine sources about counseling for partnerships involving autistic adults. The literature proposed several accommodations: therapist knowledge of autism, direct discussion of needs, concrete structure, visual supports, sensory awareness, and attention to both partners’ perspectives (Stafford, 2023).

These are reasonable possibilities to discuss with a therapist.

But there is a crucial limitation: the research has barely tested whether these adaptations improve relationship outcomes.

The only trial of a particular model identified in Stafford’s review involved one couple. After treatment, relationship satisfaction increased for one partner and decreased for the other. That is far too little evidence to declare a particular method successful for autistic couples (Stafford, 2023).

Nor should a therapist assume that every autistic person wants more structure, every non-autistic partner wants more emotional conversation, or every relationship involving ADHD has the same problems.

A review of ADHD and romantic relationships identifies relationship difficulties and intervention needs, but the abstract available for this chapter does not establish an effective ADHD-specific couples-therapy protocol (Wymbs et al., 2021).

The most useful starting point is therefore individualization.

A couple might agree with the therapist to try:

  • Giving each partner uninterrupted time to describe an experience.
  • Writing down the issue being discussed so neither person loses track.
  • Using specific situations rather than broad claims about personality.
  • Allowing time to process questions before responding.
  • Discussing how either person can request a pause when overload makes conversation difficult.
  • Reviewing proposed agreements in writing to check that both people understood them.

These are practical options, not a scientifically validated treatment package.

Both people’s experiences count

Consider Nadia, who is autistic, and her wife, Claire, who is not.

Nadia finds spontaneous changes exhausting. Claire feels frustrated when an unexpected invitation requires a lengthy discussion.

If the therapist assumes Nadia is too rigid, the therapy may become an effort to make Nadia tolerate every change.

If the therapist assumes Claire is insensitive, the therapy may dismiss Claire’s wish to enjoy occasional spontaneity.

Neither interpretation captures the entire problem.

They might explore which plans genuinely need to remain predictable, where flexibility is possible, and how to ask for changes without treating the other person’s needs as an inconvenience.

The same principle applies when both partners are neurodivergent, including partners who are neurodivergent in different ways.

A person who needs predictable routines and someone who finds routines difficult may need help designing arrangements that respect both experiences. Neither person’s diagnosis decides whose needs matter more.

The research reviewed by Stafford mainly involved White couples consisting of autistic men and non-autistic women. It offers little direct evidence about autistic–autistic couples, same-gender couples, nonbinary partners, or partnerships involving several neurotypes (Stafford, 2023).

Those gaps should make a therapist more curious, not more confident in assumptions.

If you're dating someone who is starting therapy

Therapy is theirs. Asking how it went is friendly; asking what was said each week, or treating it as a progress report you’re owed, turns it into something they have to manage around you.

Expect some unevenness. Useful sessions can leave someone raw for a day or two, and that isn’t a sign it’s going badly.

If their therapy keeps becoming a subject in your arguments, that’s usually a sign you need somewhere of your own to think — your own therapist, or couples work — rather than more access to theirs.

When couples counseling is not the right place

There is an important difference between ordinary relationship conflict and abuse.

If a partner frightens you, controls your choices, threatens you, or punishes you for disagreeing, that is not simply a communication difference.

You may feel unable to say what actually happens because you fear the consequences afterward.

Couples counseling is not recommended when an abusive partner is involved.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline warns that joint therapy in abusive relationships can be unsafe because of unequal power, possible retaliation, and the risk that abuse will be misunderstood as a problem both partners share responsibility for (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.).

A diagnosis or communication difference does not excuse coercion. Nor does abuse become harmless because both partners sometimes argue.

If you recognize controlling or abusive behavior in yourself, stop that behavior, take responsibility, and seek appropriate individual professional help. Do not make your partner participate in counseling to prove that they care or to obtain another opportunity to persuade them.

If you are experiencing abuse, consider seeking confidential, individual support from a professional or service familiar with relationship abuse before agreeing to joint sessions.

Privacy, confidentiality, and the question nobody thinks to ask

Therapy involves sharing personal information. Before you begin, you deserve to understand what happens to it.

For individual counseling, ask who can access your records, what confidentiality means in that setting, and when information may have to be disclosed.

Confidentiality has limits. The exact requirements vary by state, setting, and circumstances.

Couples counseling adds complications.

Are both partners considered clients? Does the therapist have individual meetings with either person? If one partner discloses something privately, what happens next? Who can access the records?

The American Psychological Association’s ethics code requires psychologists to clarify informed consent and confidentiality limits, and to explain roles and responsibilities when providing services involving couples or families (American Psychological Association, 2017).

Different professionals and jurisdictions may operate under different rules.

Do not assume that something said privately to a couples therapist will automatically remain secret from your partner. Ask about the therapist’s policy before sharing information you expect to remain confidential.

A useful question is:

“Before we start, could you explain who your client is, how separate conversations are handled, and what information each partner can access?”

You can also ask what happens if one person decides to stop attending.

Understanding the arrangement beforehand can prevent confusion later.

What if you cannot afford therapy?

Cost is a real obstacle, and some people have fewer local options than others.

If you have insurance through an Affordable Care Act Marketplace plan, mental-health benefits are included, although covered services and out-of-pocket costs depend on the plan and state (HealthCare.gov, n.d.).

That does not mean all couples counseling is covered.

Before booking, ask both the provider and insurer whether the particular service is covered, whether the clinician is in-network, and what you would owe.

If the cost is too high, possible avenues to investigate include public mental-health programs, employee assistance programs, college counseling services, and providers who offer reduced fees. NIMH identifies several public and institutional routes for finding care (National Institute of Mental Health, 2026).

You can ask:

“I would like to start counseling, but I have a limited budget. Do you offer reduced fees, know of lower-cost services, or have a referral you could suggest?”

You do not need to justify your finances beyond what is necessary to ask about options.

If regular therapy is inaccessible, support groups, practical resources, and conversations with trustworthy people may still provide some help. They should not be represented as replacements for professional treatment when that treatment is needed.

It is also reasonable to work on one small relationship skill while you look for professional support.

For example, you might practice identifying your feelings in writing, communicating a request clearly, or deciding what support you want from a friend.

Progress does not have to stop because an appointment is unavailable.

What if a therapist is not a good fit?

Sometimes a therapist and client simply do not communicate well together.

Other times there is a more serious problem, such as repeated dismissal of your concerns, inappropriate assumptions about your identity, or an unwillingness to explain the treatment approach.

You do not have to ignore those concerns.

There is a difference between a therapist respectfully challenging a behavior and a therapist repeatedly refusing to understand how you experience the world.

Suppose a therapist tells an autistic client that maintaining eye contact is necessary to show a romantic partner respect.

The client might explain that eye contact makes it harder to listen. A useful discussion could explore how the client and partner can communicate attention and care in ways that suit them.

If the therapist insists that eye contact is the only acceptable expression of respect, the client has reason to question whether the approach fits their needs.

The goal is not to avoid all discomfort or disagreement. It is to find help that treats your actual concerns seriously.

You can tell a therapist:

“I don’t think this approach is helping me. Could we discuss why we’re using it and whether something else would fit better?”

You can seek a second opinion or change providers.

If you do leave, you can ask about referral options or how to continue care elsewhere. You do not owe a therapist indefinite attendance because you have already invested time.

A small exercise: decide what help you actually want

If this chapter has left you with several options and no obvious place to begin, use three short questions.

1. What keeps happening?

Describe one specific experience without trying to explain your entire life.

For example:

“I agree to things I do not want because I am afraid someone will leave.”

Or:

“My partner and I have the same argument about changing plans every week.”

2. What would be different if I had useful help?

Choose something observable.

“I could express a disagreement without immediately apologizing for having one.”

Or:

“We could make plans that consider both of our needs.”

3. What is one manageable next step?

You might check your insurance directory, write an email to a therapist, ask a primary-care provider for a referral, or look into a support group.

If making an appointment feels too difficult today, the first step can simply be writing down what you would want to say.

Keep the answers somewhere accessible. You can bring them to a first appointment, use them during a conversation with a partner, or revisit them later.

They do not have to be perfect.

Getting help without giving up your own judgment

Therapy is not a place where someone else takes over responsibility for your relationships.

A therapist can offer expertise, perspective, and structured support. You still decide whether to date someone, what boundaries you need, what kind of relationship you want, and which changes you are willing to make.

Your partner has the same rights.

The familiar sculpture-and-glasses picture applies here too. A therapist may help two people describe the different views they have of the same situation. The goal is not to declare one person’s view the official version.

Sometimes understanding leads to an agreement. Sometimes it reveals an incompatibility that cannot be solved by explaining it again.

Both outcomes can give you useful information.

And sometimes the difficulty is not a relationship disagreement at all. It may involve personal distress, trauma, abuse, or a safety concern that calls for a different kind of help.

The next chapter explains where to turn in the United States when support is urgent, safety is at risk, or an ordinary therapy appointment is not enough.

Whether you seek professional treatment, practical help, or a community that understands your experiences, you do not have to learn every relationship skill alone.

Dating is something you can keep learning and practicing. Support gives you more ways to do that, at your own pace, while respecting both yourself and the people you meet.
