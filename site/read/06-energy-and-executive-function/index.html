You have a date at seven. You like the person. You’ve been looking forward to seeing them all week.

At 6:15, you’re still finishing something on your computer. You know you need to get ready, but stopping feels strangely difficult. At 6:30, you finally stand up. Then you remember that your clean clothes are in the dryer. Your phone is almost dead. You can’t find your keys.

At 6:55, you’re leaving the house.

The restaurant is twenty minutes away.

Meanwhile, the person you’re meeting is sitting at a table, wondering whether you’ve forgotten about them.

Both experiences are real. You genuinely wanted to be there. They genuinely feel disappointed.

The problem might not be your interest in the person. It might be the chain of actions between wanting to do something and actually doing it.

Dating involves plenty of those chains. You have to choose a time, make a plan, remember the plan, prepare for it, arrive, communicate, and sometimes follow up afterward. Even a simple coffee date has several separate tasks hidden inside it.

If some of those tasks are difficult for you, dating can feel harder than it appears to other people.

Understanding where things go wrong helps you make realistic commitments, explain your needs, and avoid asking someone else to carry responsibilities you haven’t learned how to handle.

Caring and doing are two different things

Executive functions are mental processes that help you organize and carry out what you intend to do. They include planning, holding information in mind, managing attention, controlling impulses, and switching between activities.

Consider the difference between these statements:

  • “I want to see you on Saturday.”
  • “I have checked my schedule, chosen a place, confirmed the time, and arranged how to get there.”

The first is an intention. The second is an intention supported by actions.

Some people move between those stages relatively easily. Others can have a clear intention and still struggle with the practical steps.

Research has found average differences in executive-function performance among autistic people and adults with ADHD. A large analysis of autism studies reported a moderate average difference between autistic and comparison groups. But there was substantial variation between individuals, and relatively few laboratory measures could reliably distinguish one person from another (Demetriou et al., 2018).

Reviews of adult ADHD likewise describe difficulties with attention, working memory, planning, and organization, while emphasizing that individual performance varies and cognitive tests do not establish an ADHD diagnosis on their own (Cortese et al., 2025).

These differences are not a ranking of intelligence, kindness, maturity, or romantic potential. Nor do they mean every autistic person or every person with ADHD will struggle with the same things.

Researchers also caution against treating autism and ADHD as two neatly separate sets of executive-function problems. Many studies have not adequately accounted for people who have both, and much of the evidence comes from research involving children (Kofler et al., 2024).

You might be excellent at planning a complicated work project but find it difficult to arrange dinner. You might remember obscure details from a conversation but forget to answer a message. Or you might have no particular difficulty with any of these things.

The useful question isn’t “What’s wrong with my brain?” It’s “Which step is difficult for me?”

There is another important distinction: an explanation is not the same as an excuse.

If you forget a date, that doesn’t prove you don’t care. But caring doesn’t erase the other person’s wasted evening.

You can acknowledge both truths without deciding that either person is the problem.

Find the step that actually causes trouble

Imagine someone tells you, “You need to be more organized.”

That instruction isn’t very helpful unless you know what part of organization is difficult.

Perhaps you’re good at choosing plans but bad at remembering them. Perhaps you remember everything but can’t get started. Perhaps your plans are reasonable until you underestimate how long each step takes.

Different difficulties call for different supports.

What happens The difficult step Something to try
You keep thinking about asking someone to meet but never settle on a plan. Choosing Reduce the options to two possible days.
You agree to a date but forget to put it in your schedule. Remembering Enter it in your calendar during the conversation.
You know you need to get ready but can’t begin. Starting Use a small first action, such as putting your shoes by the door.
You become absorbed in an activity and leave too late. Switching Set an alarm for when you must stop the activity.
Everything takes longer than you expected. Estimating time Work backward from arrival and add preparation time.
You read a message and intend to respond later, then forget. Returning to a task Save a reminder or keep the message marked for a reply.
You make plans easily but feel drained when the day arrives. Available energy Make smaller commitments and leave room for rest.

These are options to experiment with, not scientifically established treatments for dating problems.

You don’t need to adopt every suggestion. In fact, building an elaborate planning system can become another task you struggle to maintain.

Start with the difficulty you recognize most clearly.

When time doesn’t behave the way you expect

Some people can look at a clock and know they have fifteen minutes before they must leave, yet that knowledge doesn’t reliably guide their behavior.

Others can estimate how long a movie lasts but have no idea whether getting dressed will take ten minutes or forty.

You might hear these experiences called time blindness.

It’s an informal term for difficulties noticing the passage of time, estimating durations, or keeping future deadlines in mind. It is often associated with ADHD, but it isn’t a separate diagnosis.

The research deserves careful wording.

A 2023 review of adult ADHD found that studies of time perception were relatively few and produced mixed results (Mette, 2023). A meta-analysis involving children and adolescents found differences in the accuracy and consistency of experimental timing performance (Zheng et al., 2022).

A later systematic review and meta-analysis found an overall difference in time perception between ADHD and comparison groups across the lifespan, with age influencing the results. It did not establish a single adult pattern that explains everyday lateness (Metcalfe et al., 2024).

In other words, differences in time perception are supported by research, but the popular idea that everyone with ADHD inevitably loses track of time is too simple.

It also helps to distinguish three questions:

What time is it?

You might need a visible clock or a reminder to check.

How long has this activity been going on?

You might need a timer that tells you when thirty minutes have passed.

How long will the next activity take?

You might need to estimate the separate steps, rather than guessing the total.

These are related problems, but they aren’t identical.

Plan backward from the moment that matters

Suppose you’re meeting someone at 7:00 p.m.

You know the drive usually takes twenty-five minutes. That doesn’t mean you can start getting ready at 6:30.

Try working backward.

Task Time allowed
Arrive at the restaurant 7:00 p.m.
Parking, walking in, and a small buffer 10 minutes
Travel 25 minutes
Getting ready and gathering belongings 15 minutes
Begin getting ready 6:10 p.m.

The important deadline is not 7:00. It’s 6:10.

Put the preparation time in your calendar, not just the date itself.

You can also set two different alarms: one for beginning to get ready and another for leaving. Give each alarm a specific label. “Leave for restaurant” is more useful than a notification that simply says “Reminder.”

If unexpected delays regularly occur, make the buffer larger. Use your actual experience to revise your estimates.

If time disappears when you're absorbed

You may look up from something interesting and discover that far more time has passed than you expected. You may also feel certain that a task will take five minutes because it feels small, even when previous attempts took much longer.

Rather than relying on your internal sense of time, try making time visible. Use a clock you can see while working, reminders that identify the next action, and calendar entries for preparation rather than only arrival. Choose cues you will actually notice.

If an alarm repeatedly interrupts something important, adjust the system. The point isn’t to surround yourself with notifications. It’s to give yourself enough information to make a choice before you’re late.

What if you’re already running late?

Tell the other person as soon as you reasonably can.

A useful message is specific:

“Getting ready took longer than I planned. I’m sorry. My new arrival estimate is 7:20. Does that still work for you?”

Don’t claim you’re five minutes away when you haven’t left the house. A reassuring estimate that isn’t true leaves the other person unable to make an informed decision.

They may decide to wait. They may prefer to reschedule. They may not want to continue dating someone whose schedule frequently changes.

Their choice matters.

If lateness happens repeatedly, apologizing each time without changing anything is unlikely to address the practical problem. You need a more realistic plan, not a more elaborate apology.

When starting or stopping feels impossible

There is another experience that doesn’t fit neatly into ordinary forgetfulness.

You know you need to do something. You understand why it matters. You might even want to do it.

But you cannot seem to start.

Or you’re already doing something and cannot stop, even though you want to move on.

Or changing from one activity to another feels much harder than either activity by itself.

Some autistic people describe this as autistic inertia, difficulty starting, stopping, or switching actions that can occur despite a conscious intention to act.

In a qualitative study led by autistic researchers, 32 autistic adults described these experiences in focus groups. Participants reported that external prompts, helpful environments, and small starting actions sometimes made transitions easier. Stress and poor mental health could make them harder. The researchers did not establish one explanation for the experience or a treatment that works for everyone (Buckle et al., 2021).

The word inertia describes what the experience feels like. It doesn’t tell you exactly what causes it.

It also doesn’t mean every instance of procrastination is autistic inertia.

Consider two different situations:

In one, you keep putting off a phone call because you don’t want an uncomfortable conversation.

In another, you have decided to call, the conversation is welcome, and you still cannot move from your current activity to picking up the phone.

They may look identical from the outside, but what would help could be quite different.

Make transitions smaller

Instead of treating “get ready for the date” as one enormous action, separate it into steps:

  1. Stop the current activity.
  2. Put away what you’re using.
  3. Stand up.
  4. Go to the room where you’ll get ready.
  5. Begin with one familiar task.

You might discover that the hardest step is the first one. Or perhaps changing rooms is easy, but beginning to dress is difficult.

An external cue can sometimes help. That might be a timer, a written checklist, a particular piece of music, or an agreed reminder from someone who has volunteered to help.

The reminder must be welcome. Another person shouldn’t be expected to interrupt you, supervise you, or physically move you without your agreement.

If changing activities can stop you in place

A plan can be enjoyable and still require a difficult transition. You may find it easier to prepare well before you need to leave, keep a familiar getting-ready sequence, or avoid scheduling a date immediately after an absorbing activity.

Notice whether you find it hardest to start, stop, or switch. Each may need a different arrangement. If a reminder helps, choose one that doesn’t add unnecessary sensory demands or pressure.

You can explain the practical need without giving someone your entire history: “Switching activities takes me a while, so I build extra time into plans. I’d rather choose a time I can reliably make.”

When both people have similar difficulties

Rowan and Malik are both autistic. Rowan finds it difficult to stop working on a detailed project. Malik finds it difficult to begin getting ready until the last possible moment.

They like spending time together, but their first few plans involve considerable waiting.

At first, Rowan assumes Malik should understand the problem because they share a diagnosis. Malik assumes Rowan will naturally know when to leave.

Neither assumption helps.

Eventually, they discuss the particular steps that cause trouble.

For their next outing, they agree on a place and time the day before. Each puts the plan in their own calendar. Rowan schedules time to stop working. Malik lays out clothes earlier in the afternoon.

They don’t need to decide who has the more legitimate difficulty.

They need a plan that works for the two people actually making it.

Sharing a neurotype doesn’t mean sharing identical abilities, preferences, or needs.

The message you meant to answer

A message arrives while you’re working.

“Saturday afternoon sounds good. Want to meet at two?”

You read it. You like the idea. You intend to answer after finishing your current task.

Then someone calls. You finish work. You make dinner. Two days pass.

When you finally notice the conversation again, you feel embarrassed. You may wonder whether replying now will make the situation worse.

From your point of view, the message has been waiting in a mental category labeled answer later.

From the other person’s point of view, they’ve received no answer.

This is a place where intentions and observable behavior can become especially different.

The research reviewed for this chapter supports difficulties with working memory, future intentions, and task initiation in some people, but it does not establish how often ADHD or autism causes missed dating messages. There is no evidence here for a typical ADHD reply time or a rule that unanswered messages indicate executive-function difficulty (Buckle et al., 2021; Cortese et al., 2025; Gilbert et al., 2023).

Someone might not answer because they forgot. They might be busy, undecided, unwell, uninterested, or unwilling to continue the conversation.

You cannot determine the reason from silence alone.

Make replying a task you can find again

If you regularly forget to respond, experiment with a simple system.

Perhaps you leave messages marked as unread until you’ve answered. Perhaps you put a reminder on your calendar to check personal messages after dinner. Perhaps you make a habit of answering scheduling questions immediately when you have the information.

The system should fit your life. You don’t need to make yourself permanently reachable.

When you haven’t decided what you can commit to, you can acknowledge the message without pretending you’ve made a decision:

“I’d like to meet. I need to check my schedule before confirming. I’ll get back to you tomorrow evening.”

That creates a new commitment, so record it somewhere you can find it.

If you know you won’t be able to answer by tomorrow evening, choose a more realistic time instead.

For messages that establish actual plans, be particularly clear. An invitation is not automatically an agreement.

“Yes, Saturday at two works. See you at the café.”

This confirms something both people can put on their calendars.

A long conversation about interests can usually tolerate a different pace from a message asking whether someone should reserve tickets for a particular day.

You can discuss those differences instead of expecting the other person to guess.

What if someone else stops replying?

Don’t diagnose their silence.

You can make a reasonable attempt to clarify an unfinished plan, provided they haven’t asked you to stop contacting them. If you still receive no answer, don’t treat the plan as confirmed.

If the person has declined, blocked you, or asked for no further contact, respect that. Their silence is not permission to try another route.

Later chapters cover texting, interest, and communication in more detail. For now, the useful principle is that your own communication habits are something you can explain and improve, while someone else’s participation remains their choice.

Energy isn’t the same as organization

You can have an excellent calendar and still be too exhausted to enjoy the date you’ve scheduled.

You can also have plenty of physical energy and still struggle to begin a simple task.

Energy is about what you have available. Executive function is about organizing and carrying out actions. They can affect each other, but they’re not interchangeable.

Suppose you can comfortably meet a friend for coffee on a quiet Sunday morning. The same café visit on Friday evening, after a demanding workweek, may feel much harder.

The location and activity haven’t changed. Your available energy has.

Chapter 3 discussed sensory needs. Chapter 4 addressed feelings that may become clear only afterward. Chapter 5 explored the cost of masking and burnout.

Those experiences matter when you decide how much dating activity to schedule.

There may be additional influences. In the autistic inertia study, participants described stress and poor mental health as making some action difficulties worse (Buckle et al., 2021).

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis also reported average differences in aspects of attention and executive functioning among groups defined by trauma exposure or posttraumatic stress disorder. Those findings cannot establish that a particular person’s difficulties were caused by trauma or explain why they missed a particular commitment (Velotti et al., 2026).

You don’t need to decide which explanation applies before you can notice that some days require more recovery than others.

Give your energy a practical budget

Some disability and chronic-illness communities use the metaphor of spoons to describe limited daily energy. It can be a useful personal way of talking about capacity, but a fixed number of daily spoons is not a scientifically validated measurement.

You don’t need to count anything.

Instead, ask yourself:

  • How much energy does getting ready usually require?
  • Is the activity itself demanding, or is most of the effort in preparing and traveling?
  • Do you need quiet time before or afterward?
  • How many social commitments can you enjoy in one week without pushing past your limits?

The answers may change depending on work, sleep, stress, health, and what else is happening in your life.

Avoid turning the answers into permanent rules about what you’re capable of.

Consider Elena, who is fifty-four and getting to know a woman she met through friends. Elena enjoys their conversations, but her workday ends at six and she finds evening plans exhausting.

She initially agrees to dinner after work because she doesn’t want to seem uninterested. Twice, she arrives exhausted and has difficulty following the conversation.

Eventually, she suggests a different arrangement:

“I enjoy seeing you, but weeknights leave me with very little energy. Would you be interested in meeting Saturday afternoon instead?”

Her date may like that plan. She may prefer evenings. They can find out whether their schedules work together.

Elena has communicated a practical need without presenting herself as incapable or making the other person responsible for fixing it.

And if their schedules simply don’t fit, neither person has to be wrong.

Make commitments based on ordinary days

It’s tempting to imagine your future self having more time, more enthusiasm, and fewer interruptions than your present self.

You might agree to three dates in a week because you’re excited about someone. Then the week arrives, work becomes demanding, and every evening feels like another obligation.

Instead, plan around the amount of energy you usually have.

If you generally need Sunday to recover from a busy Saturday, leave that time available.

If you find one social outing per week enjoyable but three exhausting, begin with one.

This isn’t about offering someone the smallest possible amount of effort. It’s about offering effort you can actually sustain.

The next two chapters explore what you want from dating and whether your current circumstances support it. Those questions are different from executive function, but knowing your energy patterns will help you answer them.

Use tools instead of depending on memory alone

A reminder isn’t proof that your memory is defective. It’s a way of placing information somewhere you can retrieve it when you need it.

Researchers call this intention offloading: using an external tool, such as a note or reminder, to help you carry out something later.

A review of experimental research found that external reminders can support delayed intentions. One later experiment found improved performance when people used reminders for tasks requiring actions after short delays (Gilbert et al., 2023; Tsai & Gilbert, 2025).

These were cognitive experiments, not tests of dating routines or treatments for ADHD. They support the general usefulness of reminders, not any particular relationship system.

The practical question is whether a tool helps you follow through.

A simple dating system

You might use a single calendar with an entry for each confirmed plan.

Every entry contains:

  • What: coffee with Jordan
  • Where: the agreed café, with the address
  • When: Saturday, 2:00 p.m.
  • Preparation: begin getting ready at 1:00
  • Departure: leave at 1:25
  • Anything to remember: bring the book you offered to lend

You can keep this in a phone, a paper planner, or another tool you use regularly.

If a plan changes, update it immediately.

If the other person proposes changing the time, don’t rely on remembering the new arrangement after a distracting conversation.

When useful, confirm the agreed plan in a message so both people can refer to the same information.

For instance:

“Saturday at two, at the café on Main Street. Confirmed.”

This isn’t a contract. Either person can still ask to change plans. It’s simply a shared record of what was agreed.

Don’t create more reminders than you can use

Someone who already ignores a hundred phone notifications may not benefit from adding twenty more.

Try starting with one calendar and one or two reminders tied to meaningful actions.

Then ask whether they worked.

Did you see the reminder? Did you understand what it wanted you to do? Did it arrive early enough? Were you able to act when it appeared?

If the answer is no, change the system.

A reminder that repeatedly fails isn’t useful simply because it’s a reminder.

If you require another person to interrupt you, that needs to be a voluntary agreement, not an unspoken obligation.

Your partner is not your calendar

When one person struggles with planning or remembering, another person may gradually become responsible for both people’s schedules.

At first, this might happen without much discussion.

One person remembers birthdays, confirms reservations, checks travel times, and sends reminders. The other gets used to relying on them.

Over time, the arrangement may stop feeling voluntary.

A review of research on adult ADHD and relationships describes average associations with greater conflict and relationship instability, while emphasizing how little is known about the mechanisms or which interventions help (Wymbs et al., 2021).

In an Irish qualitative study, adults with ADHD described relationship experiences that included difficulties with distraction, unequal organizing responsibilities, and feeling like a burden. Some also described self-understanding as helpful. Importantly, participants were specifically asked about negative effects, so the study cannot tell us how common these experiences are across relationships or how often relationships work well (O’Brien et al., 2026).

There are two mistakes to avoid.

The first is treating someone with executive-function difficulties as if they deliberately choose every missed task.

The second is treating those difficulties as a reason someone else must take over all the planning.

Neither approach creates a fair arrangement.

Separate helping from becoming responsible

Suppose Casey often forgets plans. Their partner, Priya, is good at keeping track of a calendar.

Priya might enjoy choosing activities and making reservations. Casey might enjoy planning routes, arranging transportation, or preparing meals.

They could divide responsibilities according to preference and ability.

But “Priya is better at remembering” doesn’t automatically mean “Priya must remind Casey of everything.”

Each person still needs to agree to their responsibilities and find workable ways to carry them out.

Support can also change over time. Something that feels easy for one person during a quiet month might become difficult during a demanding period.

Check whether an arrangement is still working rather than assuming that the first division of tasks must last forever.

If the person you're dating forgets plans or messages

You may experience a forgotten commitment as a sign that you aren’t important. The other person may describe genuinely losing track of the task. Neither perspective needs to be dismissed. Rather than trying to decide what the mistake secretly means, discuss what happened, how it affected you, and what would make future plans more reliable.

You can offer a reminder or share a calendar if you want to. Ask whether that would help, and be clear about what you’re comfortable doing. You are not required to supervise another adult’s daily life, and a diagnosis doesn’t mean you must accept repeated broken agreements. What matters is whether both of you can find an arrangement that respects your needs.

Your explanation matters. So does your response.

If you repeatedly forget plans, saying “That’s just how my brain works” may explain part of the difficulty.

It doesn’t tell the other person what will happen next.

A more useful conversation might sound like this:

“I missed our call, and I understand why you’re frustrated. I had the time in my messages but didn’t put it in my calendar. I’m sorry. Next time, I’ll add it when we agree on it.”

The important part isn’t promising that you will never make another mistake.

It’s identifying something specific that you can change.

If the system doesn’t work, you can revisit it. If the same problem continues despite repeated attempts, the other person is allowed to decide whether the arrangement meets their needs.

Likewise, if you’re the person frequently left waiting, you can acknowledge someone’s difficulty without pretending the impact is acceptable to you.

“I understand that remembering plans is hard. I still need to know whether we’re meeting. If we can’t confirm by Friday evening, I’ll make other plans.”

That’s a limit on how you’ll use your own time, not a demand that the other person change their brain.

Both people have choices.

When support becomes pressure

It can be tempting to try to compensate for difficulty by demanding more of yourself.

You might promise instant replies, agree to dates on every available evening, or set dozens of alerts because you’re afraid of disappointing someone.

But a system that requires you to operate at your maximum capacity every day may not be a realistic one.

The opposite extreme can also cause problems: assuming that because a task is difficult, there is no point trying to improve your follow-through.

You don’t need perfect organization to date.

You do need enough honesty to understand your commitments and enough respect to recognize when your difficulties affect another person.

Sometimes that means making fewer plans. Sometimes it means using a calendar. Sometimes it means having a direct conversation about what each person can reasonably expect.

If executive-function difficulties regularly interfere with work, relationships, basic responsibilities, or daily life, a qualified healthcare professional can help you explore possible causes and support options. The same behavior can have different explanations, and you don’t need to diagnose yourself to ask for help.

Difficulty with a task deserves curiosity. Repeated harm to another person still deserves attention.

A small experiment: your follow-through map

You don’t need a new relationship to try this. Use an upcoming plan with a friend, family member, or someone you’re dating.

Choose one activity that has a definite time and place.

Before it happens, write down three things:

1. What part might be difficult?

Choose one: deciding, remembering, beginning, switching, estimating time, replying, or having enough energy.

Be specific.

“Remembering that I’m meeting Alex at noon” is more useful than “I’m bad at organizing things.”

2. What single support will you try?

Examples:

  • Put the commitment in your calendar immediately.
  • Set an alarm for when you need to begin preparing.
  • Allow an extra fifteen minutes for an uncertain task.
  • Prepare your belongings the previous evening.
  • Set aside a particular time to answer a message.
  • Choose a quieter or less demanding plan.

Pick one. Don’t redesign your entire life.

3. What happened?

Afterward, ask yourself:

Did you remember? Did you start on time? Was the task easier? Was the support annoying or useful? Did you need more recovery afterward than expected?

If the support didn’t help, try to find out why.

Perhaps the alarm arrived too late. Perhaps you remembered the appointment but still couldn’t stop your previous activity. Perhaps you had the time and organization, but not the energy.

That information tells you what to adjust next.

One sentence worth practicing

Complete this sentence in your own words:

“The part of making plans that’s hardest for me is ________, so something that helps is ________.”

For example:

“The part of making plans that’s hardest for me is estimating travel time, so something that helps is deciding when to leave instead of just when to arrive.”

Or:

“The part that’s hardest for me is answering messages after I’ve read them, so I set aside a time to check anything I haven’t answered.”

Or:

“The part that’s hardest for me is switching from work to social plans, so I prefer having some time between finishing work and meeting.”

You can tell another person what helps without disclosing a diagnosis, defending your entire history, or asking permission to have a need.

And if you’re dating someone who experiences these difficulties, you can ask a similarly useful question:

“Which part is difficult, and what would help us make a plan that works for both of us?”

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book. One person sees an intention that didn’t become an action. The other sees a commitment that wasn’t kept. Understanding both views gives you more useful information than deciding whose experience is real.

Know what you can offer

Dating doesn’t require a perfectly managed life.

You can be someone who needs reminders, prefers predictable schedules, takes longer to change activities, or needs quiet time after socializing. You can also be someone who loves spontaneity, remembers every detail, and rarely thinks about a calendar.

Neither arrangement automatically makes you a good or bad partner.

What matters is knowing what you can reasonably offer and recognizing that the person you’re dating has needs too.

You don’t have to promise constant availability to show interest. You don’t have to pretend you can manage a schedule that doesn’t work for you. And you don’t have to excuse every missed commitment simply because it wasn’t intentional.

You can learn how you function, choose supports that help, and make plans that leave both people free to decide whether the arrangement suits them.

So far, this part of the book has focused on understanding yourself: your past experiences, sensory needs, feelings, masking, energy, and ability to follow through.

There’s one more personal question to answer before you start looking for someone to date.

What do you actually want from a relationship?

Being able to make a date happen is useful. Knowing why you want to make one happen is just as important.
