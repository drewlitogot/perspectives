Priya likes Mel. They’ve been dating for three weeks, and Mel suggests spending Saturday at a street festival.

“That sounds great,” Priya says.

Half an hour later, Priya thinks about what she’s agreed to. The festival will be crowded and noisy. Mel wants to spend the afternoon there, but Priya knows from experience that two hours would be about her limit.

She wants to see Mel. She doesn’t want to spend six hours somewhere exhausting.

Now she has a problem. She already said yes. Changing her answer feels awkward. What if Mel thinks she’s losing interest?

Priya sends a message:

“I said yes before I thought about how long I’d be there. I’d love to go with you, but I can only manage about two hours. Could we go from 1 to 3?”

Mel replies:

“Sure. Two hours works for me.”

That’s it. No argument, no elaborate explanation, no ruined date.

But Priya did something worth learning. She noticed a limit, put it into words, and gave Mel information she didn’t have before.

Chapter 26 defined a boundary as a limit on what you will do or accept. A request asks another person to do something, and they can say no. Chapter 27 helped you find your boundaries. Now comes the practical part: communicating them clearly enough that another person can understand.

Why say it instead of hoping they’ll notice?

You might think your discomfort should be obvious.

Maybe you look at the exit or become quiet, hoping your date notices. They might not.

A review of autistic adult communication describes how some autistic people find implied meaning harder to interpret, while reading body language can require considerable effort. The review also emphasizes that communication difficulties can arise from how other people respond, not simply from one person’s differences (Marchena et al., 2025).

Chapter 19 introduced the double empathy problem, the idea that misunderstandings between people with different ways of experiencing the world go both ways (Milton, 2012). Chapter 20 explored direct and indirect communication. Here, the practical lesson is to describe your limits rather than relying on someone to guess them.

As in the sculpture-and-glasses picture, your date can’t see the situation from your position until you describe it.

In one qualitative study involving 31 autistic and 26 non-autistic respondents, both groups valued open communication in romantic intimacy, and autistic respondents particularly emphasized clarity (Sala et al., 2020). Another study interviewed 16 gender- and sexuality-diverse autistic adults in the UK. Participants described explaining their boundaries and developing individual relationship agreements. Some reported satisfying relationships with non-autistic partners who made an effort to understand them (Ciric et al., 2026).

You don’t need a diagnosis, or even to know why something is difficult for you, to communicate a boundary.

“Loud restaurants are hard for me” gives a date useful information. Whether the reason is autism, ADHD, anxiety, a past experience, or something you haven’t figured out doesn’t change your right to choose a quieter setting.

If you're not sure what's going on for you

You might recognize several explanations for your difficulties, or have been given a label that never seemed to fit. You can use the examples in this chapter without mentioning any diagnosis.

Try the approaches that seem useful and notice which ones help. Knowing what you need can come before knowing why you need it.

The shape of a boundary sentence

A boundary doesn’t have to sound formal, clever, or particularly confident.

Imagine you’re tired and someone asks you to stay another hour. You could explain your entire day.

Or you could say:

“I’ve had a good time, but I need to head home now.”

That communicates the important part.

Start with your limit

A useful sentence can have two pieces: what you need or won’t do + what you’ll do about it.

“I don’t answer casual messages after 10 p.m. I’ll reply tomorrow.”

“I need quiet time after work. I’ll call you when I’m ready.”

“I’m not comfortable staying overnight yet. I’m going home after dinner.”

“I don’t share passwords with partners. I’ll keep my accounts private.”

You can add a request when you want to explore an alternative:

“Loud places wear me out. Could we pick somewhere quieter? Otherwise, I’ll have to skip this one.”

Sometimes the complete boundary is “No, thanks,” “Not yet,” or “I need to stop.” No explanation is required first.

Only use example sentences that are true for you. Change the wording until it matches what you mean. A script is something to adapt, not an excuse to invent.

Preference, request, or boundary?

These ideas are related, but they do different jobs.

Type What it means Example
Preference Something you’d like “I prefer quieter restaurants.”
Request Something you’re asking another person to do “Could we eat somewhere quieter?”
Boundary A limit on your own participation “I can’t stay somewhere that loud. I’ll need to leave if it’s too noisy.”

A preference might become a request. A request might lead you to explain a boundary. But a boundary isn’t a way to force someone to accept your request.

If you want dinner at six and your date prefers eight, you can ask to meet earlier. They can decline, and you can decide whether eight works for you.

The same distinction matters in more serious situations.

Boundary about your actions Attempt to control someone else
“I don’t reply to messages while I’m working.” “You’re not allowed to text anyone else while I’m working.”
“I’m not ready to meet your friends yet.” “You can’t see your friends without me.”
“If I’m being shouted at, I’ll stop the conversation.” “You must agree with me or I’ll punish you.”

“I won’t stay in a relationship where I’m regularly shouted at” can affect another person’s choices, but it’s still a decision about your own participation.

A supposed boundary that restricts someone’s friendships, clothing, independence, or movements deserves closer examination. Such restrictions can be part of controlling behavior (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.). If you recognize yourself imposing them, stop. Identify the concern underneath them, take responsibility for harm, and seek support if controlling behavior is becoming a pattern. Chapter 36 addresses this in more detail.

Direct doesn’t mean unkind

Compare these two statements:

“You never give me any space!”

“I need two evenings a week to myself. I’d like us to plan around that.”

Research on interpersonal assertiveness, meaning expressing your needs while respecting other people’s rights, suggests that being more forceful is not automatically better. How people respond depends partly on the situation and their relationship (Ames et al., 2017). That research isn’t a test of dating boundary scripts.

You can be kind without making your limit negotiable. You don’t have to deliver it perfectly for it to count.

“I don’t know yet” is a valid answer

Sometimes you can’t identify your limits in advance.

You might never have tried the activity, or you’re still learning to recognize what you need.

You can say:

“I’m still figuring out what I’m comfortable with. I may need to change plans if something doesn’t feel right.”

“I don’t know how I’ll feel about that yet. Can I decide later?”

“I’m not sure. I don’t want to agree to it right now.”

You don’t owe someone an immediate yes just because they asked a question.

If predicting your limits is difficult, start with previous experiences. If you felt exhausted after a three-hour gathering last weekend, plan for two hours next time. If you felt uncomfortable discussing a personal subject on your last date, decide not to discuss it on the next one.

Your boundaries can change as you learn more. Updating one isn’t dishonesty.

Before, during, or after

There are three useful times to communicate a boundary. All of them count.

Before: give yourself time to think

If you already know a situation could be difficult, communicating beforehand makes planning easier.

Sam, 27, is autistic. He’s preparing for a first date with Jordan, who isn’t autistic. Jordan suggested a restaurant with loud music. Sam knows he’ll struggle to follow a conversation there.

He texts the day before:

“Looking forward to Friday! Could we pick somewhere quieter? I have trouble following conversations when there’s loud music. I’ll probably head home around 9, too.”

Jordan suggests a smaller café. If she couldn’t accommodate the request, they could consider another plan.

If you lose track of time

If you often become absorbed in an activity or conversation, a departure-time boundary is easier to keep with an external reminder. Set a phone alarm or vibrating watch reminder before the date. You can explain why you’ve set it if you want to.

If you promise to answer a question later, set a reminder for that too.

During: say something when you notice

Sometimes you don’t discover a limit until you’re already uncomfortable. Perhaps dinner was pleasant, but afterward your date suggests going somewhere crowded. You realize you need quiet.

Overload means more is coming in, such as noise, light, people, questions, or feelings, than you can handle at once. You don’t have to wait until you can no longer manage.

“I need ten minutes somewhere quieter.”

If a conversation becomes too personal:

“I’d rather not talk about my family tonight.”

If an interaction is moving faster than you want:

“I want to slow down. I’m not ready for this.”

If you need something to stop immediately, communicate that as clearly as you can or move away if possible. You don’t need to finish an explanation first.

After: you recognized your discomfort late

You might go home from a date and only the next morning realize that part of the conversation felt uncomfortable. Or you might recognize that you accepted another invitation because you felt put on the spot, not because you’d decided you wanted to go.

You can return to the subject afterward.

“I’ve been thinking about yesterday. I wasn’t comfortable talking about my past relationship. I’d like to leave that topic alone for now.”

“When you invited me to meet your parents, I said yes before I’d thought about it. I’m not ready yet.”

If changing your answer affects plans, tell the person as soon as you reasonably can. Acknowledging inconvenience doesn’t invalidate your limit.

If you recognize your limits afterward

You might find it easier to identify what you need by looking back at what happened than by predicting your response beforehand.

Try an if-then plan: “If the noise becomes too much for me, I’ll step outside for ten minutes.” You can also explain that you need longer to answer important questions: “I sometimes need until the next day to think about big decisions.”

If you tend to say yes too quickly

Priya’s festival invitation illustrates another difficulty: agreeing before you’ve had enough time to decide.

Maybe excitement makes you answer immediately. Maybe disappointing someone feels difficult. Maybe you learned in an earlier relationship that refusing wasn’t safe. Some people with ADHD, past trauma, or a strong habit of pleasing others may recognize these experiences, but the supplied research doesn’t establish a single explanation.

You don’t have to know the cause before changing how you respond.

“Let me think about it. I’ll tell you tomorrow.”

Set a reminder, and answer when you said you would.

You don’t have to speak

Speaking face to face is one way to express a boundary. It isn’t the only way.

In a UK study of 245 autistic adults, participants described advantages of written communication, including time to organize thoughts, find words, and review what they’d said. Phone calls were widely disliked, while face-to-face conversations were often easier with familiar, accepting people (Howard & Sedgewick, 2021). You might use speech when you’re comfortable talking; texting when you need thinking time; written notes when you want to prepare your words; or communication aids, such as a phone screen, card, communication app, or gesture, when speech is difficult. Sometimes leaving or stepping away is more important than finding a sentence.

A typed boundary isn’t less real than a spoken one.

When your partner communicates differently

Dev and Marcus are two autistic men in their early fifties. They’ve been dating for several months.

Dev likes discussing problems immediately. Marcus needs time to think privately before answering. When Marcus asks to pause a discussion, Dev worries Marcus doesn’t want to talk about it at all. When Dev asks several questions in a row, Marcus feels pressured to answer before he’s ready.

They discuss the difference on a relaxed afternoon.

Marcus explains:

“When I’m trying to answer something important, I need more time. If I say I want to think about it, I’m not ignoring you. Can we come back to it the next day?”

Dev replies:

“That works, but I need to know when we’ll talk again. Otherwise I keep wondering.”

They agree on a pause and a time to continue.

Sharing a neurotype doesn’t guarantee identical preferences. Nor does having different neurotypes prevent satisfying relationships (Ciric et al., 2026).

When the words disappear

There may be moments when you know something is wrong but can’t explain it. Your mind goes blank. Your voice becomes quiet. You can answer simple questions but cannot form a longer sentence.

One possibility is a shutdown, when your brain and body pull inward and slow down or stop, often after overload. Speech may become very difficult.

In a 2025 qualitative analysis of autistic adults’ accounts, some participants described becoming frozen, withdrawing inward, or losing access to speech. Some could manage only short answers. Excessive input, feeling unsafe, and masking, which means hiding or suppressing natural traits to fit in, were associated with shutdown experiences (Paris et al., 2025).

A 2026 review of 18 studies described communication changes during distress and possible supports such as communication aids, reduced demands, and calmer environments. Only five of those studies focused on adults, so these findings aren’t a tested approach to dating situations (Oldenburg et al., 2026).

You don’t need to determine why you go quiet before preparing alternatives.

Start with two fallback phrases

If this feels like a lot, begin with just two sentences:

“I need a break.”

“I can’t answer right now.”

Save them on your phone and practice saying or showing them when you’re comfortable.

If you can still speak a little

Short phrases are enough:

“Stop, please.”

“Too loud. Need quiet.”

“I need to go.”

If you can’t speak

Prepare a message you can show or send:

“I can’t talk right now. I need space. I’ll message when I can.”

Or:

“I need to leave. Please don’t touch me.”

Keep it on your phone, on a small card, or in a communication app. With someone you know and trust, you could agree on a gesture that means you need a break. Agree on its meaning in advance; someone who hasn’t learned the signal cannot be expected to understand it.

Leaving safely

Knowing how you’ll leave before you need to can help. Have access to your own transportation, transit fare, or a trusted ride. If speaking or moving becomes difficult during a shutdown, consider how to leave before reaching that point. The shutdown study identified difficulty speaking or moving as a possible barrier to leaving harmful situations (Paris et al., 2025).

If it’s safe and possible, give a brief explanation as you go:

“I need air. I’ll explain later.”

If you leave unexpectedly and want to reassure your date, a short later message can be enough:

“I had to leave because I needed quiet. I’m home now. I’ll talk tomorrow.”

Only send that message if you’re safe and able, and don’t promise a time unless it’s realistic. You aren’t obligated to reassure someone who frightened or harmed you.

Safety matters more than politeness. If someone is threatening or controlling, announcing your plans or confronting them may put you at greater risk. Prioritize safety and seek confidential support in choosing what to do next. US relationship-violence guidance recommends safety planning suited to the person’s circumstances, not one required response for everyone (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, n.d.). Chapters 36 and 59–60 cover recognition and support.

If your words speed up under stress

Some people go quiet when upset. Others speak much faster, offer many explanations, or say more than they intended.

If you notice this, try: “Give me a minute to get my thoughts together.” If your words came out more sharply than you wanted, you can apologize for the wording later without withdrawing the boundary itself.

What happens after you say it?

Responses can range from acceptance to pressure. The difference matters.

They accept it

You say you need to leave by nine. They say, “Okay.”

“Thanks” is enough.

They ask questions

Suppose you say you need to spend Sunday alone. Your date asks whether you mean every Sunday or just this weekend. That’s a reasonable question. They may be trying to plan time together.

Asking clarifying questions can be a normal part of autistic communication, although non-autistic people sometimes interpret them as challenges (Marchena et al., 2025).

You can answer if you want to:

“Usually Sundays. I need time to prepare for the workweek.”

If you don’t know:

“I’m still figuring that out. I’ll tell you when I know.”

If the reason is private:

“It’s personal, and I’d rather not explain right now.”

Questions can seek understanding. Demands that you prove a limit is legitimate are different, and you needn’t keep supplying reasons.

They want something different

Not every disagreement is pushback. Imagine you want to see someone once a week, but they want to spend several evenings together.

They can say once a week isn’t enough; you can maintain that it’s all you’re comfortable offering. You might find a compromise or discover that your needs aren’t compatible. Neither outcome makes someone wrong.

They push against your boundary

Pushback is different from a genuine question or disagreement. You might notice that someone keeps asking after you’ve declined, mocks your need to leave, dismisses sensory discomfort as trivial, or treats your limit as an argument they intend to win.

You don’t have to convince them that your experience is real. When it’s safe, you can repeat your decision without a longer argument:

“I understand you’d prefer something different. I’m still going home.”

Or:

“I’ve answered that. I don’t want to discuss it anymore.”

If your boundary involves leaving or ending a conversation, follow through when it’s safe. You don’t need to keep repeating the sentence until the other person agrees.

Perspective: Men

If you’ve encountered expectations that you should initiate dates, take the lead physically, or always welcome intimacy, saying you want to slow down may feel uncomfortable.

Someone might question your masculinity or interest when you refuse. That doesn’t make your boundary less important. You can say, “I like you, but I’d rather take things slowly,” or “I don’t want to be physically intimate tonight.” You can also ask to share the work of initiating dates. Your boundaries count regardless of your gender or your partner’s.

Perspective: Non-binary

Your name and pronouns are something you can ask others to use. You might say, “My name is Alex, and I use they/them.” If someone accidentally uses the wrong pronoun and corrects themselves, a brief correction may be enough for you.

Deliberate mockery or repeated refusal is different from a mistake. Your boundary concerns your own participation: “I won’t continue dating someone who deliberately disrespects my name or pronouns.” You can decline questions about your body, history, or steps you’ve taken or haven’t taken. “I don’t discuss that” is enough. If enforcing a boundary could put you at risk, choose the safest response rather than feeling required to confront someone.

You feel guilty or frightened afterward

Sometimes the difficult part comes after the conversation. The other person accepted your boundary, but you keep wondering whether you sounded rude. Or they looked disappointed, and you feel responsible for fixing that disappointment.

You might be tempted to change your decision just to stop feeling uncomfortable. Before doing that, ask yourself:

Did my needs change, or am I afraid of the other person’s reaction?

Someone can be disappointed and still respect your boundary. You can care about their feelings without agreeing to something you don’t want.

A relationship might still end over incompatible needs. That can hurt without anyone being wrong.

If you suspect a genuine misunderstanding, chapter 24 offers ways to clarify it. You can send one clarification if you want to and contact is welcome. If someone has clearly ended contact, respect that decision.

Perspective: Women

If you’ve experienced pressure to be agreeable or soften every refusal, a straightforward boundary may feel harsher to you than it is.

You can be courteous without turning “no” into “maybe.” “No, thank you” is a complete answer. If you want to explain, you can, but you don’t need to provide a reason the other person approves of.

You may recognize yourself in more than one of these gender perspectives, or in none. They describe expectations some people encounter, not rules about how any gender behaves.

If saying no has felt unsafe before

If refusing someone has led to punishment, threats, or anger in earlier relationships, even a small boundary may feel frightening. Sometimes that fear reflects past experiences. Sometimes it is a real warning about the person in front of you.

You can practice small boundaries in relationships where you feel safe, such as choosing a different restaurant or asking for more notice before plans. Notice whether the other person accepts your choice without punishing you afterward. If you’re afraid of their reaction, don’t treat boundary-setting as an exercise you must complete. Chapters 36, 57, and 59 discuss unsafe patterns, past experiences, and finding support.

When a boundary gets crossed

There’s a difference between someone misunderstanding your limit and someone repeatedly disregarding it.

If your partner forgets once that you don’t answer casual messages during work, then adjusts when reminded, it may be a misunderstanding. If they repeatedly expect immediate replies and become angry when you don’t answer, that’s a pattern worth taking seriously.

But you don’t have to wait for a pattern before protecting yourself. A single incident can be serious enough to end a date or relationship, particularly when it involves physical safety, sexual behavior, threats, or intimidation.

US government and specialist domestic-violence guidance identifies controlling behavior, threats, intimidation, pressure, and repeated disregard for someone’s autonomy as possible signs of abuse (National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.; U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.). Not every disagreement, forgotten preference, or disappointing response is abuse.

Someone asking for a different dinner time is not the same as someone threatening you for refusing.

And if someone deliberately crosses a boundary you’ve clearly communicated, that’s their responsibility, not a failure of your wording.

You don’t have to find a more convincing sentence. You also don’t have to announce every limit in advance to deserve respect and safety. Someone’s responsibility not to harm you doesn’t depend on whether you managed to say the perfect words, particularly when you felt unable to speak.

If someone is intimidating or controlling, announcing consequences or attempting a confrontation may be unsafe. Seek confidential support and make a plan suited to your circumstances (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, n.d.). Chapter 36 addresses abuse; chapters 59–60 cover support.

Following through without making threats

A boundary becomes useful when you know what you’ll do with it. That doesn’t mean punishing someone who disagrees. It means choosing an action you can reasonably take yourself.

Time: You said you’d leave at nine. At nine, you say goodbye and go home. If you genuinely want to stay longer, that is also your choice.

Communication: You said you wouldn’t answer nonurgent calls during work. When your date calls, you wait until you’re available.

Conversation: You said you wouldn’t continue a discussion while being shouted at. If shouting starts, you pause the conversation and step away if it’s safe. You can arrange another time to discuss the subject.

Following through also means being careful about what you promise. “I’ll text you tomorrow” creates an expectation. If you aren’t sure you’ll be ready, say “I’ll message when I’m ready to talk” instead.

If you agree to a particular time and can’t meet it, send an update when you can. You can respect your boundary while still being considerate about commitments you’ve made.

Practice one sentence

Choose one boundary from chapter 27. Start with something small that you might need in the coming week: leaving a gathering by a certain time, choosing a quieter venue, keeping a personal topic private, or taking longer to reply to messages.

Spend two minutes on three steps:

  1. Write one sentence. Start with “I need,” “I don’t,” or “I’m going to.” For example: “I don’t answer calls after 10 p.m.”
  2. Choose how you’ll communicate it. Say it aloud once, type it into a draft message, or save it in a note.
  3. Decide what you’ll do next. For example: “If someone calls after 10, I’ll return the call tomorrow.”

That’s enough. You don’t need to begin with your most difficult boundary.

A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials involving 503 participants found that assertiveness training reduced social anxiety on average. Those trials didn’t establish whether practicing specific boundary sentences improves romantic relationships or protects people from unsafe partners (Hede et al., 2026).

Situation Something you could say
Choosing a venue “Could we go somewhere quieter? I can’t manage somewhere that loud.”

Texting pace “I don’t usually reply during work. I’ll get back to you afterward.”
Personal questions “I’m not comfortable discussing that yet.”
Touch “I don’t want a hug. A wave is fine.”
Physical intimacy “I want to stop. I’m not ready to go further.”
Digital privacy “I don’t share passwords with people I date.”
Emotional energy “I can’t have this conversation tonight. Can we talk tomorrow?”
Buying thinking time “I need some time to decide. I’ll tell you tomorrow.”
Not knowing your limits “I’m still figuring out what I’m comfortable with.”

No sentence makes an unsafe person respectful. These words only make your meaning clear.

You are allowed to know what you need. You are allowed to still be figuring it out. You can notice a boundary late, communicate it in writing, or change your answer. You can say a boundary kindly without making it optional.

And you can recognize that the person you’re dating has boundaries of their own.

The next chapter turns the conversation around: what happens when someone else tells you their boundary? What if they say no, not yet, or nothing at all?

A relationship needs room for both people’s limits. Knowing how to express yours is one part of making that room.
