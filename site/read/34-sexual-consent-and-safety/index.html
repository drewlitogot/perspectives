You can be attracted to someone and not want to have sex with them.

You can want sex, but not tonight. You can want one sexual activity but not another. You can want sex only if certain protection is used. You can want to send someone a private photograph without wanting it saved, shown to friends, or shared online.

All of these distinctions matter.

Consent fundamentals, covered in Chapter 30, apply to every form of sexual contact. But knowing the definition of consent and navigating a real sexual situation are different skills. When you’re close to someone, excited, nervous, distracted, or uncertain, it helps to know what to say and what to pay attention to.

Sexual safety has two sides: making sure the other person is freely choosing what happens, and making sure you can freely choose, too.

Neither person has to become an expert at reading minds. Both have to take responsibility for making the situation clear and safe.

Before anything happens

At 38, Devin is dating Luis, who is 41. They’ve been seeing each other for a few weeks, and both want to become more physically intimate.

They have kissed before, but they haven’t had sex together.

One evening, Luis asks, “Would you like to stay over?”

Devin does. But Devin also knows that staying overnight and agreeing to sex are different things.

“I’d like to stay,” Devin says. “I’m comfortable kissing and cuddling. I’m not sure about anything beyond that yet.”

“That’s fine,” Luis answers. “You don’t have to decide tonight.”

Nothing dramatic has happened. Nobody has made a speech about consent. Two people have simply made their expectations clearer.

That conversation leaves room for them to enjoy the evening without Devin wondering whether staying over creates an obligation.

It also gives Luis information he could not reliably have obtained from body language.

Sexual conversations do not need to be elaborate. They do need to be clear.

Before becoming sexually intimate with someone, useful questions include:

  • What do you actually want? What kinds of contact interest you, and what would you rather not do?
  • What are your boundaries? Is anything uncomfortable, painful, too personal, or simply unwanted?
  • What protection will you use? Have you agreed about contraception, barriers, and sexual health?
  • What happens if someone changes their mind? Can either person pause or stop without an argument?
  • What stays private? Are photographs, recordings, or sexual messages off the table?

You do not need to negotiate every possible future situation in one conversation. You can discuss what is relevant now, then ask again when something changes.

Nor does a conversation establish permanent permission. Someone who wanted sex last week might not want it today. Someone who agreed to one activity may not want another.

The important habit is being able to talk before acting.

Researchers reviewing sexual consent among college students found that people’s understanding and communication of consent are more complicated than any single rule about words or behavior can capture. That review, however, largely concerns college populations, not adults of every age or people with different communication needs (Muehlenhard et al., 2016).

For practical purposes, clarity is still a better choice than guessing.

Checking in while you’re together

A conversation before sex is useful, but it cannot predict every feeling that might arise during sex.

Someone may discover that an activity feels uncomfortable. A position may hurt. A particular touch may be unexpectedly unpleasant. A person who wanted to continue a minute ago may no longer want to.

This is why checking in matters.

A check-in is a brief question or pause that makes room for the other person to express what they want.

“Would you like to keep going?”

“Is this still comfortable?”

“Do you want to stop for a while?”

These questions work best when the answer genuinely matters to you. Asking permission while treating refusal as an obstacle defeats the purpose.

A check-in becomes particularly important when an activity changes, when someone seems uncomfortable, or when you are unsure whether the other person wants to continue.

RAINN’s guidance emphasizes that sexual consent is ongoing and that hesitation, silence, and lack of resistance should not be interpreted as permission (RAINN, 2026).

There is an important distinction here: you are not looking for a performance of excitement. You are looking for freely given agreement.

Some people smile, laugh, or speak excitedly when they are comfortable. Others are quiet, serious, or not outwardly expressive. A person can give a clear, genuine yes in a calm voice.

Likewise, physical arousal does not establish consent. The body can respond to stimulation independently of what a person wants (RAINN, 2026).

If you’re uncertain, ask. If you cannot get a clear answer, pause.

A pause is not a failure

Suppose Casey and Morgan are having sex. Morgan becomes quiet and turns away.

Casey isn’t sure what has changed.

Rather than continuing or interpreting Morgan’s reaction, Casey stops.

“Do you want to take a break?”

Morgan nods.

They stop. Later, Morgan explains that a particular sensation was unpleasant and they needed a moment to understand what they were feeling.

Casey’s response does not require knowing the cause. Stopping was appropriate because Casey no longer knew whether Morgan wanted to continue.

This matters because people can have difficulty speaking, identifying discomfort, or responding quickly for many reasons.

Someone might need extra processing time. Someone might experience sensory overload. Another person may become frightened, uncertain, or disconnected from what they are feeling.

You cannot reliably distinguish those experiences just by looking.

When the situation is unclear, stop the sexual activity first. Understanding can come afterward.

Do not turn a pause into an interrogation. Someone who is already struggling to communicate may find repeated questions difficult. Give them time and space, and do not resume without renewed, clear agreement.

If words become difficult

In a 2025 study of autistic adults describing shutdown, participants reported experiences such as being unable to speak, having difficulty moving or responding, and needing time to recover. Some described feeling unsafe or cornered. The researchers analyzed descriptions from 50 adults, so the findings illustrate experiences rather than establishing that everyone experiences shutdown the same way (Paris et al., 2025).

A shutdown is not permission to continue.

Nor should someone have to speak perfectly during an intensely uncomfortable moment to make their boundaries count.

If your words sometimes disappear

If overload sometimes makes speaking difficult, you can discuss this with a partner before sexual activity. You might prefer shorter questions, more time to answer, or an agreed hand signal meaning “pause.”

You may also find it easier to describe your preferences in writing when you are calm.

These are ways to make communication more accessible. They are not obligations placed on you. Your partner still has a responsibility to stop when your agreement is unclear. Silence or an absent signal never becomes automatic permission.

If you're dating someone who needs time to respond

A partner who becomes quiet or stops responding may need more processing time, or may be experiencing overload or shutdown. Do not assume you know which.

Stop sexual activity, reduce demands, and give them room to communicate in whatever way works for them. If they cannot give a clear answer, do not continue.

Later, when they are comfortable, ask whether there is a better way to check in. Both partners deserve a communication approach they understand and can freely use.

An agreed signal can help, but it must never become an excuse to continue whenever someone fails to use it.

The responsibility to respect consent belongs to both people.

Alcohol, cannabis, and other drugs

Alcohol and other substances can complicate sexual decisions.

You might have encountered the idea that someone becomes more willing to have sex after a few drinks. But reduced inhibition and freely chosen agreement are not the same thing.

CDC sexual-health guidance notes that alcohol and drugs can affect decision-making and increase sexual risk-taking (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024b).

A person who is too impaired to understand what is happening or make an informed choice cannot give valid consent. RAINN identifies this as a central sexual-consent principle (RAINN, 2026).

The safest practical rule is straightforward:

If you cannot tell whether someone can understand and freely choose what is happening, do not initiate or continue sexual activity.

Wait until both people can make an informed decision.

This applies whether someone has been drinking alcohol, using cannabis, taking other drugs, or experiencing another condition that makes their ability to decide uncertain.

It also applies when you are the person whose judgment may be affected.

There is no single amount of alcohol that tells you everything about someone’s capacity to consent. US states have different legal rules about intoxication and incapacity. Do not mistake a particular number of drinks, a person’s ability to walk or talk, or the absence of obvious drunkenness for proof that sex is legally or ethically permissible.

For legal questions, RAINN provides state-specific information. A qualified legal professional can explain the law in an individual situation.

Drinking is not the only situation to think about

Consider two people who have been drinking at dinner. They have an enjoyable evening, but later one person seems confused about where they are or what the other person is proposing.

That is not a moment for negotiation about whether they are “drunk enough” to make sex inappropriate.

It is a moment to stop.

The person can be helped to a safe situation without sexual contact.

Being sober yourself does not give you authority to decide that an impaired person must want what you want.

The reverse matters, too. If someone violates your sexual boundaries while you are intoxicated, their behavior is not made acceptable by your drinking or drug use.

Most sexual harm is not limited to intoxicated situations

It would be misleading to make alcohol and drugs the entire sexual-safety discussion.

In one study of autistic and non-autistic adults, most participants’ most recently reported experiences of violence did not involve alcohol or drugs. The sample was mainly Australian, recruited online, and was not representative of the wider population (Gibbs et al., 2023).

Sexual pressure and violations of consent can occur between sober people who know each other well.

Safety therefore requires attention to how people behave toward one another, not just what substances they have taken.

When agreement becomes pressure

A person might technically say “okay” without freely wanting what follows.

Imagine that someone has already explained they don’t want sex. Their partner becomes upset about the refusal. The atmosphere turns tense. Eventually, the person agrees because they want the unpleasant situation to end.

That is not the same as freely choosing sex.

Consent is not something you obtain by making refusal difficult.

Pressure can leave someone feeling that agreeing is the only way to restore peace, avoid anger, or protect a relationship.

In interviews with 24 autistic adults who had experienced interpersonal victimization or abuse, Douglas and Sedgewick found accounts of unwanted sexual activity, pressure within relationships, and people becoming passive or shutting down instead of resisting. Some participants blamed themselves afterward for not communicating refusal more clearly (Douglas & Sedgewick, 2024).

The study was small and predominantly UK-based, with participants recruited because they had experienced abuse. It cannot tell us how frequently these experiences occur among all autistic adults.

It does, however, illustrate an important distinction between outward compliance and freely chosen participation.

If you find yourself agreeing to avoid conflict

Perhaps you dislike disappointing people. Maybe arguments are frightening, or you grew up believing that your needs would create problems.

You may notice yourself saying yes before checking whether you actually want something.

A useful question is:

If I knew the other person would accept my refusal calmly, would I still want to do this?

You do not need to answer instantly.

You can say, “I’m not sure what I want yet. I need to stop and think.”

A person who cares about your freedom to choose will not require you to make a sexual decision immediately.

If you discover that you agreed because you were afraid of the consequences of refusing, that does not make someone else’s pressure acceptable.

You also do not need to prove that your discomfort is sufficiently serious. Not wanting an activity is enough reason to decline it.

If you’re the person who wants to continue

You may be disappointed when a partner says no. Disappointment is a feeling, not a permission slip.

What matters is what you do with it.

You can accept the answer, stop sexual contact, and deal with your feelings without putting responsibility for them on your partner.

If you notice that your partner is going along with something reluctantly, stop. Do not treat their hesitation as a problem to solve or a signal that they need convincing.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly pressuring someone for sex, stop asking and stop the sexual activity. Take responsibility for your behavior and seek qualified professional help to change it. Do not make the person you pressured responsible for reassuring or forgiving you.

Chapter 35 discusses sexual assault in more depth. Chapter 36 covers patterns of manipulation and abuse.

Sexual safety also means agreeing about protection

Imagine that both people genuinely want to have sex. They’ve communicated clearly, they’re comfortable, and neither is being pressured.

There is still another conversation to have.

What are you agreeing to do to protect each other’s sexual health?

Chapter 33 covered the basic information about sexually transmitted infections (STIs), contraception, and available protections. Here the important skill is putting that information into practice with another person.

A sexual-health conversation can be simple.

“I’d like us to use condoms.”

“Okay.”

“Have you been tested for STIs recently?”

“Not recently. I’d rather talk about testing before we have sex.”

“That’s fine.”

There is no need to turn the discussion into an accusation or an interview.

The point is to make sure both people understand the conditions under which they are comfortable having sex.

What to agree about

Depending on your bodies, sexual activities, and circumstances, you may need to discuss pregnancy prevention, condoms or other barriers, STI testing, vaccination, and relevant preventive medications.

The CDC recommends properly used condoms, STI testing, and recommended vaccinations among the approaches to reducing STI risk. It also emphasizes that many infections cause no noticeable symptoms (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024a, 2024b).

That means someone’s appearance, personal hygiene, or confidence that they’re healthy cannot replace appropriate sexual-health information.

“Don’t worry, I’m clean” is not a useful substitute for discussing testing and protection. Having an STI is not a measure of whether someone is clean or respectable.

A more useful conversation addresses which tests someone has had, approximately when they had them, whether anything relevant has happened since, and what protection the partners intend to use.

CDC testing guidance explains that appropriate tests and timing depend on individual circumstances, including sexual practices and anatomical sites involved. Some infections may require throat or rectal testing, and a routine test does not necessarily check every infection or body site (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026b).

You do not have to become a medical expert to have this conversation.

You can consult a sexual-health clinician about which tests, vaccinations, or preventive treatments apply to you.

Protection is part of consent

Suppose Taylor agrees to sex with Sam on the condition that a condom is used.

That is what Taylor agreed to.

If the condom is unavailable, comes off, breaks, or someone wants to stop using it, the original agreement does not automatically extend to unprotected sex.

The activity needs to stop while the people involved decide what they want to do next.

This principle applies to other agreed precautions and boundaries, too.

Wanting sex with someone does not mean accepting whatever conditions the other person chooses.

If you cannot agree about protection, you can choose not to have sex.

You do not owe someone sexual access because you are dating them, because you have had sex before, or because you initially expressed interest.

And you cannot insist that someone accept a level of risk they do not want.

If protection fails and there is a concern about pregnancy or STI exposure, seek prompt advice from a qualified healthcare professional about the available options. Chapter 33 provides the broader sexual-health background.

If talking about sexual health feels embarrassing

It is common to feel awkward discussing bodies and sex, particularly when you have never done it before.

You might worry that asking about testing will sound suspicious, or that discussing contraception will make the moment uncomfortable.

But you do not need perfect wording.

You can begin with something plain:

“I haven’t had many conversations about sexual health, so this feels awkward. I still want us to talk about protection before anything happens.”

Saying that you are inexperienced with the conversation is different from saying you are irresponsible.

You are allowed to learn.

A partner who is also inexperienced may appreciate having the subject raised clearly.

Different experiences, the same right to choose

Research on sexual victimization among autistic adults suggests that some groups face elevated risks, but the evidence does not support a single universal figure.

In a Canadian study of 95 autistic and 117 non-autistic adults, autistic participants reported more sexual victimization and scored lower on measures of sexual knowledge. The authors found a statistical relationship involving sexual knowledge, but this does not prove that education by itself prevents victimization (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

A later US online study involving 663 participants found differences in certain categories of reported victimization, but not every category showed a statistically significant difference after adjustment for demographic characteristics. Its published abstract provides limited detail about the underlying measures (Akers & Peterson, 2026).

These studies differ in whom they recruited and how they measured experiences. Neither can establish how often sexual victimization occurs among all autistic adults in the United States.

More importantly, higher reported victimization does not mean that autistic people are inherently incapable of giving consent, understanding sex, or protecting their boundaries.

Responsibility for violating consent remains with the person who violates it.

Perspective: Men

If you have absorbed the expectation that men should always want sex, remember that your actual wishes matter more than that expectation. You are allowed to decline, change your mind, or need time.

Sexual victimization is not limited to women. Brown-Lavoie and colleagues found that autistic men in their Canadian sample reported several forms of sexual victimization more frequently than non-autistic men. The study was not representative of all men, but it makes clear why men’s experiences should not be left out of sexual-safety education (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

Perspective: Women

Being in a relationship does not create a standing obligation to have sex.

In Gibbs and colleagues’ multinational online study, partners or former partners accounted for 51% of the most recent incidents of sexual violence reported by autistic women. This is a finding from one selected sample, not a population-wide estimate (Gibbs et al., 2023).

A partner’s familiarity with you, your body, or your sexual history never replaces your current agreement. Your boundaries still apply with someone you love.

Perspective: Non-binary

You can describe your body, your boundaries, and the language you prefer without having to accept someone else’s assumptions about your gender. A partner can ask respectfully what kinds of touch and language are comfortable rather than guessing.

Non-binary adults were included in some of the studies discussed here, but in small numbers. For example, three of the 24 participants in Douglas and Sedgewick’s qualitative study were non-binary. That research offers insight into participants’ experiences but cannot establish a distinct risk profile for all non-binary people (Douglas & Sedgewick, 2024).

Your right to informed, freely given sexual consent does not depend on whether research has adequately represented you.

The same principle extends across age, orientation, disability, and experience.

Nobody needs a diagnosis to ask for clearer communication. Nobody has to disclose a diagnosis to establish a boundary.

Sexting, photographs, and sexual privacy

Sexual intimacy can also happen through a phone or computer.

Sexting means sending sexual messages, photographs, or videos electronically.

Some adults enjoy consensual sexting. Others dislike it, prefer only written messages, or do not want sexual content on their devices at all.

The important distinction is not whether sexting is inherently good or bad.

It is whether each person freely agrees to the specific communication or image use involved.

Permission to receive is not permission for everything else

Imagine that Lena and Tori are dating.

Lena enjoys flirtatious messages but does not want to exchange nude photographs.

She tells Tori, “I like our messages, but I don’t send sexual pictures.”

Tori accepts that.

A few weeks later, Lena receives a sexual photograph from Tori without any prior discussion.

Even if Tori intended the picture as flirtation, the two had not agreed to exchange sexual images.

A respectful approach would have been to ask whether such photographs were welcome before sending one.

The same principle applies to asking someone to send you an image.

They may decline. They may agree to one photograph but not another. They may decide they do not want to participate in sexual messaging at all.

None of these choices requires a justification.

Digital consent should be specific. In particular, these are separate decisions:

  • Whether to receive sexual messages or images.
  • Whether to send or create sexual images.
  • Whether a photograph or recording may be saved.
  • Whether it may be shown or forwarded to anyone else.
  • Whether any intimate activity may be photographed or recorded in the first place.

Agreeing to sex does not grant permission to record it.

Agreeing to create an intimate photograph does not grant permission to distribute it.

And agreeing to receive someone’s private image does not make it yours to share.

The US Department of Justice explicitly distinguishes consenting to create or privately share an intimate image from consenting to its disclosure to others (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

Private means private

Imagine sending a photograph to someone you trust, believing only that person will see it.

You later discover that other people have received copies.

That is a violation of the privacy you expected, regardless of whether you originally agreed to take or send the photograph.

The CDC includes nonconsensual sharing of sexual images among forms of technology-facilitated sexual violence (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026a).

One US study of 365 college students also found an association between digital dating abuse and other forms of dating aggression. This does not mean that consensual sexting is abusive, or that sending a sexual message inevitably leads to harm. It means harmful digital behaviors deserve attention in discussions of relationship safety (Reed et al., 2016).

You should not forward, screenshot for sharing, publicly post, or show someone else’s intimate content without explicit permission.

If a partner asks you to delete their private images, respecting that request is the appropriate response. Do not treat past willingness to send something as a permanent entitlement to possess it.

Think about privacy before creating an image

Even when everyone involved genuinely consents, digital material can be difficult to control once it exists.

Before creating an intimate image, consider whether you are comfortable with the possibility that it could be saved, copied, or disclosed despite your wishes.

You may decide that you do not want intimate photographs or recordings at all.

That is a complete answer.

You can also choose to engage in sexual messaging without creating images.

A relationship does not become less genuine because you refuse to produce permanent digital records of intimate moments.

If someone shares an intimate image without permission

There are steps available in the United States.

The Department of Justice explains that federal civil law provides a potential legal remedy for certain nonconsensual disclosures of intimate images. Requirements and exceptions apply, and state laws also differ (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, n.d.).

The Federal Trade Commission provides guidance about reporting image-based abuse and requesting removal.

Under the TAKE IT DOWN Act, covered online platforms have been required since May 19, 2026, to provide a reporting process for covered nonconsensual intimate images, including certain altered and AI-generated images. They generally must remove qualifying reported images and known identical copies within 48 hours of a valid notice (Federal Trade Commission, 2026).

For adults, StopNCII.org offers a tool intended to help participating platforms identify and limit the spread of intimate images. The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children’s Take It Down service addresses intimate imagery involving people under 18.

These systems cannot guarantee that every existing copy will disappear.

If someone has shared your intimate content without permission, you do not have to negotiate with them to earn your privacy back. Consider using the platform’s reporting process and obtaining support from an appropriate service or legal professional.

Sexual images involving minors carry additional serious legal concerns. This adult handbook does not attempt to summarize those laws. Do not create, request, save, or forward sexualized imagery of minors.

When something doesn’t feel right

Sexual situations are not always easy to understand while they are happening.

You might realize afterward that you agreed to something you did not want. You may recognize that a partner’s behavior made it hard to refuse. Or you might discover that you crossed someone’s boundary without recognizing it at the time.

Different responses are needed depending on what happened.

If you’re worried about something that happened to you, you do not have to determine immediately which legal or clinical label applies. You can stop contact, seek support, and take time to decide what you need.

If you believe someone violated your sexual consent, RAINN offers confidential support and information about available options at RAINN.org. Chapter 35 discusses sexual assault, medical care, and reporting decisions in more detail.

If you realize that you crossed another person’s sexual boundary, stop immediately. Do not continue because you are embarrassed, disappointed, or unsure how serious the mistake was.

Take responsibility without requiring the other person to comfort you, explain their response, or forgive you. Seek professional help if you need support understanding and changing your behavior.

A misunderstanding is not a reason to ignore a boundary once it becomes clear.

Three conversations worth practicing

You do not need to memorize dozens of scripts.

Instead, practice putting three ideas into your own words.

Before sexual activity:

“I’d like to talk about what we’re comfortable doing and what protection we want to use.”

During sexual activity:

“Do you want to keep going, or would you rather pause?”

About digital privacy:

“I’m comfortable with messages, but I don’t want private photographs or recordings.”

Try saying each sentence out loud.

If the wording feels unnatural, change it. The goal is not to deliver a perfect line. It is to become comfortable asking a question or stating a limit without needing to invent the words under pressure.

You can also practice what accepting an answer sounds like.

“Okay.”

“Thanks for telling me.”

“We can stop.”

Those responses leave the other person free to choose without having to manage your reaction.

One final distinction

Being sexually safe is not the same as guaranteeing that nothing uncomfortable, embarrassing, or disappointing will ever happen.

Two people may want different things. One may feel ready before the other. Someone may change their mind. A sexual-health discussion may reveal an incompatibility. An enjoyable evening might end without sex.

None of those outcomes means the interaction failed.

What matters is whether both people remain free to choose, and whether their choices are respected.

Good sexual communication does not require telepathy, extraordinary confidence, or a particular kind of brain. It requires a willingness to ask, listen, accept an answer, and stop when the answer is unclear.

The same skills that make sexual contact safer also make ordinary dating safer: saying what you want, recognizing what the other person wants, and respecting the difference.
