You can want someone to love you without wanting to get married.

You can want romance without wanting sex. You can want sex without wanting romance. You can want a committed partner but not want to live together. You can enjoy being single and still sometimes wish someone were sitting beside you on the couch.

You can even want a relationship someday without wanting to date right now.

None of these answers is a contradiction.

Yet when someone asks, “What are you looking for?” it can feel as though there should be one correct response. A serious relationship? Something casual? Marriage? Whatever happens?

Perhaps you’ve never considered the question beyond I want someone to like me. Perhaps you’ve spent years imagining a future partner without thinking much about what the two of you would actually do together on an ordinary Tuesday.

Or perhaps you’ve been told what you should want for so long that it’s difficult to separate your own wishes from everyone else’s.

The question isn’t whether your answer sounds normal, impressive, or appealing to a potential partner.

It’s whether the answer is yours.

The relationship you imagined might not be the relationship you want

Think about the relationships you saw growing up.

Maybe your parents married, shared a home, raised children, and stayed together. Maybe they separated. Maybe you watched a parent remain in an unhappy relationship. Maybe you grew up without seeing many relationships at all.

Then there were movies, television shows, books, religious expectations, and conversations with friends. Some presented marriage and children as the natural destination. Others suggested that an exciting relationship should involve constant passion, spontaneous adventures, and two people who never grow tired of each other.

It’s easy to absorb these pictures without deliberately choosing them.

At 37, Natalie has always said she wants to get married. When a friend asks what appeals to her about marriage, she struggles to answer.

She likes the thought of having someone who knows her well. She wants affection, shared meals, and a person to call when something wonderful or terrible happens.

But the thought of sharing a bedroom every night makes her uncomfortable. She enjoys her quiet apartment and wants to keep it. She isn’t interested in parenting.

Natalie has been describing her goal as marriage because that was the familiar name for the kind of connection she wanted.

What she actually wants is a committed romantic partnership with considerable independence. Marriage might fit that picture, but it isn’t necessarily the essential part.

Now consider Daniel, 29. He insists that he wants a casual relationship. He likes the idea of appearing independent and doesn’t want to seem overly serious.

Yet when he imagines someone he cares about dating other people, he feels unhappy. He wants exclusivity, regular time together, and the possibility of building a shared future.

Daniel may discover that his actual goal is more committed than the label he has been using.

Neither person needs to adopt a different goal to become acceptable. Both need a more accurate description.

A relationship goal is a description of the connection you would like to build, not an answer you give to make yourself attractive to somebody else.

There isn’t one normal answer

In a nationally weighted Pew Research Center survey conducted in 2022, 57% of single American adults said they weren’t looking for a relationship or casual dates at that time. Among single adults, 22% were open to either casual dates or a committed relationship, 13% wanted only a committed relationship, and 7% wanted only casual dates. These were broad survey categories, not an exhaustive list of human wishes (Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

The point isn’t that you should copy the most popular answer.

It’s that even within a limited set of options, people want different things.

You don’t have to aim for the relationship your friends have, the one your parents expected, or the one that seems easiest to explain.

You do have to be truthful about what you’re seeking, particularly when someone else’s feelings and choices may be affected.

Separate the things you want

“Being in a relationship” sounds like a single desire, but it can contain several different ones.

Companionship means having someone whose company you enjoy. You might want regular conversation, shared activities, or someone to spend holidays with.

Romance means wanting a kind of closeness you experience as romantic. That might include dating, romantic affection, or imagining yourselves as partners. Different people express romance differently.

Sexual connection means wanting some form of sexual intimacy. It may be important to you, unimportant, or something you aren’t sure about.

Commitment means making an agreement about your ongoing relationship. Commitment might involve exclusivity, supporting each other, making plans, or sharing responsibilities.

Shared daily life means deciding how closely your routines and practical lives will connect. You might prefer separate homes, a shared household, or something in between.

These desires can overlap, but one does not automatically include the others.

Someone might want romantic affection and long-term commitment while having little interest in sex. Someone else might enjoy sexual intimacy without wanting a romantic partnership. Another person might value the companionship of a partner but need substantial time alone.

You may recognize several desires, only one, or none at the moment.

You may also want different amounts of each.

For instance, you might enjoy spending Saturday afternoon with a partner but not want to spend every evening together. You might love physical affection but dislike surprise touching. You might want a family but prefer to establish a relationship slowly.

The sensory needs, emotional processing, masking, and energy patterns discussed in earlier chapters can help you understand the conditions under which connection feels comfortable.

They don’t decide what kind of relationship you must want.

Wanting quiet evenings doesn’t mean you don’t want romance. Needing time to identify a feeling doesn’t mean the feeling isn’t there. Finding frequent texting exhausting doesn’t automatically mean you want a distant partner.

These experiences are information about your needs, not a complete description of your desires.

What do you picture when you picture being loved?

Try imagining a day rather than a wedding, a first kiss, or a dramatic declaration.

It’s an ordinary Wednesday. You’ve finished work or your usual activities. Nothing exciting is happening.

What would you like a partner to do?

Maybe you’d enjoy making dinner together. Perhaps you’d prefer eating separately and talking afterward. Maybe you’d like a message asking about your day, but not a phone call. Perhaps you’d like your partner nearby while each of you works on something different.

What happens on a difficult Wednesday?

Would you want company, practical help, reassurance, quiet, or time alone before talking? Would you want someone who could share responsibilities without waiting for you to organize everything?

Then imagine the other person’s Wednesday.

They may want things that differ from yours.

A relationship isn’t simply the life you imagined with another person inserted into it. It involves two people with preferences, limits, and responsibilities.

You don’t have to agree on every preference. But the relationship you want must allow both people to remain people.

Romantic attraction and sexual attraction aren’t the same thing

One reason the question “What do you want?” can be difficult is that society often treats romance and sex as a package.

People are expected to find someone attractive, develop romantic feelings, want sexual intimacy, and eventually build a conventional relationship.

But those experiences don’t always occur together.

Sexual attraction means experiencing another person as someone you’re sexually drawn to.

Romantic attraction means experiencing another person as someone you’re romantically drawn to.

You might experience both toward the same person. You might experience one without the other. You might rarely experience either.

And you can value companionship, affection, loyalty, or partnership regardless of how attraction works for you.

The asexual spectrum

Asexuality generally describes experiencing little or no sexual attraction.

The asexual spectrum, sometimes called the ace spectrum, includes a range of experiences involving absent, infrequent, or conditional sexual attraction.

Two terms you might encounter are:

  • Graysexual: Someone who experiences sexual attraction rarely or under limited circumstances.
  • Demisexual: Someone who experiences sexual attraction only after developing a substantial emotional connection.

These descriptions are not stages that everyone moves through. They are ways people may understand and describe their experiences.

Asexuality also doesn’t automatically tell you whether someone wants romance, enjoys affection, experiences sexual desire, has sex, or wants a partner. Attraction, sexual behavior, and relationship goals are different questions.

A study comparing asexual, graysexual, and demisexual participants found differences in their reported desire, behavior, and identities. The participants were recruited from an asexual-spectrum community survey, so the findings describe that sample rather than everyone with these experiences (Copulsky & Hammack, 2023).

An older British probability survey found that approximately 1% of respondents reported no sexual attraction to anyone. But it used data collected decades ago and a narrower definition than today’s range of asexual-spectrum identities. It is not a reliable count of how many Americans currently identify as asexual or somewhere on the ace spectrum (Bogaert, 2004).

You don’t need to calculate how common an orientation is before deciding whether a label helps describe you.

Consider Maya, a 34-year-old woman who enjoys romantic affection. She wants someone to celebrate birthdays with, share difficult days with, and possibly marry.

She rarely experiences sexual attraction. For years, she has assumed that a romantic relationship would require her to pretend otherwise.

Learning about asexuality gives Maya language for a question she can now discuss openly: What kind of intimacy would feel right to her, and would a potential partner want something compatible?

The label doesn’t make that conversation unnecessary. It makes her experience easier to describe.

The aromantic spectrum

Aromanticism generally describes experiencing little or no romantic attraction.

The aromantic spectrum, sometimes called the aro spectrum, includes people whose romantic attraction is absent, rare, or conditional.

Aromanticism isn’t the same as asexuality.

Someone can be aromantic and experience sexual attraction. Someone can be asexual and experience romantic attraction. Someone can identify with both spectrums or neither.

An international survey of 1,642 people in aromantic communities found varied experiences of romance, identity, and relationships. Participants also described the frustration of having aromanticism treated as though it must be a form of asexuality. Because respondents came from an online community sample, the study cannot tell us how common aromanticism is in the general population (Fowler et al., 2024).

Being aromantic doesn’t mean being incapable of affection, friendship, loyalty, or love.

Consider Ellis, a 42-year-old nonbinary person who enjoys deep friendships and values building a dependable life with people they trust. Ellis doesn’t experience the kind of romantic attraction their friends describe.

They sometimes wish for a close, lasting partnership, but they don’t want to promise conventional romance in order to obtain it.

Ellis might prefer a committed non-romantic partnership, sometimes called a queerplatonic relationship. The people involved define the relationship’s meaning and agreements rather than relying on a standard romantic script.

Or Ellis might prefer close friendships without a partnership.

Neither answer needs to be corrected.

You don’t have to know your label

Perhaps you recognize parts of these descriptions but aren’t sure where you fit.

Maybe you’ve experienced attraction only once. Maybe you find people beautiful without wanting sex or romance with them. Maybe you want closeness but don’t know what kind.

You can describe those experiences without choosing an identity immediately.

For example:

“I enjoy affection, but I’m still figuring out what sexual intimacy means to me.”

Or:

“I want a close partnership. I’m not sure yet how I experience romantic attraction.”

The important part is being honest about what you know and what remains uncertain.

You also don’t owe anyone sexual activity to prove that you’re interested in them. Nor does a label remove the need for consent. Every person retains the right to decide whether they want a particular kind of contact.

If you are exploring your identity, you can take that exploration seriously without treating another person’s boundaries as an experiment.

If you've only ever been handed one script

If the only relationship you’ve seen described in detail is the standard one — date, exclusivity, moving in, the rest in order — it’s easy to assume that wanting something else means wanting nothing.

Work out the parts separately. Living alone, wanting sex, wanting company every day and wanting one person for decades are four different questions, and you’re allowed a different answer to each.

An honest “I don’t know yet, and here’s what I do know” is a far better starting position than a borrowed answer you’ll have to walk back later.

Your brain doesn’t determine your relationship goal

If you struggle to read flirtation, become tired during social activities, or need substantial time alone, someone may assume you aren’t interested in relationships.

You might even have begun to believe it.

But difficulty with certain parts of dating is not the same as a lack of desire for connection.

In a Dutch study comparing 675 autistic adolescents and adults with more than 8,000 general-population peers, approximately half of the autistic participants reported being in a romantic relationship. Most of those who were partnered lived with their partner (Dewinter et al., 2017).

A separate large online survey found that a majority of its autistic respondents expressed interest in sexual relationships, although their reported experiences and orientations differed from those of non-autistic respondents (Weir et al., 2021).

Neither study represents all autistic people, and interest in sexual relationships isn’t a measurement of every kind of romantic desire.

Still, these findings challenge the assumption that autistic people are generally uninterested in connection.

Other studies have reported higher rates of asexual identification or low sexual interest among autistic participants than among comparison groups. A systematic review found an association but could not establish why it exists (Attanasio et al., 2022). A large survey of people already identifying on the asexual spectrum also found overlap with autism, alongside considerable variation in relationship and sexual experiences (Chan & Hung, 2025).

That overlap is not a reason to treat asexuality as a symptom of autism or as something to fix.

An autistic person might be asexual, heterosexual, bisexual, gay, or have another orientation. They might want marriage, casual dating, a non-romantic partnership, or no relationship.

None of these choices can be predicted from the diagnosis alone.

The same principle matters when attention, memory, and organization are difficult.

One Irish qualitative study asked 355 adults reporting ADHD about negative effects on their romantic relationships. Participants described experiences involving distraction, perceived rejection, self-understanding, and uneven practical responsibilities. The researchers acknowledged that asking specifically about negative experiences shaped the findings (O’Brien et al., 2026).

Those findings cannot tell you what relationship structure a person with ADHD ought to choose.

They can remind you to think beyond attraction.

If you find daily planning difficult, what expectations about shared responsibilities would be workable? If you often need quiet after social contact, how much time together would feel sustainable? If you respond to emotional conversations slowly, what communication arrangements would allow both people to be heard?

These are relationship questions, not tests of whether someone is capable of love.

Two autistic partners may have very different answers. A neurotypical person and an autistic partner may share many preferences. Two people with ADHD may agree about romance but disagree about how much structure they want in daily life.

Remember the sculpture and glasses picture from the beginning of the book. Different people see a relationship through different experiences. Instead of assuming that one person’s description is the correct one, describe what you want and ask what the other person wants.

The same principle applies whether your brains work similarly or differently.

What kind of relationship would you agree to?

Wanting a partner is one question.

Deciding what the partnership allows is another.

Some people want a relationship that is exclusive. Others want arrangements in which more than one romantic or sexual relationship may be possible.

The difference matters because people need to know what they’re agreeing to.

Monogamy

Monogamy usually means a relationship in which two people agree to romantic and sexual exclusivity.

Many people find that kind of commitment appealing.

They may want to focus their romantic and sexual attention on one partner. They may feel most comfortable knowing that both people have agreed not to pursue those connections elsewhere.

But even when two people both say they want monogamy, they still need to understand what each means.

Does exclusivity begin after a particular conversation, or has someone assumed it starts after the first date? Are both people comfortable with their partner maintaining close friendships? How do they want to discuss changing feelings?

The word monogamy doesn’t answer every question.

It’s a starting point for an agreement.

Consensual non-monogamy

Consensual non-monogamy describes relationships in which the people involved knowingly and freely agree that certain romantic or sexual connections with others are permitted.

There are different forms.

Polyamory allows the possibility of multiple loving or intimate partnerships with everyone’s informed agreement. Other open relationships may permit sexual connections outside a primary relationship without necessarily seeking additional romantic partnerships.

The details depend on the people involved. Labels alone don’t establish the agreements (Moors et al., 2021).

Consensual non-monogamy is not simply having multiple partners. The word consensual matters.

People must know what arrangement they’re participating in and be able to decide freely whether they want it.

Dating or having sexual contact with others while concealing it from a partner who believes the relationship is exclusive is not consensual non-monogamy.

Nor is an agreement freely given if someone feels they must accept it to avoid threats, punishment, or persistent pressure.

Both monogamy and consensual non-monogamy involve responsibilities to the people affected by the arrangement.

Is one structure better?

People sometimes describe monogamy as the only serious form of commitment. Others suggest that non-monogamy is inherently more honest, more mature, or more enlightened.

Neither claim should decide your relationship for you.

In two large U.S. quota samples of single adults, approximately 21% to 22% reported that they had participated in some form of consensual non-monogamy at least once. This measures lifetime experience among single respondents, not how many people currently practice it (Haupert et al., 2017).

Another U.S. study of 3,438 single adults found that 16.8% expressed interest in polyamory and 10.7% reported having experienced it. Interest and experience were measured separately (Moors et al., 2021).

A meta-analysis combining 35 studies and 24,489 participants found no statistically significant average difference in reported relationship satisfaction or sexual satisfaction between monogamous and non-monogamous respondents. Individual studies varied, however, and the evidence relied on self-reports from samples that often recruited volunteers online. The result doesn’t establish that every arrangement works equally well for everyone or that changing structures causes satisfaction to improve (Anderson et al., 2026).

For a beginner, the practical question is not which structure can be declared the winner.

It’s which agreements you genuinely want to make and are prepared to honor.

Noah, 43, wants a sexually and romantically exclusive relationship. Camila, 40, is interested in polyamory.

They like each other, but when they discuss their goals, they discover the difference.

Noah says, “I want exclusivity in a relationship. I’m not interested in changing that.”

Camila replies, “I don’t want to promise exclusivity. It’s important that I’m clear about that.”

Neither person has done anything wrong.

They also don’t have to turn this difference into a debate where one person eventually wins.

They may simply want incompatible relationships.

That can be disappointing. It doesn’t mean either person’s preference is illegitimate.

You can change your mind, but you can’t change the agreement alone

Someone who once preferred monogamy may later become curious about a different arrangement. Someone who tried non-monogamy may decide they want exclusivity.

A change in preference can be discussed. It doesn’t automatically change an existing agreement.

The other person is allowed to say no.

And if a relationship cannot continue without one person accepting an arrangement they don’t want, ending it may be the respectful choice.

An agreement is not meaningful if one person is free to redefine it while the other is expected to accept the consequences.

Think about the shape of your everyday life

Exclusivity isn’t the only significant relationship decision.

Imagine meeting someone who is kind, attractive to you, and interested in you.

Now imagine discovering that they want children while you firmly do not.

Or that they expect to move across the country in two years, while you have no intention of leaving your community.

Or that they want to share nearly every evening, while you need regular time by yourself.

These differences don’t make either person defective.

But they may matter far more than how exciting the first date felt.

Before dating seriously, it’s worth considering a few practical questions.

Children: Do you want to raise children? Do you already have children? Are you open to dating someone who does? Is your answer uncertain?

Marriage: Is legal marriage something you want, something you might consider, or something you don’t want?

Living together: Would you like to share a home eventually? Would you prefer separate homes even in a committed relationship?

Time together: How often would you realistically enjoy seeing a partner? How much time alone would you want to preserve?

Location: Are you willing to move, or is staying near your current home, work, family, or support network important?

Daily responsibilities: What expectations do you have about managing money, household work, planning, and supporting each other through difficult periods?

You don’t need perfect answers. You may not know what living with a partner would be like if you’ve never done it.

But an answer such as “I have never lived with a partner, and I would want to discuss that carefully” is more informative than pretending you have no preferences.

Needs, preferences, and uncertainties

Not everything you want carries the same importance.

A need is something you consider necessary for a relationship to be acceptable to you.

A preference is something you would enjoy but might willingly approach differently.

An uncertainty is something you haven’t decided.

For example, someone might say:

“I need a relationship without pressure for sexual intimacy. I would prefer a partner who enjoys quiet weekends. I’m uncertain whether I want to live together.”

Another person might say:

“I need to be with someone who also wants children. I’d prefer to get married. I haven’t decided whether living in a city or a smaller town would suit me best.”

There is no universal list of needs.

One person’s preference may be another person’s firm boundary.

It helps to distinguish a boundary from a request. A boundary describes what you will do or accept; a request asks someone else for something, and they can say no.

“I won’t continue a relationship that requires sexual exclusivity” describes a boundary.

“Would you consider a non-monogamous relationship?” is a request.

If the answer is no, the person asking must respect it. They can decide whether the relationship remains suitable, but they cannot make acceptance a condition that the other person is pressured into meeting.

You are responsible for recognizing your own limits and respecting someone else’s.

What you want and what you can offer are different questions

It’s possible to know exactly what kind of partner you want and still be uncertain about what participating in that relationship would involve.

You might want someone who communicates regularly while finding frequent messages difficult yourself.

You might want a dependable partner but know that remembering appointments and making plans requires deliberate systems.

You might want considerable independence while also expecting someone to be available whenever you feel lonely.

These aren’t reasons to stop wanting a relationship.

They are reasons to think about what an agreement would look like from both sides.

Suppose Tessa and Jordan, both autistic, enjoy spending time together. Tessa loves long conversations and would happily talk for several hours. Jordan enjoys their conversations but often needs a quiet evening afterward.

Tessa’s wish for conversation is reasonable. Jordan’s need for quiet is reasonable.

If they continue dating, the useful conversation is not about which person cares more.

It’s about whether they can find a pattern both genuinely enjoy.

Tessa might say, “I really like our longer conversations. Would a regular evening together work for you?”

Jordan might answer, “I’d enjoy that. I’d also like us to have some evenings without an expectation to call.”

The proposal gives each person information and room to respond.

A different couple might make different arrangements.

The important point is that wanting something from a partner is not the same as having a right to receive it.

Likewise, needing support is not the same as owing someone a promise you cannot keep.

If you know that certain kinds of contact, commitment, or shared responsibility will be difficult, acknowledge that when it becomes relevant. You don’t need to disclose a diagnosis to describe how you function.

And when you’re dating someone whose needs differ from yours, don’t assume their preferences measure their affection.

A person who wants time alone may care deeply about you. A person who wants frequent contact may be expressing a genuine preference rather than trying to control you.

Ask what the preference means before deciding what it says about the relationship.

If you're dating someone who isn't sure what they want yet

Uncertainty about the shape of a relationship isn’t the same as uncertainty about you. Someone can be clear that they like you and genuinely unsure what they want their life to look like.

Ask about the specific thing you need to know rather than the whole future: “Do you want to keep seeing just me for now?” is answerable. “Where is this going?” often isn’t.

You’re entitled to a timeline for your own sake. Say what you need and when, rather than waiting indefinitely and resenting it.

“Not right now” is a real answer

Sometimes the clearest answer to “What do you want?” is “I’m not looking for a relationship right now.”

That doesn’t mean you’re rejecting love forever.

You might enjoy your life as it is. You might want to devote time to friendships, family, education, work, creative interests, or simply having more control over your schedule.

You might like the idea of a partner someday but have no interest in meeting new people at present.

You may also be unsure, and prefer not to make promises while you decide.

As the Pew survey found, more than half of single American adults surveyed in 2022 weren’t actively seeking dates or a relationship at that time (Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

Singlehood also isn’t necessarily experienced as an unwanted waiting period.

One study combining several samples of single adults, along with longitudinal data from the Netherlands, found that satisfaction with being single tended to increase after midlife. The study identified age-related patterns, not a guarantee that remaining single makes any individual happier (Park et al., 2022).

Some people enjoy singlehood. Others want a relationship and find being single painful. Some experience both feelings at different times.

You don’t have to deny loneliness to be comfortable with a decision not to date. You also don’t have to treat occasional loneliness as proof that you should start dating immediately.

Wanting a relationship eventually and wanting to pursue one today are separate questions.

Chapter 8 will examine readiness in more detail, including how your current circumstances and support affect the decision to date.

For now, consider whether you’re choosing to pursue a relationship or merely feeling that you should.

You don’t need an excuse to remain single

You don’t have to demonstrate that you’re too busy, too stressed, too independent, or too hurt to date.

“I’m not interested in dating right now” is sufficient.

You can change that answer later without contradicting yourself.

The same freedom belongs to everyone else.

Someone who doesn’t want to date you doesn’t owe you a more convincing reason. Their decision isn’t a puzzle you have to solve.

A meaningful relationship requires two people who freely want to participate.

It’s okay to be undecided, but don’t pretend to be decided

Not knowing exactly what you want is common enough to deserve a practical response: say what you do know.

You may want to meet people without promising a long-term relationship. You may be interested in a committed partnership but unsure about marriage. You may know you want romance while still exploring what sexual intimacy means to you.

Uncertainty is not dishonesty.

Misrepresenting your intentions is.

Suppose Amir, 27, has never dated seriously. He’s curious about a committed relationship but doesn’t know whether he wants one immediately.

He could say:

“I’m interested in getting to know someone and seeing whether a relationship feels right. I’m not ready to promise a long-term commitment before we’ve spent time together.”

That communicates uncertainty without suggesting a commitment already exists.

Now suppose Denise, 51, knows she does not want an exclusive relationship.

“I enjoy dating, but I’m not looking for exclusivity. I want to be clear about that so you can decide whether it works for you.”

She isn’t asking the other person to accept her preference. She’s providing information they need to make their own decision.

Both people can state their intentions without making promises about feelings they haven’t developed.

And both need to listen when the other person answers.

If your answer changes, discuss it. Don’t assume the other person’s answer has changed with yours.

You can discover a preference without acting on it immediately

Sometimes you only learn what matters after encountering a situation.

You might discover that you prefer more time alone than you expected. You might develop romantic feelings after previously thinking you weren’t interested in romance. You might enjoy dating but realize you don’t want the long-term structure you imagined.

You can acknowledge new information without rushing to reorganize your life around it.

If you process feelings slowly, give yourself time to consider what you experienced before making a promise.

If you have a strong initial attraction, remember that attraction tells you something about your feelings toward a person, not necessarily everything about the relationship you want.

A new preference is worth understanding. It doesn’t automatically require immediate action.

An exercise: Write your relationship description

Set aside ten or fifteen minutes.

You are not making a permanent declaration. You’re recording your best understanding of yourself today.

Write your answers somewhere private, in full sentences or short phrases.

First: Describe the connection.

Complete this sentence:

“I would like a relationship where…”

Try to describe ordinary experiences rather than using only a label.

For example:

“I would like a relationship where someone and I enjoy spending time together, support each other, and still have independent lives.”

Or:

“I would like romance and a committed partner, but I don’t want to assume that means marriage or children.”

If you don’t want a relationship at present, write that.

Second: Separate the parts.

Consider romance, sexual intimacy, affection, companionship, exclusivity, living arrangements, marriage, and children.

For each, ask:

“Do I want this, not want this, or genuinely not know?”

You may discover that some answers feel clear and others are only guesses.

Leave uncertain answers uncertain.

Third: Find the important differences.

Choose one thing you consider a need, one thing you would prefer, and one thing you’re willing to explore.

For example:

  • Need: “I want a partner who respects my need for time alone.”
  • Preference: “I’d enjoy sharing a home eventually.”
  • Uncertainty: “I don’t know whether I want children.”

Don’t select a need merely because it seems respectable. Don’t call something negotiable when you already know you would be unhappy agreeing to it.

Fourth: Consider the other person’s experience.

Complete two more sentences:

“A person dating me could reasonably expect…”

“A person dating me should know that…”

Perhaps you can offer affection, reliability, careful listening, or shared activities. Perhaps you should explain that you need advance planning, take time to reply, or prefer less physical contact.

Focus on what you can realistically offer, including the arrangements that help you follow through.

Fifth: Check whose answer you’re giving.

Look at what you’ve written.

Ask yourself:

“If my family, friends, community, and potential dates had no opinions about my relationship choices, which of these answers would remain?”

You may find that some wishes are genuinely yours even though other people share them.

You may also recognize a goal you’ve been pursuing because it was expected.

You don’t need to resolve every disagreement with your upbringing today. Simply notice what belongs to you.

Finally, write a short statement describing your current relationship goal.

It might be:

“I’m interested in an exclusive romantic relationship, but I want to get to know someone slowly before deciding about a long-term future.”

Or:

“I want a committed partnership with affection and companionship, and I’m uncertain about sexual intimacy.”

Or:

“I enjoy my independence and don’t want to date right now.”

Or simply:

“I’m still figuring out what kind of relationship I want. I don’t want to imply that I’m ready for commitments I haven’t chosen.”

Keep the statement somewhere you can revisit it.

As you learn more about yourself, you can revise it.

The purpose is not to produce a perfect answer. It’s to have a more truthful starting point.

A relationship you choose, not one you perform

When you don’t know what you want, it’s tempting to let the other person’s preferences decide.

If they want to see you every day, you may assume you should want that too. If they want marriage, you may agree because marriage sounds like the expected destination. If they want sexual intimacy, you may worry that declining means you aren’t suitable for a relationship.

Or you may do the opposite: reject anything that feels unfamiliar before considering whether you might actually enjoy it.

Neither approach requires you to examine your own preferences.

You don’t need to become a different person to deserve companionship. But you do need to recognize that another person will bring equally real wishes and limits.

You might find someone whose goals resemble yours closely. You might find someone whose preferences differ in ways you can both comfortably accommodate.

You might also meet a wonderful person who wants a relationship you don’t want.

A person can be attractive, kind, and genuinely interested in you without being someone with whom you want to build the same life.

That’s not a failure of affection. It’s a difference worth respecting.

Your relationship goals don’t need to be fashionable, conventional, or impressive. They need to be honest enough that another person can understand what you’re inviting them into.

And you need to remain open to hearing their answer, including when it differs from yours.

Knowing what you want doesn’t guarantee that you’ll find it. But it gives you a way to recognize when you’re being offered something you don’t actually want, and a way to describe what you do.

You don’t need the entire future mapped out.

You need a starting point that belongs to you.
