Priya likes falling asleep with her partner’s arm around her. She loves holding hands, kissing goodnight, and sitting close enough that their shoulders touch. But when she’s tired, the feeling of someone stroking her skin can become unbearable.

Her partner, Alex, loves physical affection too. For Alex, kissing and touching sometimes lead naturally to wanting sex. Priya enjoys sex at times, but she doesn’t always experience that connection.

Both are autistic. Both love each other. Neither is doing anything wrong.

For a while, they don’t discuss the difference. Alex worries that asking for sex will make Priya uncomfortable. Priya worries that turning away from a kiss will make Alex feel rejected. Eventually, ordinary affection becomes complicated. Alex hesitates before reaching for her. Priya wonders whether every cuddle comes with an expectation.

The problem isn’t that they want different things. It’s that they haven’t yet explained what those things mean.

They begin with a simple conversation. Priya explains that she wants to be close to Alex without wondering what might be expected afterward. Alex explains that kissing sometimes makes them interested in sex, but that doesn’t mean they expect Priya to feel the same way.

They agree that a cuddle can just be a cuddle. If either wants something different, they can ask, and the other can answer without pressure.

Nothing about their sensory preferences has changed. They simply understand each other better.

That distinction matters. Intimacy isn’t about getting another person to want what you want. It’s about finding forms of closeness that both people genuinely welcome.

Intimacy is more than sex

Intimacy means being close to someone in a way that feels personal and meaningful. It can involve your feelings, your body, your thoughts, or experiences you share.

Emotional intimacy might mean telling your partner something you’ve never told anyone else, trusting them with an insecurity, or feeling comfortable being yourself without pretending.

Physical intimacy might mean holding hands, cuddling, leaning against each other, kissing, or having sex.

Sexual intimacy involves mutually wanted sexual experiences. These may or may not be part of a romantic relationship.

These forms of closeness can overlap, but they aren’t interchangeable.

Someone might love sex but dislike cuddling. Someone else might crave hugs and kisses but have little interest in sex. Another person might enjoy sex without experiencing it as an especially emotional activity.

A qualitative study comparing 31 autistic and 26 non-autistic adults found that both groups valued nonsexual affection. Some autistic participants described cuddling and other forms of closeness as especially meaningful, while distinguishing them from sexual activity. Some wanted romantic relationships without wanting sex (Sala et al., 2024).

The study was small and included mostly women or feminine-identifying participants. It doesn’t establish how most people experience intimacy. It does show why assuming that everyone connects touch, sex, and emotional closeness in the same way can create misunderstandings.

Consider two people watching television together.

One feels loved when their partner moves closer and rests a head on their shoulder. The other feels loved when their partner remembers which show they wanted to watch and makes time to enjoy it together.

Neither expression is automatically better.

You don’t have to turn intimacy into a checklist of activities that every relationship must include. The important questions are whether the people involved want the closeness they’re sharing and whether it works for their relationship.

A relationship doesn’t have to include sex

Sex is important to many people. For some, it is a major part of how they experience romantic closeness. For others, it matters less, or not at all.

Asexuality describes experiencing little or no sexual attraction, although people who identify as asexual vary in how they feel about sex and whether they choose to have it. Aromanticism describes experiencing little or no romantic attraction. Sexual attraction and romantic attraction are different experiences.

An international survey of 309 autistic and 310 non-autistic adults found that autistic respondents reported non-heterosexual orientations, including asexuality, more often than the comparison group. Because participants were recruited online rather than through a representative population sample, the findings shouldn’t be treated as universal rates (George & Stokes, 2018). Another smaller study found different orientation patterns, illustrating how results can depend on sampling and measurement (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

The practical point doesn’t depend on percentages.

You can want romance without sex. You can want sex without romance. You can want both, neither, or something that changes over time.

You don’t need to have sex to prove that you’re an adult, that you’re attracted to someone, or that your relationship is legitimate.

You also don’t need to pretend sex is unimportant to you if it matters deeply.

Two people can care for each other and still want relationships that don’t fit together. The answer isn’t to declare one person’s needs normal and the other’s unreasonable. It’s to find out whether both can be satisfied with a relationship they freely choose.

Talking about what you actually want

Many people can discuss their jobs, finances, favorite foods, and future plans without much difficulty. Ask them what kind of physical affection they enjoy, and suddenly the words disappear.

There may be embarrassment. You might worry about sounding inexperienced or demanding. You might not know what you like yet. You might be afraid that an honest answer will hurt someone.

But silence doesn’t make differences disappear.

A meta-analysis combining 93 studies and 38,499 people found that better sexual communication was associated with greater sexual satisfaction and relationship satisfaction. The quality of communication showed stronger associations than simply how often couples discussed sex (Mallory, 2022).

That doesn’t prove that talking automatically improves a relationship. Much of the evidence is correlational: people in happier relationships may also find these conversations easier.

It does suggest that communicating well deserves attention.

And communicating well doesn’t mean delivering a sophisticated speech about sexuality. Often, it means making something unclear a little clearer.

You might say:

“I really like kissing you. I don’t always want it to lead to sex, though.”

Or:

“Physical affection helps me feel close to you. Could we talk about what kinds of touch you enjoy?”

Or:

“I’m still figuring out what feels comfortable for me. Can I tell you when something isn’t working?”

Your partner may have questions, preferences, or uncertainties of their own.

A useful conversation leaves room for both people.

Talk when you’re not in the middle of deciding

Imagine someone saying, “Can we talk about how often we have sex?” just as their partner is getting ready to sleep.

Even a reasonable question can feel urgent when the other person is exhausted or when an answer seems to determine what happens next.

For conversations about preferences, an ordinary, private moment with no immediate expectation is often easier.

You can ask whether it’s a good time to discuss something personal. Your partner can say that another time would be better.

Start with curiosity instead of an accusation.

“You never want to cuddle” makes a claim about the other person’s behavior.

“I miss cuddling with you, and I’d like to understand how you feel about it” explains your experience and invites theirs.

If the conversation is uncomfortable, it doesn’t all have to happen at once. Some people prefer to write their thoughts down first. Others need time to understand what they’re feeling before answering.

A delayed answer can be a thoughtful answer.

You don’t have to know everything about yourself yet

You might have reached adulthood without much sexual experience. You might have had sex before but never felt comfortable discussing it. Perhaps previous partners made the decisions, and you went along because you assumed that was how relationships worked.

Now someone asks what you want, and you genuinely don’t know.

That’s an acceptable answer.

“I’m not sure yet” communicates something important. So does “I used to enjoy that, but lately I haven’t.”

You can explore your preferences privately without committing to any particular experience with another person.

Being unsure doesn’t mean giving someone else permission to decide for you.

If you don’t know whether you want a particular activity, you can wait. You don’t owe anyone an experiment with your body to discover the answer.

When touch feels wonderful, uncomfortable, or both

Physical affection isn’t just an idea. It’s a sensory experience.

A hug has pressure, warmth, smell, texture, duration, and movement. A kiss involves closeness and sensations that might be delightful to one person and uncomfortable to another.

Your response can depend on the kind of touch, the person, the setting, and how you’re feeling that day.

In interviews with autistic adults, some participants described touch as intense or startling, especially when unexpected. Others enjoyed physical closeness but needed limits or different approaches. Some described becoming “touched out,” when they no longer wanted additional contact. Not every autistic participant reported sensory difficulties (Sala et al., 2024).

A separate study of autistic people’s narratives also documented that sensory experiences could contribute positively to intimacy or make it difficult, depending on the individual and circumstances (Gray et al., 2021).

This isn’t exclusively an autistic experience. Anyone can have preferences about touch. What matters is that partners don’t assume those preferences match.

You might like firm hugs but dislike light strokes. You might enjoy kissing but find certain sounds distracting. You might want to cuddle for ten minutes and then need your own space. You might want affectionate touch more frequently than your partner does.

Those are things you can describe rather than mysteries your partner must solve.

If you're dating someone whose comfort with touch changes

A partner might enjoy a hug one afternoon and need more personal space that evening. If they explain that they’re experiencing sensory overload, take the explanation seriously. It doesn’t automatically mean they’re upset with you or no longer attracted to you.

Ask what feels comfortable rather than deciding for them. They might prefer a shorter hug, different pressure, advance notice, or no touch at all for a while. These are possibilities, not universal autistic preferences. Their answer matters more than any general rule.

Make affection easier to understand

Consider Nadia, 42, and her girlfriend, Michelle, 45.

Nadia loves sitting against Michelle on the couch, but she dislikes being unexpectedly touched from behind. Michelle initially interprets Nadia moving away as a sign that she’s upset.

When they discuss it, Nadia explains the difference.

“I love being close to you. I just don’t like being surprised by touch.”

Michelle asks, “Would you rather I ask before putting my arms around you?”

“Yes. That would help.”

They also discover that Nadia enjoys resting her legs against Michelle’s while reading. Michelle prefers holding hands.

Their solution isn’t to find the one correct way to cuddle. It’s to keep learning what works for both.

Other possibilities include adjusting lighting, reducing background noise, choosing comfortable bedding, taking breaks, or allowing someone to move away without having to explain immediately.

These are practical options, not treatments shown to work for every neurodivergent couple.

One particularly useful agreement is that nonsexual affection doesn’t automatically become sexual affection.

A kiss isn’t a contract for anything afterward. Cuddling doesn’t create a debt. Neither does getting into bed together.

If someone wants to change the activity, they can ask.

And the other person can say no.

Desire doesn’t always arrive the same way

You may have absorbed the idea that sexual desire should appear suddenly and powerfully. Someone looks at their partner, attraction strikes, and everything else happens naturally.

Real experiences can be less predictable.

Sexual-medicine guidance distinguishes two patterns.

Spontaneous desire means becoming interested in sex before sexual activity or closeness begins. The desire appears without needing an immediate intimate situation.

Responsive desire means desire may develop in an already wanted, comfortable intimate context rather than appearing beforehand (International Society for Sexual Medicine, 2022).

These describe possibilities, not two permanent kinds of people.

You might experience spontaneous desire sometimes and responsive desire at other times. Your interest might also be absent altogether, regardless of the setting.

Crucially, responsive desire isn’t permission to push past reluctance.

It doesn’t mean you should start sex when you don’t want it, hoping you’ll eventually enjoy yourself. It doesn’t mean your partner should continue touching you after you’ve said no because desire might appear.

Sexual activity requires freely given, ongoing consent. A physical response, including arousal, doesn’t establish consent (Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network, 2026).

You can enjoy being close to your partner without knowing whether you will become interested in anything sexual.

You can also decide that you don’t want to find out.

What if you almost never feel interested first?

Suppose you rarely experience spontaneous desire. Your partner sometimes assumes that means you’re not attracted to them.

You can explain what you know about your experience.

“I don’t often think about sex out of nowhere. That doesn’t necessarily mean I’m unhappy with you.”

If responsive desire describes your experience, you can say so. But you don’t have to use the term, and you shouldn’t promise that desire will appear.

Your partner can listen without treating your explanation as a procedure for making you want sex.

If your desire has changed recently, other questions may be relevant. Stress, medications, illness, hormonal changes, and life circumstances can affect sexual wellbeing (A.D.A.M. Editorial Team, 2024; International Society for Sexual Medicine, 2022).

A change isn’t automatically a relationship problem. But if it worries you, hurts, or interferes with something you want, medical advice may be useful.

Your experience deserves attention without becoming an obligation to produce desire on demand.

When one person wants more than the other

Daniel, 47, has been married to Mateo, 49, for six years.

Daniel would like sex more frequently. Mateo enjoys their relationship and feels attracted to Daniel, but he is generally less interested in sex.

At first, Daniel takes this personally. He wonders whether Mateo still finds him attractive.

Mateo feels that every conversation about sex ends with Daniel disappointed. Eventually, Mateo begins avoiding the subject altogether.

Neither feels understood.

Differences in sexual desire are common enough to be a subject of relationship research, but they don’t affect every couple the same way. In a study of 229 committed couples using daily diaries and a 12-month follow-up, greater differences in partners’ sexual desire predicted greater later sexual distress (Jodouin et al., 2021).

That finding doesn’t mean every couple with different desires is unhappy.

In a qualitative study of 300 adults reporting differences in sexual or affectionate desire, some considered the mismatch a problem while others did not. The study also distinguished wanting more sex from wanting more affection, which aren’t necessarily the same issue (Clark et al., 2024).

For Daniel and Mateo, the first useful change is to stop treating every discussion as a negotiation over when sex will happen next.

Daniel explains that he misses feeling sexually connected. Mateo explains that he sometimes hesitates to initiate affection because he worries Daniel will expect sex.

They begin discussing what each of them misses, wants, and feels comfortable offering.

They don’t need to produce an immediate solution.

Desire is not a debt

A higher-desire partner isn’t wrong for wanting sex. A lower-desire partner isn’t wrong for not wanting it.

You can express disappointment without blaming someone for having a different level of interest. You can express your limits without apologizing for possessing them.

The important distinction is between wanting something and being entitled to receive it.

No one owes their partner sex because they’re married, because they had sex last week, because their partner is frustrated, or because they’ve been together for years.

And nobody has to pretend that a substantial difference in desire is easy to live with.

A relationship can contain a genuine incompatibility without containing a villain.

The decision is whether the relationship, as both people are willing to live it, meets their needs.

Affection and sexual desire can be separate mismatches

Imagine a couple who agree that they enjoy their sex life, but one person wants to hug and cuddle much more frequently than the other.

Their difference isn’t primarily about sex.

Another couple might have plenty of affection but disagree about how important sexual connection is to their relationship.

If both problems are treated as identical, the discussion can go nowhere.

A useful distinction is:

“Are you missing sexual experiences, affection, or the feeling of being wanted?”

The answer might be more than one.

You can also ask what kinds of closeness are mutually enjoyable now, without trying to make them substitutes the other person must accept.

A date night, a long conversation, a cuddle, and sex may all matter, but they aren’t interchangeable items in an account that must balance.

Saying no without losing closeness

You are allowed to decline sexual activity with someone you love.

Your partner is allowed to decline sexual activity with you.

Those statements remain true when the person asking is kind, when the relationship is healthy, and when the other person feels disappointed.

Research comparing different responses to unwanted sexual opportunities found that kindly declining could protect relationship satisfaction better than engaging in sex primarily to avoid disappointing a partner. The findings were more complicated for sexual satisfaction, and they don’t mean anyone must make a refusal affectionate or comforting (Kim et al., 2018).

One way to decline is simply:

“Not tonight. I’m not interested in sex.”

If it feels genuine, you might add:

“I’d still like to spend time with you.”

But that second sentence is optional. You don’t have to offer a consolation activity, explain your reasons, or promise sex later.

The receiving partner can respond:

“Okay. Thanks for telling me.”

They may still feel disappointed. Feelings aren’t violations. What matters is what someone does with them.

Disappointment doesn’t justify repeated requests, punishment, withdrawing affection to make someone reconsider, or treating the answer as a challenge to overcome.

A study of couples transitioning to parenthood found associations between understanding a partner’s lack of sexual desire and relationship satisfaction. The results varied across some outcomes and concerned a particular life stage, so they shouldn’t be converted into universal rules about gender (Muise et al., 2017).

The broader practical principle is straightforward: you can care about your own needs while respecting another person’s answer.

If you recognize yourself applying pressure

Perhaps you’ve responded to a refusal by arguing, asking repeatedly, or making your partner feel responsible for your disappointment.

Stop that behavior.

Don’t defend it by explaining how much the rejection hurt. Your feelings may need attention, but they don’t give you control over another person’s body.

Accept the boundary without reopening the request in that moment. If this is a repeated pattern, seek appropriate professional help to change it.

The chapters on consent and sexual safety explain these responsibilities in greater detail. A relationship doesn’t create an exception to them.

When words become difficult

Sometimes a person realizes they’re uncomfortable only after an interaction has started. Sometimes overload makes speech difficult, or a shutdown makes it hard to answer questions.

A partner who becomes quiet, withdrawn, or unable to respond clearly isn’t giving permission to continue.

Pause. Don’t rely on an earlier yes to interpret the present moment.

Couples can discuss accessible ways to communicate boundaries ahead of time, but no communication agreement should be treated as permission to ignore uncertainty.

When you don’t know whether someone wants an activity to continue, stop and check. If they cannot freely and clearly communicate agreement, don’t proceed.

Your body deserves consideration too

Not every sexual difficulty is about communication or compatibility.

Some people experience pain, discomfort, changes in desire, or difficulties with sexual functioning. These can have multiple contributors, including medical conditions, medications, stress, and other circumstances (A.D.A.M. Editorial Team, 2024; International Society for Sexual Medicine, 2022).

You don’t need to identify the cause yourself.

You also don’t need to keep participating in something that hurts while you search for an explanation.

If sexual activity is painful, stop the activity. If pain persists, changes suddenly, or concerns you, contact an appropriate healthcare professional. Seek prompt medical attention for severe pain or injury.

A clinician can help assess possible physical causes and discuss suitable options. A qualified sexual-health professional may also be helpful when distress continues.

Pain isn’t a test of commitment. Neither is the ability to have an erection, experience lubrication, reach orgasm, or perform a particular sexual activity.

Your worth as a partner doesn’t depend on producing a specific physical response.

And a physical response isn’t evidence that you wanted what happened. Sexual consent must still be freely given (Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network, 2026).

If your attention, desire, and body seem out of sync

Some adults find that they want sexual intimacy but have trouble staying mentally engaged. Others notice that desire fluctuates substantially or that their bodies don’t respond as expected.

A systematic review of ADHD and sexuality found some group-level differences in desire, sexual satisfaction, and sexual functioning. But the underlying studies were relatively few, small, and varied in quality (Soldati et al., 2020).

The review doesn’t establish that everyone with ADHD has a particular pattern of desire or that any one accommodation will solve the problem.

If attention is difficult, you can talk about what helps you remain comfortable and present. That might involve fewer distractions, less pressure to follow an expected sequence, or choosing a time when you have more energy.

These are suggestions to explore, not treatments supported by controlled trials.

If changes in sexual functioning concern you, particularly when they occur alongside medication changes or other health concerns, discuss them with your healthcare professional rather than changing prescribed treatment yourself.

Bodies, identities, and feeling comfortable being seen

Intimacy may involve allowing someone to see parts of you that you usually keep private.

That can feel exciting, comforting, exposing, or some combination.

Your comfort with your body doesn’t have to match your partner’s expectations. You may prefer certain words, kinds of touch, clothing, or levels of privacy.

In the qualitative study of autistic and non-autistic adults, some participants described gender dysphoria, pain, or other experiences that affected physical intimacy. The study wasn’t designed to establish how often these experiences occur among transgender or nonbinary people (Sala et al., 2024).

Perspective: Non-binary

If someone uses words for your body, gender, or sexual role that don’t fit how you understand yourself, you can tell them what language you prefer. You don’t have to accept a description because a partner finds it familiar or flattering.

You may also have preferences about which parts of your body you want touched, seen, or discussed. These preferences deserve the same respect as any other boundary. You don’t have to explain your gender identity or medical history to justify them.

Gender-neutral language in a relationship is useful when it matches what the people involved want. Some people prefer gendered language. The goal is not to enforce the same words for everyone, but to learn which words feel respectful to your partner.

The same principle applies to body image more generally.

You might feel self-conscious about scars, weight, mobility, body hair, aging, or how your body responds during intimacy.

You can tell someone what helps you feel comfortable. You can also ask them not to comment on a feature you feel sensitive about.

A partner can appreciate your body without assuming that appreciation erases your discomfort.

And you can care about someone deeply without having the knowledge or ability to resolve every insecurity they experience.

When past experiences make intimacy complicated

Some people find particular kinds of intimacy difficult because of earlier experiences. A touch, situation, or expectation may bring up feelings that don’t seem to fit what’s happening now.

You don’t need a diagnosis to recognize that something feels uncomfortable or unsafe.

You also don’t need to force yourself to continue an activity simply because your present partner hasn’t done anything wrong.

It’s possible for a partner to have good intentions and for the activity still to be something you don’t want.

The responsibility is to respect the present boundary, not to demand a complete explanation of its origins.

You might say:

“I want to stop. I don’t feel comfortable continuing.”

Or:

“I care about you, but I need more space right now.”

A respectful partner doesn’t require a detailed history before accepting that answer.

If earlier sexual experiences have affected your sense of safety, professional support may help you understand your responses. The next chapters on trauma in relationships and finding support address those concerns more fully.

The important point here is that closeness shouldn’t require you to ignore your own distress.

A safety concern worth naming

Not every uncomfortable sexual experience is a matter of two people failing to communicate.

If you’re being pressured, coerced, or touched without your freely given consent, that is a safety issue. A partner’s affection, neurotype, emotional distress, or desire doesn’t excuse the behavior.

A Canadian study comparing 95 autistic adults with 117 non-autistic adults found lower measured sexual knowledge and higher self-reported experiences of unwanted sexual contact and coercion among the autistic participants (Brown-Lavoie et al., 2014).

The study doesn’t prove why those differences occurred. Nor does it mean that autistic people are incapable of recognizing harm or protecting themselves.

A broader review identified gaps in accessible sexual education and reported vulnerability to sexual victimization, while emphasizing the variation in autistic people’s sexual experiences (Motamed et al., 2025).

The lesson isn’t to become suspicious of everyone or to treat neurodivergence as helplessness. It’s that accessible information and clear boundaries matter.

If you’ve been pressured or harmed, the chapters on sexual assault and recognizing abuse explain safety and support options. For someone facing an immediate threat, getting to safety takes priority over discussing the relationship.

No relationship conversation is a substitute for safety.

What if your needs really don’t match?

Sometimes two people discuss their preferences, respect each other’s boundaries, make reasonable adjustments, and still discover a major difference.

Perhaps sex is essential to one person’s vision of a romantic relationship, while the other doesn’t want a sexual relationship.

Perhaps one partner needs frequent affectionate touch while the other finds that level of contact uncomfortable.

Perhaps a couple has spent considerable time searching for an arrangement both enjoy and hasn’t found one.

There may be no mutually satisfying solution.

That doesn’t mean the relationship was fake. It doesn’t mean someone failed to love enough. And it doesn’t mean either person must change their orientation, desire, or limits.

Love doesn’t erase compatibility questions.

You can recognize that a partner’s needs are legitimate without agreeing to meet them. You can recognize that your own needs are important without turning them into demands.

Some couples choose a relationship with less sex or different forms of affection than they once expected. Others discover that they cannot both be satisfied with the relationship they’re willing to share.

Those decisions belong to the people involved.

What isn’t an acceptable solution is ongoing pressure, reluctant compliance, or pretending that someone has freely agreed when they haven’t.

If both people want to explore the problem further, a qualified professional may be able to help them discuss their options. That doesn’t guarantee the relationship will change in the way either person hopes.

Sometimes understanding each other reveals a workable compromise. Sometimes it reveals an incompatibility that was difficult to acknowledge.

Clarity is useful in either case.

A small exercise: Your intimacy map

You can do this privately. You don’t need to show your answers to anyone, and you don’t need to have a partner to complete it.

Think about five questions:

  1. What helps you feel emotionally close? Think about conversation, shared activities, reassurance, quiet company, or anything else that matters to you.
  2. What physical affection do you enjoy? Include forms of touch that have nothing to do with sex.
  3. What do you dislike or need to limit? Consider sensory discomfort, privacy, timing, fatigue, and unwanted expectations.
  4. What does sex mean to you, if anything? Is it an important form of connection, something you enjoy occasionally, something you’re uncertain about, or something you don’t want?
  5. What would help you communicate your preferences? You might prefer speaking, writing, taking time to answer, or talking away from an intimate situation.

If you’re in a relationship, you can invite your partner to consider the same questions independently.

Then compare only what you’re both comfortable sharing.

Try to understand the answers before looking for solutions. If your partner wants something you don’t, you can acknowledge its importance without agreeing to provide it.

Your answers can change with experience, health, circumstances, or new understanding. This isn’t a lifetime contract.

It’s a way to make your preferences easier to see.

Closeness that leaves room for two people

A healthy intimate relationship doesn’t require identical desires, matching sensory preferences, or the ability to anticipate everything your partner wants.

It requires room for two people to have different experiences without either person’s consent becoming less important.

You can love someone and need space. You can want affection without wanting sex. You can want sex and accept that your partner doesn’t. You can change your mind halfway through an intimate moment, and your partner can do the same.

You can also admit that something important isn’t working.

The goal isn’t to make every difference disappear. It’s to understand which differences you can comfortably live with, which you can adapt to together, and which may make the relationship unsuitable.

Sometimes that begins with an ordinary sentence:

“I like being close to you. Can we talk about what that means for each of us?”

The next chapter looks more closely at how partners with different ways of thinking, communicating, and processing the world can learn to understand one another. Intimacy is one place where those differences become visible, but neither partner has the complete view.

And when understanding each other feels difficult, you don’t have to figure it out without support.
