Morgan had a good first date on Saturday. The conversation was easy, they laughed together, and both said they would like to meet again.

On Sunday morning, Morgan sends a message:

“Yesterday was fun. I’d like to see you again.”

By Sunday evening, there is no reply.

Morgan starts reviewing the date. Maybe the other person was only being polite. Maybe the message sounded desperate. Maybe replying the next morning was too soon. Maybe the other person met somebody better.

Meanwhile, the other person has been helping a relative move. Their phone is somewhere in a bag. They haven’t read the message.

Morgan doesn’t know that. Morgan knows only that no reply has arrived.

This is one of the problems with texting: you can see what someone sends, but not everything happening around the message. A few words, a delay, or a punctuation mark can start a much larger story in your head.

Sometimes that story is right. Sometimes it isn’t.

Texting is still a useful way to build a connection. You can share something funny, make plans, express affection, or have a conversation without both people being available at the same moment. For some people, writing also makes communication much easier.

The skill is not learning to interpret every message perfectly. It is learning what a message can tell you, what it cannot tell you, and how to communicate without making either person guess unnecessarily.

A reply is not a stopwatch measuring affection

One person replies within five minutes. Another replies after work. Someone else reads a message, thinks about the answer, and returns to it the next morning.

None of those patterns, by itself, tells you exactly how interested someone is.

Reply timing can influence impressions, though.

In a 2026 experiment, 543 heterosexual adults in the United States and United Kingdom imagined having a first date and receiving a follow-up text. Participants reported more interest in continuing a relationship when the first message arrived the next morning than when it arrived immediately or two days later (Teichmann et al., 2026).

That finding needs careful interpretation.

The researchers tested an imagined situation, not actual dating outcomes. They studied when someone initiated the first message after a date, not how quickly someone replied during an ongoing conversation. The study also did not establish a rule for older adults, queer couples, or people whose communication needs differ.

It certainly does not mean that you should deliberately delay a message to make someone want you more.

There is no research-backed number of minutes or hours you must wait before texting someone you like.

If you enjoyed a date and want to say so, you can say so. You do not need to perform indifference.

If you need time to think, sleep, work, or recover your energy before answering, that is also reasonable.

The important distinction is between timing and meaning.

A slow reply is something you can observe. Why the person replied slowly is something you may not know.

When delays start to matter

Suppose you have been talking with someone for three weeks. They often disappear for several days, rarely answer questions, and never help make plans.

You do not have to decide whether they are busy, forgetful, uncertain, or uninterested before deciding whether this arrangement works for you.

You can look at the whole pattern.

Are they making time for the connection? Do they follow through on plans? Can the two of you agree on a way to communicate that works?

A single delayed reply gives you very little information. A repeated pattern can tell you something about how the relationship is functioning, even when you do not know the reason behind it.

You may like someone and still find that the amount of contact they can offer is not enough for you.

You may also genuinely care about someone and be unable to maintain the amount of contact they want.

Neither person needs to be the villain for that difference to matter.

Two people can have different texting rhythms

For Maya, 36, texting is a way to stay connected during the day. She likes sending short updates about things she sees, hearing about her partner’s afternoon, and exchanging a message before bed.

Her girlfriend, Denise, 43, enjoys their conversations but rarely looks at her phone while working. She prefers one longer exchange in the evening.

Maya initially interprets the quiet afternoons as distance. Denise feels puzzled because she believes their evening conversations are going well.

Neither has explained what ordinary texting means to them.

Studies of couples suggest that messaging frequency and relationship satisfaction are not connected in one simple way.

In a Canadian survey of 647 young adults, more frequent and responsive texting was associated with greater relationship satisfaction among people in long-distance relationships, but not among those who lived geographically close to their partners (Holtzman et al., 2021).

Another study of 302 partnered university students found that people reporting greater attachment anxiety tended to want more messages than they received and to reply faster than their partners. Satisfaction with texting frequency mattered more to relationship quality among those participants (Patel et al., 2022).

These were associations, not proof that texting causes satisfaction or that wanting frequent contact means someone has a particular attachment type.

There is no universal right amount of texting.

Instead of counting messages, Maya and Denise could compare expectations.

Maya: “I like hearing from you during the day. Is that something you enjoy, or does it interrupt your work?”

Denise: “I like getting your messages. I usually can’t answer until evening, though.”

Maya: “That helps. I was sometimes wondering whether you wanted to talk.”

Denise: “I do. I’m just not looking at my phone much during the day.”

They haven’t agreed that Denise must always answer. They have made the silence less mysterious.

Three things worth discussing

You do not need a formal conversation about every texting habit. But when a relationship starts becoming regular, these questions can help:

  1. Frequency: Do you enjoy frequent small messages, occasional longer conversations, or something in between?
  2. Response time: Are there parts of the day when you are usually unavailable? Does either of you need more time to think before responding?
  3. Practical messages: How will you handle messages that need an answer for plans, travel, or other time-sensitive decisions?

The answers can change with work, health, family responsibilities, and how the relationship develops.

Treat them as agreements to revisit, not permanent rules.

When writing is easier than speaking

Imagine that someone asks you a serious question face-to-face.

You understand the words. You may even know how you feel. But finding a clear answer while another person waits is difficult.

Twenty minutes later, you know exactly what you wanted to say.

For someone with that experience, texting can provide breathing room.

A British study asked 245 autistic respondents about communication preferences across different situations. Participants often preferred email when contacting unfamiliar people or organizations, while text messaging and face-to-face communication ranked highly with people they knew well (Howard & Sedgewick, 2021).

In their written explanations, participants described several advantages of written communication:

  • Time to think and find the words they wanted.
  • Less pressure to respond immediately.
  • A record they could reread.
  • More control over the environment in which they communicated.

The researchers also found that preferences depended on who the person was talking to and how comfortable they felt.

This was a self-selected UK survey, not a study of dating couples. It does not mean every autistic person prefers texting, or that face-to-face communication is inherently harder for them.

Some people enjoy calls. Others find them exhausting. Some are comfortable talking to a trusted partner but prefer writing when discussing unfamiliar or emotional topics.

The useful question is not “What should someone with this diagnosis prefer?”

It is “What helps this person communicate well?”

If you need time to find your words

You might be able to explain yourself much more clearly in writing than during a fast conversation. You do not have to apologize for having a communication preference.

You could say, “Sometimes I need time to put my thoughts into words. For important conversations, writing first helps me explain myself better.”

You can also ask for time during an in-person conversation. Needing a pause does not mean you are avoiding the question. You are responsible for following through if you have agreed to return to the conversation.

If you're dating someone who needs time to answer

A preference for written communication does not automatically mean someone is less interested in emotional closeness. For some people, writing makes closeness easier because they can express what they actually mean.

Ask which situations are easier in writing and which work better in person. You can explain your own preferences too. If you enjoy hearing someone’s voice, it is reasonable to say that, but a phone call should not become a test of whether they care about you.

This is the double empathy problem in an ordinary setting. Two people may be communicating differently without either one being unwilling to connect.

Understanding that difference works better than requiring one person to communicate exactly like the other.

The message you meant is not always the message they read

Consider these two messages:

“That’s fine.”

“That’s fine!”

Are they different?

Perhaps. One person may use an exclamation point to express enthusiasm. Another may use it out of habit. Someone else may rarely use punctuation at all.

Now consider:

“Sure.”

Was that enthusiastic agreement, reluctant agreement, or simply a short answer?

Without more information, you may not know.

What the evidence says about tone

A series of five experiments published in 2005 found that people sending emails tended to overestimate how accurately their intended tone came across, especially when communicating things such as humor or sarcasm (Kruger et al., 2005).

One explanation is that the writer already knows how the words are supposed to sound. The reader does not have access to the writer’s imagined voice.

But that does not mean people are generally terrible at understanding written messages.

A more recent two-study investigation compared how senders intended the emotional tone of everyday messages with how recipients understood them. The researchers found that senders and recipients were often quite accurate in identifying whether the messages were positive or negative (Pollmann & Roos, 2025).

The two findings are not necessarily contradictory.

The older experiments tested particular kinds of tone communication. The newer research examined broad positive or negative emotional tone in familiar communication settings.

Recognizing that a message feels positive is not the same as knowing whether someone is flirting, making a joke, or agreeing reluctantly.

Texting can communicate tone well, but it cannot guarantee that every intention will be understood.

Make important meanings easier to find

You do not need to write every message with perfect precision. Playfulness, jokes, abbreviations, and casual language are all fine when both people enjoy them.

For something important, however, clarity is often more useful than cleverness.

Compare:

“Whatever you want.”

With:

“I don’t have a strong preference between the two restaurants. I’d be happy with either.”

The second message tells the reader much more.

Or compare:

“Guess I’ll see you sometime.”

With:

“I’d like to see you again. Are you interested?”

The second message offers a question the other person can actually answer.

If you are uncertain about someone else’s meaning, you can ask:

“Did you mean that as a joke? I’m having trouble telling from the message.”

Or:

“When you said you’re fine with either option, did you mean you really don’t have a preference?”

Asking about tone is not an accusation. It is an alternative to treating your first interpretation as a fact.

What about emojis?

Emojis can provide useful emotional information, but they are not a universal dictionary.

A US experiment involving 260 adults found that fictional messages from close friends were rated as more responsive when they included emojis that matched the message’s content. Participants also rated the imagined relationship more positively (Huh, 2025).

The experiment concerned fictional conversations between friends. It did not show that adding emojis improves real romantic relationships.

Use them when they feel natural and when the other person understands them.

Do not assume that someone who rarely uses emojis is cold, or that someone who sends hearts necessarily wants a romantic relationship.

An emoji is part of a message, not a substitute for knowing what the relationship is.

When you forget to reply

Alex reads a message while waiting in line at the grocery store.

His date asks whether Saturday afternoon would work.

Alex thinks, “Saturday should be fine. I’ll check my calendar when I get home.”

At home, he puts groceries away, starts cooking, and gets absorbed in another task.

The message disappears from his attention.

Three days later, he remembers.

Now he feels embarrassed. He worries that replying so late will seem rude, so he puts it off again.

From the other person’s perspective, Alex was asked a reasonable question and did not answer.

Both experiences matter.

Forgetting a message does not necessarily mean you don’t care. But the other person still has to make plans without an answer.

Research on ADHD and romantic relationships has documented relationship difficulties while identifying important gaps in understanding exactly how attention and executive-function differences contribute to them. The available review does not establish that forgetting romantic text messages is a specific cause of relationship problems (Wymbs et al., 2021).

That distinction matters. There is no basis for diagnosing someone’s attention or intentions from their texting habits.

Still, if you recognize Alex’s experience, you can try changing the system you use instead of relying only on remembering.

If you read messages and then forget them

You may open a message when you cannot give it your attention, intend to return later, and lose track of it. ADHD can be one possible explanation for difficulties with attention and organization, but you do not need a diagnosis to use a reminder.

Try leaving messages unread until you can answer, setting a reminder for a specific time, or adding an important reply to your calendar or task list. Choose one method that fits how you already organize your day.

If you forget, acknowledge it and answer the original question. A long explanation is usually less useful than a genuine response and a practical change.

For Alex, that could mean:

“Sorry I missed your message. I saw it while I was out and forgot to come back to it. Saturday afternoon works for me, if you’re still available.”

The message acknowledges what happened without expecting the other person to pretend it was not inconvenient.

If Alex repeatedly forgets, he needs something more reliable than repeating the same apology.

When you need more time but haven’t forgotten

Sometimes you remember the message perfectly well. You simply do not have the energy or information to answer properly.

A short acknowledgment can help, when you are able to send one:

“I saw your question. I need to check my schedule, and I’ll answer tomorrow.”

Or:

“I want to think about that before replying. Can I get back to you this evening?”

Only promise a particular time if you can reasonably follow through.

You also do not need to send a preliminary acknowledgment to every casual message. That could turn texting into a second job.

The purpose is to make important communication more reliable, not to make yourself available every moment.

If you’re dating someone who often forgets

It is reasonable to believe someone when they say they forgot.

It is also reasonable to notice how repeated missed messages affect you.

Try describing the practical problem rather than assigning a motive.

“When I don’t hear back about plans, I’m not sure whether to keep the time available. Could we agree on a way to handle that?”

Perhaps the two of you decide that invitations should include a date by which an answer is needed. Perhaps the other person prefers calendar invitations for plans.

If the arrangement still does not work, you are allowed to reconsider it.

Understanding why someone struggles does not require accepting every consequence of the struggle. Equally, wanting reliable communication does not entitle you to constant access to another person.

Affection and everyday connection

Texting does not have to be about logistics.

A message can communicate appreciation, curiosity, or the simple fact that someone was on your mind.

In a survey of 276 US young adults in committed relationships, affectionate texting was associated with greater reported attachment to a partner, although other findings about texting and relationship quality were mixed (Schade et al., 2013).

The study was correlational. It cannot establish whether affectionate texts strengthened relationships, whether people in closer relationships sent more affectionate texts, or both.

Still, affectionate communication is something you can practice without treating it as a formula.

“You mentioned your interview was today. Hope it went well.”

“I had a really good time talking with you yesterday.”

“That song you told me about came on while I was driving. I liked it.”

These messages do not demand a particular response. They share a genuine thought.

The most useful messages usually sound like the person sending them, not a script copied from somebody promising to make strangers fall in love.

Match the conversation, not a quota

Suppose Harper sends a detailed paragraph about a book they are enjoying. Their date answers with a thoughtful question.

A longer exchange makes sense.

Later, the same date writes, “Just finished work. Exhausted.”

That might call for a short response rather than another long conversation.

This builds on the earlier chapters about conversation and sharing interests. The same attention to whether another person has space to participate applies in text.

If you enjoy long written explanations, you can ask:

“I have a lot of thoughts about this. Want the longer version?”

If your date enjoys those conversations, there is no reason to force every exchange into three sentences.

If they prefer shorter exchanges, that is useful information too.

Good communication does not require identical styles. It requires enough flexibility for both people to participate willingly.

When a text conversation becomes difficult

Imagine that two people disagree about a canceled plan.

One sends a detailed explanation. The other interprets it as criticism. The first person then writes several more messages trying to clarify.

Soon they are discussing not only the canceled plan but also the meaning of each other’s words.

Should they stop texting and talk in person?

Not necessarily.

In a small US experiment, 43 couples were randomly assigned to discuss a conflict either face-to-face or through a messaging app. Researchers observed fewer positive conflict behaviors in the text group, but they did not find detectable differences in negative behavior, perceived progress toward resolution, or reported emotional responses (Ruppel et al., 2021).

That is not proof that text is equally good for every difficult conversation. The study was small, and a failure to detect a difference does not establish that none exists.

It does suggest that a rule such as “never discuss serious matters by text” is too simple.

For some people, a face-to-face conversation makes emotional meaning clearer.

For others, especially when they need time to find words, writing may make it possible to explain themselves carefully.

The better question is whether the current conversation is helping.

Choose a medium that gives both people a fair chance

If text exchanges are becoming confusing, you can offer an alternative:

“I’m having trouble explaining this clearly by text. Would you be comfortable talking about it when we see each other?”

But if your partner needs writing, they might reasonably answer:

“I can explain myself better in messages. Could we keep writing, but take more time between replies?”

Neither preference automatically wins.

You might agree to exchange written thoughts first and talk afterward. Or to continue writing without expecting immediate responses.

If one person needs a pause, the other should not treat that as permission to keep demanding answers.

Research on delayed replies during imagined romantic conflicts found that people expected quicker responses during conflict and reported negative feelings when those expectations were violated (Huang & Yao, 2024).

That helps explain why waiting can feel especially uncomfortable during a disagreement. It does not establish that a delayed response is deliberate punishment.

You can communicate a pause clearly:

“I need to stop this conversation for tonight. I can return to it tomorrow.”

If someone has agreed to return, following through matters.

A pause is different from disappearing without explanation whenever a difficult subject arises.

Sometimes a misunderstanding will already have happened. The next chapter deals with recognizing and repairing those moments, rather than trying to settle every conflict immediately.

When waiting for a reply makes you anxious

You send a message at lunch.

At 2 p.m., you check your phone.

At 2:15, you check again.

By dinner, you have begun imagining rejection.

The absence of a message has become the center of your afternoon.

Waiting can feel particularly difficult when you care about the person or are uncertain about the relationship. Studies of attachment and messaging have found associations between attachment anxiety, wanting more contact, and sensitivity to reply patterns (Patel et al., 2022).

Those findings do not tell you why any particular person feels anxious. In this chapter’s evidence, there is no direct study establishing that trauma causes anxiety about dating-text response times.

Whatever the reason for your feelings, you can learn to separate the available information from the explanation you are imagining.

Separate the fact from the story

Try three questions.

1. What do I actually know?

“I sent a message yesterday afternoon. They haven’t replied.”

2. What explanation am I adding?

“They must have lost interest.”

“They’re deliberately ignoring me.”

“I said something embarrassing.”

Those are possible interpretations, not established facts.

3. What can I reasonably do?

Perhaps nothing yet. You can continue your ordinary activities and allow time for a reply.

If enough time has passed that a follow-up would be reasonable in the context of your existing conversation, and contact is still welcome, you can send one simple message.

“Just checking whether you’d still like to get together this weekend. No problem if not.”

Then leave the decision with the other person.

There is no scientifically established number of hours after which a follow-up becomes appropriate. Context matters, including how much you have spoken, whether you had made plans, and what the person has already communicated.

A second message is not permission to start sending messages indefinitely.

If someone has declined, asked for no contact, or blocked you, stop contacting them. Do not look for another way to reach them.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly messaging someone who does not want contact, stop and seek support for managing the feelings driving that behavior.

Silence can still be an answer about your choices

You do not have to interpret every silence as rejection.

But you also do not have to wait indefinitely for someone who is not participating.

Suppose you invited somebody to a movie on Friday. The show is Saturday, and they have not answered.

You can make different plans.

That does not require deciding that they are a bad person. You are simply not going to reserve your entire weekend around an unanswered invitation.

If they reply later, you can decide whether you are still interested and available.

You are responsible for your choices. They are responsible for theirs.

Neither person owes the other a romantic relationship, and receiving a message does not create an obligation to answer immediately.

Planning messages need a little more clarity

There is a difference between sending someone a funny photo and asking whether they can meet you at the train station.

The second message has a practical consequence if nobody answers.

When you need information by a particular time, say so.

“Would you like to go to the art museum Saturday? I need to buy tickets by Thursday evening, so let me know before then if you’re interested.”

That tells the other person what you need without demanding an answer on your schedule for every conversation.

If there is no reply by Thursday, do not buy a ticket on their behalf and assume they agreed.

You can make your own plan.

For a genuine emergency or urgent safety issue, ordinary dating-text expectations are not an adequate communication plan. Use the appropriate emergency or immediate-support channel rather than relying on someone to notice a message.

For ordinary arrangements, it is worth agreeing on what counts as time-sensitive.

Someone who prefers infrequent social texting might still be happy to answer practical questions promptly. Someone who sends dozens of casual messages might be unavailable during work.

These distinctions are easier to manage when they are spoken about rather than guessed.

When texting feels like an obligation

It is pleasant to receive messages from someone you like.

It is less pleasant when every notification feels like a demand to prove that you care.

Consider Jordan and Casey, both in their forties. Jordan enjoys voice messages because speaking is easier than typing. Casey finds unexpected audio difficult to process and prefers written messages that can be reread.

Neither preference is unreasonable.

A workable exchange might be:

Jordan: “I like sending voice notes, but I know you prefer text. Would occasional voice notes be okay?”

Casey: “Sometimes, yes. For plans or important questions, please write it out.”

Jordan: “That works. If I send something you can’t listen to, you can ask me to type it.”

That conversation does not require either person to explain a diagnosis.

The same principle applies to notification sounds, read receipts, calling without warning, late-night messages, and how often someone checks their phone.

A preference can be shared without becoming a demand.

And you should notice when the other person’s requests stop being invitations to cooperate and start becoming pressure.

Being expected to report your whereabouts, prove who you are speaking to, surrender access to your messages, or answer immediately to avoid someone’s anger is not the same as ordinary communication reliability.

The later chapters on boundaries and recognizing manipulation examine these differences more fully.

For now, remember that getting close to someone does not mean giving them unlimited access to your attention or private communications.

The four messages worth knowing

You do not need a collection of elaborate texting techniques.

A few clear messages cover many everyday situations.

Situation Something you can say
You enjoyed spending time together. “I had a good time today. I’d like to see you again.”
You need more time to answer. “I want to think about that. Can I reply tomorrow?”
You forgot a message. “Sorry I missed this. I forgot to come back to it. Here’s my answer…”
You want to understand their preferences. “What kind of texting works best for you? I usually reply in the evenings.”

These aren’t required phrases. Change them to sound natural to you.

Notice what they have in common: each one says something real. None requires the other person to guess, and none attempts to produce a particular emotional reaction.

A small exercise: your texting expectations

Think about the last time you felt uneasy because of a message, or the absence of one.

Write down three sentences:

  1. “When someone doesn’t reply, my first assumption is ______.”
  2. “When I don’t reply, it’s usually because ______.”
  3. “A reasonable thing I could tell someone about my texting habits is ______.”

Now imagine dating someone whose answers to the first two questions are different from yours.

Would they necessarily be wrong?

Would you necessarily be wrong?

Probably not. But those differences might need a conversation.

If you are getting to know someone now, choose one small preference you can communicate clearly. Perhaps you prefer texting to calling, need time before answering complicated questions, or like to know when plans are confirmed.

You don’t need to explain your entire communication history.

One useful sentence is enough to begin.

The person matters more than the notification

Morgan eventually receives a reply to the Sunday message.

“Sorry, spent the whole day helping my sister move. I’d love to see you again. Are you free Thursday?”

All the imagined explanations about rejection suddenly look different.

But the lesson is not that every delayed reply will have a happy explanation.

Sometimes a person really is losing interest. Sometimes they want less contact than you do. Sometimes they care but are not able to communicate as reliably as you need.

You cannot determine all of that from the time beside a message.

You can pay attention to how someone communicates over time, explain your own needs, ask reasonable questions, and decide whether the connection works for both of you.

Texting is a way to communicate with another person, not a puzzle you must solve to earn their affection.

The next skill is knowing what to do when your meanings genuinely do not match: when something you said hurts someone, something they said confuses you, or a misunderstanding needs to be repaired.
