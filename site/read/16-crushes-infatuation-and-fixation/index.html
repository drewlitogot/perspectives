Chapter 16: Crushes, infatuation and fixation

Leila is 39. She meets Sonia at a friend’s birthday party. They spend twenty minutes talking about books, work, and a terrible apartment they both once rented. Sonia laughs at Leila’s jokes. Before leaving, they exchange numbers.

By bedtime, Leila has replayed the conversation several times.

By the next afternoon, she has imagined their first date. By the weekend, she has imagined meeting Sonia’s family, sharing an apartment, and spending Sunday mornings together.

Sonia has sent two friendly messages.

Leila knows almost nothing about her.

The feelings are real. The imagined relationship is not.

There is nothing embarrassing about developing a crush. Attraction can make an ordinary Tuesday much more interesting. You might smile when someone’s name appears on your phone. You might remember an insignificant comment for weeks. You might spend a little extra time getting ready because you hope to see them.

But sometimes a crush begins occupying more and more of your attention. The person becomes the first thing you think about when you wake up and the last thing you think about before sleeping. A friendly message feels wonderful. A delayed reply feels devastating.

Eventually, you may be spending more time thinking about the relationship than actually participating in one.

The important question is not how strongly you feel. It is how accurately you understand the situation, how much the feelings are affecting your life, and what you choose to do about them.

A crush, infatuation, and limerence are not quite the same thing

A crush is an ordinary word for romantic attraction, often toward someone you do not know very well. You enjoy thinking about them. You hope they might be interested in you.

Infatuation describes especially intense early attraction. You might feel excited, distracted, physically drawn to someone, or unusually eager to spend time with them.

Neither word describes a disorder. Both can be part of an enjoyable experience.

Researchers studying romantic love describe strong motivation to be close to another person, focused attention, idealization, and sometimes intrusive thoughts. Intrusive thoughts are thoughts that appear even when you would rather concentrate on something else. These experiences can happen during early mutual romantic love, not just one-sided attraction (Bode & Kushnick, 2021).

Limerence is a research term for a more consuming experience: involuntary preoccupation with someone, intense longing for their feelings to be returned, and difficulty disengaging from thoughts about them.

It may involve an imagined relationship that feels much more developed than the real one. The person may occupy your attention even when you are trying to work, rest, or spend time with friends.

Researchers have recently developed a questionnaire measuring two aspects of limerence: an intense need for attachment, and neglect of yourself or other people while focused on the desired relationship (Marshall et al., 2025).

Limerence is not a formal clinical diagnosis. There is no established test that can tell every person whether their crush has crossed a medical threshold.

There is also no reliable population estimate of how common it is.

In a 2026 study involving 1,647 participants reporting limerence experiences, researchers found that episodes could be prolonged and recurrent. In a smaller group of 51 people who recorded their experiences over seven days, thoughts about the desired person occupied roughly half their reported waking thought (Evans et al., 2026).

That is a description of the people who participated, not a prediction about how long your feelings will last.

You do not need to decide whether you have limerence to recognize that your attention has become difficult to manage.

Someone can have a powerful crush without being in distress. Another person can have a less dramatic attraction that becomes disruptive because they spend every evening checking for messages.

The label matters less than the experience.

The person you know and the person you imagine

Return to Leila and Sonia.

Leila knows that Sonia is funny, enjoys reading, and shares a few experiences with her.

She does not know whether Sonia wants a relationship, how she handles disagreements, what she wants from her future, or whether they would enjoy living together.

Yet Leila has begun imagining answers.

Perhaps Sonia’s kindness at the party means she would be a caring partner. Perhaps their shared sense of humor means they would understand each other perfectly. Perhaps finding someone so interesting means she has finally met the person she has been waiting for.

Each possibility could turn out to be true.

None has been established.

The imagined version of Sonia fills in missing information with details Leila hopes are there.

This distinction appears in a small qualitative study of six people describing limerence. Their accounts involved both a real person and an idealized version of that person, along with rumination, anxiety, and emotional distress (Willmott & Bentley, 2015).

Rumination means repeatedly thinking through the same subject without reaching a useful conclusion.

You can enjoy imagining a future with someone. You do not have to prohibit daydreaming. The problem begins when possibilities start feeling like facts.

For example:

  • What happened: Sonia laughed at Leila’s joke and asked for her number.
  • What Leila imagines it means: Sonia sees her as a potential long-term partner.
  • What Leila actually knows: Sonia enjoyed talking with her and was willing to exchange contact information.

That last point is promising. It is also incomplete.

Sonia gets to decide what she wants. Leila gets to find out whether they enjoy knowing each other better.

Neither has to commit to the relationship Leila has imagined.

This is where the sculpture-and-glasses picture from earlier in the book becomes useful. You have a perspective on the other person, but you cannot see their experience simply by examining your own feelings.

You need to discover what they actually think and want.

Imagining a future is not the same as building one

A common mistake is to measure a relationship by how vivid the imagined future feels.

You might picture holidays, a wedding, raising children, or getting older together before you know whether the other person wants a second date.

The imagined future can feel comforting. It may even motivate you to approach someone.

But it cannot substitute for time spent learning about each other.

Suppose you spend three months imagining a relationship with someone you see occasionally at work. You may feel as though you have spent three months developing a romantic connection.

From their perspective, you may still be a pleasant coworker they barely know.

Both experiences can be genuine. They are not the same experience.

The distinction is especially important if you have spent years wanting a relationship. The possibility of finally being loved may become attached to one particular person.

Then losing that possibility can feel like losing your entire imagined future.

When that happens, try separating two statements:

“I want a loving relationship” and “I want a loving relationship with this particular person.”

The first is a hope you can continue pursuing.

The second depends on someone else’s independent feelings, choices, and availability.

Why uncertainty can feel so powerful

Imagine sending someone a friendly message.

They reply warmly.

You feel wonderful.

You send another message the next day. Hours pass without a reply.

Suddenly, you are replaying your last conversation. Was your message too long? Did you misunderstand their friendliness? Did they meet someone else?

Then a reply arrives.

Everything feels wonderful again.

It is tempting to treat this emotional movement as evidence that the connection is unusually important.

But the size of your reaction does not tell you how compatible you are.

The research offers some clues about why intense preoccupation can occur, although it does not establish one explanation that applies to everyone.

In the 2026 limerence study, stronger difficulties were associated with insecure attachment, reported adverse childhood experiences, obsessive-compulsive thinking traits, and several mental-health difficulties. During the seven-day sampling period, participants reported more fantasy during periods of sadness or loneliness (Evans et al., 2026).

These are associations, not proof of causes.

Having experienced childhood adversity does not mean you will become fixated on someone. Having a strong crush does not mean you have unresolved trauma.

Another study developing the limerence questionnaire found a small association between anxious attachment and the intense-attachment factor. It did not find an equivalent association with the neglect factor, and avoidant attachment was not associated with either factor (Marshall et al., 2025).

That is a useful caution against explanations that claim every fixation comes from the same childhood experience or attachment pattern.

The question for your own life is simpler:

What am I asking this person’s attention to tell me?

Perhaps you want to know whether they like you.

Perhaps you want reassurance that someone could choose you.

Perhaps you are lonely, and imagining the relationship gives you something pleasant to anticipate.

Perhaps you simply find this person exceptionally attractive.

You can acknowledge any of these possibilities without declaring them the cause of your feelings.

You can also recognize that uncertainty sometimes creates questions that more thinking cannot answer.

If you do not know whether someone wants to date you, replaying their smile a hundred times will not reveal their intentions.

A respectful conversation might.

And if they do not want a relationship, no amount of analysis can turn their decision into another answer.

Intensity is not compatibility

Some people describe meeting someone and immediately feeling certain that this is the person they are meant to be with.

Others worry when they do not experience that dramatic rush.

Neither reaction reliably answers the question of whether two people would make good partners.

The romantic-love literature includes early preoccupation and intense motivation. It also describes lasting romantic love in which strong affection persists without the same obsessive thinking (Bode & Kushnick, 2021).

A relationship does not have to remain mentally consuming to remain meaningful.

Nor does a strong first-week attraction establish that the relationship will work.

Research comparing different kinds of unrequited and mutual love among mostly young US participants found that unrequited forms were generally less satisfying and involved less actual knowledge of the other person.

Interestingly, obsessive thinking did not consistently distinguish unrequited from mutual love in one comparison (Bringle et al., 2013).

So it would be inaccurate to say that thinking constantly about someone proves your feelings are unhealthy.

It would be equally inaccurate to say that thinking constantly about someone proves you have found your ideal partner.

Intensity tells you something about your attraction. Compatibility requires information about both people.

Chapter 13 introduced the kinds of information that matter: values, goals, habits, communication, and the realities of ordinary life.

An exciting first date provides very little of that information.

Consider two possibilities.

At 26, Marcus meets Eli. Their conversation is electric. They share a sense of humor, find each other attractive, and stay awake messaging until two in the morning.

After several dates, they discover that Marcus wants children and Eli is certain he does not.

Their attraction was real. Their difference matters too.

At 44, Priya meets Daniel. Their first date is pleasant, although neither experiences an extraordinary rush of feeling. Over several conversations, they discover that they enjoy similar routines, communicate comfortably, and want compatible futures.

They may choose to continue dating.

Neither example guarantees a successful relationship. That is precisely the point.

A dramatic beginning is not proof of a good future, and a quieter beginning is not proof of a bad one.

You are allowed to enjoy excitement without making it responsible for answering every question.

When your attention locks onto one person

Sometimes a new person becomes extraordinarily interesting.

You remember small details about their preferences. You want to understand everything about them. You think about conversations long after they end.

You may find it difficult to shift your attention back to work or other interests.

People sometimes describe this as hyperfocus, meaning unusually sustained attention that is hard to redirect.

Research involving two adult samples found an association between ADHD traits and greater reported hyperfocus in activities such as studying, hobbies, and screen use. But the study did not examine romantic fixation or attention directed toward a particular person (Hupfeld et al., 2019).

A Brazilian study of 306 adults found higher passionate-love scores among participants screening high for ADHD traits, alone or combined with autism traits, compared with participants in its comparison group. The group screening high for autism traits alone did not show a significant difference in passionate-love intensity (Soares et al., 2021).

That study used online recruitment and screening questionnaires rather than clinical diagnostic interviews. Most participants were women, and passionate love is not identical to limerence.

It does not establish that ADHD causes romantic fixation or that autistic people experience romantic attraction in one particular way.

A 2025 scoping review also identified a shortage of direct research connecting ADHD and limerence (Bradbury et al., 2025).

The useful conclusion is not that one type of brain is destined to become obsessed.

It is that if your attention becomes difficult to redirect, you can learn ways of responding to that experience without treating it as a character defect.

If a new person takes over your attention

You may recognize the feeling of becoming so interested in something that hours pass without you noticing. If a new romantic interest seems to occupy that same kind of attention, notice whether you are still eating, sleeping, completing responsibilities, and spending time with other people. The research does not establish romantic hyperfocus as a distinct ADHD process, but you can respond to the difficulty you experience.

Try keeping the routines you already rely on. Put your phone away during activities that need your attention. Make plans that do not depend on receiving a message. You do not have to stop being excited about someone to keep the rest of your life available to you.

When a person feels like a special interest

Some people use the term special interest for a subject that holds unusually deep, sustained attention.

You might wonder whether a new romantic interest has begun occupying that place in your life.

Perhaps you remember everything the person has told you. Perhaps you keep wanting to talk about them. Perhaps learning about them feels as absorbing as learning about a favorite subject.

The available research does not establish how frequently autistic adults experience romantic interests this way, or whether such experiences are more likely to become limerence.

You can use a description that makes sense of your experience without assuming it describes everyone who shares your neurotype.

There is, however, an important difference between being interested in a subject and being interested in a person.

A subject cannot decide whether it wants your attention.

A person can.

You might enjoy learning everything about astronomy. Astronomy does not need privacy, time alone, or a choice about how much attention to receive.

A person does.

They have their own preferences, friendships, responsibilities, and feelings. They may enjoy some of your attention and not all of it.

Their interest can also change.

That does not make your attention wrong. It means that interest in another person must leave room for the person to choose how involved they want to be.

If someone becomes an intense interest

You may feel most comfortable when you understand a subject thoroughly. A new person, however, is not a problem to solve or a subject you can fully master. You can enjoy learning about them while accepting that they may want different levels of conversation, contact, or privacy.

If uncertain social signals are hard to interpret, asking directly can be kinder to both people than repeatedly guessing. An answer of “no” or a request for space needs to be respected even when the reasons are unclear. You do not need to understand every reason behind a limit to honor it.

This also applies when two autistic people date each other.

Tariq and Noel may both enjoy long conversations about shared interests. Their enthusiasm might make the relationship comfortable and exciting.

But sharing a neurotype does not guarantee identical communication preferences.

Tariq may enjoy frequent messages throughout the day. Noel may prefer a long conversation every few evenings.

Neither preference is automatically better.

They can discuss what feels comfortable, and each person can decide whether the arrangement works for them.

If you’re the person receiving intense attention

Being the center of someone’s attention can feel flattering, uncomfortable, enjoyable, exhausting, or several of those things at once.

You do not have to interpret intensity as a promise of long-term devotion. You also do not have to treat an enthusiastic partner as a problem merely because they communicate differently from you.

Suppose your new partner wants to message throughout the day, but you cannot comfortably maintain that pace.

You might say:

“I enjoy talking with you. During work, I need to focus on my job, so I usually answer messages in the evening.”

That describes what you can offer.

The other person can decide whether that works for them.

If they respect your limit, you have learned something useful. If they repeatedly disregard it, you have learned something important too.

A partner is not responsible for curing another person’s fixation or providing unlimited reassurance.

Both people deserve a relationship in which their choices matter.

The difference between interest and permission

Strong feelings may create a powerful urge to act.

You want another conversation. Another reply. Another opportunity to explain your feelings.

But wanting contact is not the same as having permission for it.

Imagine that Jasmine, 52, meets someone through a dating app. They have a pleasant conversation, and she asks whether they would like to meet.

The person declines.

Jasmine feels disappointed. She may wonder what could have happened if they had spent more time together.

That disappointment is understandable.

It does not create permission to continue pursuing them.

A person’s answer matters more than your interpretation of their potential.

The same principle applies when someone asks for distance, blocks communication, or stops participating in an exchange.

Silence is not a hidden yes.

You may not know why someone stopped responding. They might be busy, uninterested, uncertain, or dealing with something private.

You do not need to discover the explanation before respecting the lack of participation.

If you are unsure whether someone is interested and there is an appropriate opportunity to ask, a clear invitation can replace prolonged guessing:

“Would you like to go on a date with me sometime?”

Then allow the other person to answer freely.

If they decline, accept the answer. If they do not respond, do not treat that as an invitation to keep trying.

If someone has already indicated that they do not want contact, asking again is not appropriate.

Your feelings belong to you. Another person’s participation belongs to them.

When pursuit becomes harmful

There is a difference between thinking constantly about someone and repeatedly directing unwanted behavior toward them.

The first may be distressing, but private thoughts are not the same as harassment.

The second can affect another person’s safety and freedom.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes stalking as a pattern of unwanted behavior that creates fear or safety concerns. Examples include repeated unwanted messages, following someone, or monitoring their activities (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025).

The US Department of Justice describes stalking in terms of a course of conduct directed at someone that would cause a reasonable person fear for safety or substantial emotional distress (U.S. Department of Justice, Office on Violence Against Women, 2023).

These are general US descriptions. Specific legal definitions and requirements vary by state.

Not every awkward interaction or unwanted message legally constitutes stalking. But you do not need to reach a legal threshold before deciding that unwanted pursuit must stop.

If someone has told you they do not want contact, their answer is enough.

If you recognize yourself continuing to seek contact after a refusal, stop.

Do not make the other person responsible for helping you manage the urge. Talk with a trusted person or qualified mental-health professional instead. Create distance from situations that encourage you to keep pursuing someone who has not welcomed it.

If you are receiving repeated unwanted contact, you do not owe the other person another explanation or conversation. Prioritize your safety and consider assistance from a local victim-support service or law enforcement when appropriate.

A small Australian study published in 2007 examined reports from parents of 25 autistic and 38 non-autistic young people. It found differences in how participants learned about romance and in some reported pursuit behaviors (Stokes et al., 2007).

That study cannot establish that autistic adults generally pose a stalking risk. Its small sample, parent-report design, older framing, and focus on younger people make broad conclusions inappropriate.

A more recent scoping review discusses possible connections between limerence, fixation, and harmful pursuit, but it does not demonstrate that people experiencing limerence typically progress to stalking (Bradbury et al., 2025).

Most importantly, a person’s diagnosis, intentions, and depth of feeling do not determine whether another person has consented to contact.

Someone may genuinely believe they are acting out of love.

The other person still gets to say no.

The later chapter on persistence, harassment, and stalking examines these issues in greater detail.

When a crush starts costing you your life outside it

A crush can make life more interesting.

It should not become the only thing your life contains.

You might notice that your attention has narrowed when you regularly abandon activities you enjoy, neglect responsibilities, lose sleep, or find yourself unable to focus on anything except the person.

Perhaps you once looked forward to a weekly game night. Now you attend only if you think the person might be there.

Perhaps you stopped talking with friends because you spend every free moment waiting for a message.

Perhaps you keep checking someone’s social-media activity even though doing so leaves you distressed.

You do not need to wait for a crisis to change these habits.

Try approaching the problem as one of attention and choice rather than a battle against your feelings.

You cannot necessarily decide to stop being attracted to someone.

You can decide not to organize your entire day around the attraction.

Keep parts of your life that belong to you

Start with ordinary commitments.

Eat meals. Maintain your usual sleep schedule where possible. Complete necessary tasks. Spend time with people who are not connected to the crush. Continue activities that matter even when they do not involve romance.

These are practical suggestions, not scientifically established treatments for limerence.

The purpose is not to distract yourself so successfully that you never think about the person.

It is to avoid letting one uncertain relationship determine whether the rest of your life continues.

Suppose you have plans with friends on Friday, but the person you like has not replied to your invitation.

You could spend Friday waiting for a possible response.

Or you could keep your plans.

If the person is interested in dating you, the relationship can develop alongside your existing life.

You do not have to make yourself permanently available to prove that your feelings are sincere.

Notice what keeps the thoughts going

If checking messages repeatedly leaves you distressed, try reducing how often you check them.

If looking at someone’s social-media profile encourages hours of speculation, consider taking a break from viewing it.

If you spend evenings imagining conversations that have not happened, gently return your attention to something you have actually chosen to do.

You are not required to punish yourself for having a thought.

A thought can appear without becoming an instruction.

You might think, I want to know what they’re doing.

You can answer that thought with, I don’t need to know.

You might think, I want them to change their mind.

You can answer, They are allowed to make their own choice.

This does not guarantee that the feeling will disappear. The goal is to make choices that respect both your wellbeing and the other person’s independence.

A practical exercise: fact, interpretation, and permission

When a crush feels especially intense, take a piece of paper or open a private note.

Choose one interaction you keep thinking about.

Separate it into three parts.

1. What actually happened?

Record only something you could describe without guessing at the other person’s intentions.

For example:

“Alex smiled when they saw me.”

“We talked for thirty minutes.”

“They haven’t answered my invitation.”

2. What am I interpreting it to mean?

Write down the explanation your mind has supplied.

For example:

“Alex must have romantic feelings for me.”

“They probably want the same future I want.”

“They haven’t answered because they’re trying to decide.”

These are possible interpretations, not established facts.

You can recognize that distinction without insisting that your interpretation is false.

3. What am I free to do that respects both people?

This question turns attention toward your own choices.

You can enjoy the memory of a pleasant conversation.

You can make one clear invitation when appropriate.

You can allow the other person time to respond.

You can continue your life if they are not interested.

You cannot decide their feelings for them, and you cannot turn an absence of agreement into permission.

The exercise does not need to make you feel better immediately. Its purpose is to separate what you know from what you hope, and what you hope from what you are entitled to expect.

Try it especially when you notice yourself reaching a dramatic conclusion based on a small interaction.

If the feelings will not settle

Sometimes ordinary adjustments are not enough.

You may find yourself repeatedly returning to painful thoughts despite wanting to stop. You may be struggling to work, sleep, maintain friendships, or accept that a relationship is unavailable.

You might feel ashamed because you know your thoughts are disproportionate to the amount of time you have spent with the person.

Shame is not necessary for recognizing a problem.

You can take the difficulty seriously without describing yourself as dangerous, defective, or incapable of love.

A qualified mental-health professional can help you examine the distress and any repetitive behaviors connected with it.

There is some preliminary research on treatment, but not enough to identify a proven treatment specifically for limerence.

A 2021 case study described improvement in one 28-year-old after an individualized cognitive-behavioral approach, including exposure and response prevention, cognitive restructuring, and behavioral activation. Improvement was reported at a nine-month follow-up (Wyant, 2021).

Exposure and response prevention is a structured therapeutic approach that involves working with distressing triggers while refraining from associated compulsive responses.

One case cannot establish that this treatment will work for other people with limerence. It should not be treated as a self-help prescription.

If your preoccupation is causing substantial distress, you can describe the actual experience to a professional rather than arriving with a particular diagnosis.

For example:

“I keep thinking about someone who isn’t interested in dating me. I know I need to respect their decision, but the thoughts are interfering with my everyday life. I want help managing them.”

That gives a professional useful information about what you are experiencing and what you want to change.

You can seek help whether or not you identify with the word limerence.

When the feelings are not returned

One of the hardest parts of an intense crush is accepting that the other person may not feel the same way.

You can understand their answer intellectually while still wanting a different outcome.

You might find yourself thinking that they would love you if they knew you better, if circumstances changed, or if you could finally explain how much they mean to you.

Those thoughts do not require action.

An imagined future can be difficult to release even when the relationship never began.

You may be disappointed about the person themselves, the experiences you hoped to share, and the future you pictured.

Those are meaningful feelings.

But the imagined future was never an agreement between two people.

You can be disappointed without turning your disappointment into a reason to challenge another person’s decision.

You can also be disappointed without deciding that you have lost your only opportunity for love.

If Sonia tells Leila that she enjoyed their conversations but does not want to date, Leila might still think about her for a while.

She may still find Sonia attractive.

She may still wish the answer were different.

None of that prevents Leila from respecting the decision.

And accepting the answer does not require her to pretend the feelings never mattered.

Sometimes moving forward means allowing an attraction to remain unfinished.

There is no final conversation that must happen, no special explanation to obtain, and no obligation to transform the disappointment into friendship.

You can decide what distance you need while respecting the other person’s choices.

Let attraction open a door, not write the entire story

A crush tells you that someone has caught your attention.

Infatuation tells you that the attraction is especially strong.

Limerence describes an experience in which longing and preoccupation may become consuming.

None of these experiences, by itself, tells you whether the other person wants a relationship with you.

None tells you whether the two of you would be compatible.

And none takes away your responsibility to respect the other person’s independence.

You do not have to distrust every strong feeling. Attraction is one of the reasons people begin dating.

You can enjoy it.

You can let yourself be curious.

You can appreciate the possibility of finding someone who understands you.

Just leave enough room for reality.

A person you like is not yet the person you imagine spending your life with. They are someone you can get to know, if they also want that.

The purpose of dating is not to prove that your first impression was correct.

It is to discover whether two real people, rather than one person and an imagined future, want to build something together.
