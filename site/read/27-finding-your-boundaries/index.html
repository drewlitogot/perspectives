Morgan likes Sam. They’ve been on three dates, their conversations are easy, and Morgan wants to see where things go.

There is just one problem. Actually, several small problems.

Sam likes holding hands while walking. Morgan enjoys it for a few minutes, then starts wishing for some space. Sam sends messages throughout the workday. Morgan likes receiving them but feels increasingly distracted by the expectation of answering. And last night, when Sam asked about Morgan’s childhood, Morgan gave a long explanation and later wished they hadn’t.

Sam hasn’t necessarily done anything wrong. Morgan hasn’t necessarily done anything wrong either. They may simply have different preferences, some of which neither person knows about yet.

Morgan has been concentrating on whether Sam likes them. Now there’s another question worth asking:

What actually feels right for Morgan?

That question can be surprisingly difficult to answer, especially when you’re excited about someone, inexperienced with dating, accustomed to accommodating other people, or unsure what you’re feeling.

Finding your boundaries means getting better at answering it.

You don’t have to know all your boundaries already

The previous chapter explained that a boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept. A request asks another person to do something, and that person can say no.

Before you can communicate a boundary, though, you have to discover what it is.

Some limits are obvious. You might know that you don’t want anyone reading your private messages, that you dislike being tickled, or that you won’t date someone who insults you.

Others become clear through experience. You may discover that you enjoy spending an entire Saturday together but need Sunday alone. Or that you’re comfortable discussing your family, but not the painful details of a particular relationship.

Sometimes you find a boundary only after something happens that you didn’t enjoy.

That’s information you can use going forward. You don’t have to repeat the experience to prove that the limit is real.

The National Alliance on Mental Illness describes physical, emotional, and time boundaries as areas people can identify through reflection on their needs and comfort (National Alliance on Mental Illness, n.d.). For dating, it also helps to look at sensory, digital, and sexual boundaries. These six categories are a practical way to organize your thinking, not a scientific test that determines what your limits should be.

There is no standard number of dates, hugs, messages, hours together, or personal disclosures that everyone ought to accept.

You are allowed to have different answers from the person you’re dating.

Physical boundaries: what happens to your body and space

Physical boundaries concern touch, proximity, and access to your body.

Consider a few ordinary situations:

  • Someone reaches for your hand while walking.
  • Your date sits close enough that your legs touch.
  • A person you’ve just met offers a hug.
  • Your partner wants to cuddle while watching television.
  • Someone touches your hair or takes something from your hands.

You might welcome some of these and dislike others.

You might also welcome an action from one person but not another. Being comfortable hugging a close friend doesn’t mean you have to hug a new date. Enjoying someone’s hand on your shoulder doesn’t mean you want to be touched without warning.

Attraction does not create an obligation to accept touch.

Neither does being in a relationship.

Physical boundaries also include personal space. Maybe you like talking face-to-face but feel uncomfortable when someone stands very close. Maybe you enjoy sitting together without touching. Maybe you like physical closeness most of the time but need more room when you’re tired.

Try considering three questions:

  1. What kinds of touch do you enjoy, dislike, or feel uncertain about?
  2. Does your answer change depending on the person, place, or situation?
  3. Do you prefer someone to ask before touching you, including when the touch is usually welcome?

You don’t need one answer that applies to every relationship for the rest of your life.

Wanting closeness and wanting space can coexist

Nadia, 34, enjoys cuddling with her girlfriend, Elise. She also dislikes unexpected hugs from behind.

Elise initially finds that combination confusing. She enjoys spontaneous affection and associates it with feeling close.

Neither preference is inherently unreasonable. They experience the same kind of closeness differently.

Nadia’s limit isn’t a rejection of Elise. It’s a distinction between touch she enjoys and touch she doesn’t.

Elise may still wish spontaneous hugging were part of their relationship. That wish is real too. But wanting a form of affection doesn’t entitle either partner to it.

They can explore other ways of expressing affection that both people welcome.

This is the difference between finding compatibility and trying to make someone accept an unwanted experience.

Sensory boundaries: the conditions matter

Physical boundaries answer questions about touch and space. Sensory boundaries also consider what touch, sounds, light, smells, textures, and other sensations feel like.

You might want a hug but dislike scratchy clothing against your face. You might enjoy kissing but find strong perfume unpleasant. You might like sitting together at a restaurant but struggle to have a conversation when music is playing loudly.

Some people enjoy intense sensory experiences. Others prefer less stimulation. One person can have both kinds of preferences.

In a qualitative study, Gray and colleagues examined autistic adults’ accounts of sensory experiences in sexuality and relationships. Their findings described both sensory sensitivities and sensation seeking, including experiences involving touch, sight, sound, and smell (Gray et al., 2021).

Another qualitative study compared the experiences of 31 autistic and 26 non-autistic adults. Some autistic participants described touch as startling or unpleasant, while others valued physical affection and closeness. Participants also described ways that clear communication helped them navigate physical intimacy (Sala et al., 2024).

These were relatively small, self-selected samples. They don’t establish what every autistic person likes, and they don’t mean sensory preferences are limited to autistic people.

They do illustrate an important distinction: wanting the person and wanting a particular sensation are not the same question.

A person can want closeness without wanting every form of touch.

Finding the conditions that work

Imagine you’ve enjoyed several dates at a quiet coffee shop. Then you go to a crowded restaurant, and by the end of the evening you desperately want to leave.

Does that mean you don’t enjoy dating this person?

Not necessarily.

Think about the surroundings separately from the relationship. Perhaps it was the noise, the lighting, the crowd, the length of the evening, or a combination.

Ask yourself:

  • Which parts of this experience felt comfortable?
  • Which specific sensations were unpleasant?
  • Would a different setting have changed how I felt?
  • Did I want less contact with the person, less sensory input, or both?

Your boundary might be that you won’t stay in a venue after the noise becomes too much. Your preference might be to choose quieter places from the start.

Those are different observations, and both are useful.

You also don’t have to find a way to tolerate every sensation. Sometimes an accommodation makes something enjoyable. Sometimes the answer is simply that you don’t want it.

Emotional boundaries: how much of yourself you share

Dating involves getting to know another person. That doesn’t mean giving them unrestricted access to your private life.

Emotional boundaries concern what you share, when you share it, and how much emotional responsibility you are prepared to take on.

Examples include:

  • Whether you discuss painful childhood experiences.
  • How much detail you share about past relationships.
  • Whether you’re ready to talk about mental health, family conflict, or personal fears.
  • How much emotional support you can offer at a particular moment.
  • Whether you want to discuss a difficult subject in person rather than through messages.

You can be sincere without revealing everything.

You can also care about someone’s feelings without becoming responsible for making every difficult feeling disappear.

Privacy is not dishonesty

Consider Priya, 42, who has recently started dating Daniel.

Daniel is comfortable discussing his previous marriage. Priya prefers not to discuss her own painful breakup until she knows someone better.

Neither person has to adopt the other’s pace.

One study by Kil and colleagues examined how people respond to different preferences for privacy and disclosure in romantic relationships. Across two studies, approaches that respected personal autonomy were viewed more favorably than controlling approaches. In the couples study, autonomy-supportive responses were associated with greater disclosure (Kil et al., 2022).

That finding doesn’t mean respecting privacy is a technique for persuading someone to reveal more. It means personal disclosure and personal privacy both deserve consideration.

A healthy relationship doesn’t require immediate access to someone’s most private experiences. U.S. public-health guidance includes respect for privacy, space, and independent choices among features of healthy relationships (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2026).

There is an important qualification: privacy isn’t permission to mislead someone about matters that affect their safety, consent, or significant shared decisions. You can choose not to discuss every detail of your past while still being truthful about information necessary for an informed relationship.

The earlier chapter on disclosure covered whether and when to tell someone about your neurotype. You don’t need to treat every personal difference, diagnosis, or life experience as a disclosure deadline.

Emotional support has limits too

Suppose someone you’re dating has had a terrible day. You want to listen, and you’re glad they trust you.

But you’ve also worked ten hours, barely eaten, and can’t concentrate on a long conversation.

There are two legitimate needs here: their desire for support and your need for rest.

Finding your emotional boundary means noticing what you can freely offer without ignoring your own needs.

That might be a short conversation tonight and a longer one tomorrow. It might mean listening but not trying to solve the problem. It might mean recognizing that a problem requires help beyond what a dating partner can reasonably provide.

The goal isn’t to become emotionally unavailable. It’s to make your care something you can offer willingly rather than something you feel forced to produce.

Time boundaries: how much togetherness works for you

Some people love long dates, frequent calls, and spontaneous plans.

Others enjoy dating but need regular time alone, predictable schedules, or uninterrupted periods for work and personal interests.

Neither approach is automatically more loving.

Time boundaries concern how much of your schedule, attention, and energy you can offer.

They include time for sleep, work, hobbies, friendships, family, recovery, and doing absolutely nothing.

Maintaining interests and relationships outside a romantic partnership is part of the healthy-relationship picture described in U.S. public-health guidance. Demands that someone withdraw from other people or surrender control of their time can be warning signs of controlling behavior (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2026; Office on Women’s Health, 2025).

That doesn’t mean asking for more time together is wrong. It means both people need room to make choices freely.

More time doesn’t always mean more connection

Evan, 29, enjoys his dates with Theo, 31. Theo would happily spend Friday evening, Saturday, and Sunday together.

Evan genuinely likes Theo. He also needs a quiet Sunday to prepare for work and recover from a busy week.

If Evan ignores that need, he may start dreading plans he would otherwise enjoy.

The relevant question isn’t whether Evan likes Theo enough. It’s how much time Evan can spend together and still enjoy the relationship and maintain his other responsibilities.

Theo might discover that a relationship with only one shared evening a week wouldn’t meet his needs. That’s useful information about compatibility.

Neither person must pretend to want the other’s preferred schedule.

Think about your own time:

  • How often would you ideally like to see someone when dating?
  • What amount of time together leaves you feeling comfortable afterward?
  • How much notice do you prefer before making plans?
  • Which responsibilities or personal activities do you want to protect?
  • How available do you want to be by phone?

You aren’t setting a universal schedule. You’re finding a workable starting point.

If you lose track of time, forget to reply, or have difficulty switching between activities, the earlier chapters on energy and messaging offer practical tools. Those difficulties don’t automatically tell you what your boundaries should be.

Digital boundaries: your phone is still yours

Dating doesn’t erase personal privacy.

Digital boundaries concern how people communicate with you, what they can access, and what information about you gets shared.

Possible areas include:

  • Passwords and access to accounts.
  • Whether someone uses your phone or other devices.
  • Location sharing.
  • Photographs, intimate images, and public posts.
  • Whether your relationship appears on social media.
  • How much of your daily activity you discuss or share online.
  • When you are available for messages and calls.

Some couples willingly share a location to coordinate plans. Others don’t. Some enjoy posting photographs together. Others prefer keeping their relationship private.

The important distinction is between something freely chosen and something demanded through pressure or fear.

Privacy isn’t proof of wrongdoing

Suppose you don’t want a partner reading your messages with friends.

That doesn’t mean you’re hiding an affair. Your friends may have shared private information with you, and you may simply want your conversations to remain private.

Similarly, you can decline to share your location, passwords, or photographs without needing to prove that you’re trustworthy.

In a survey of 365 U.S. college students, Reed and colleagues studied digital dating abuse, including controlling and monitoring behaviors. Digital abuse was associated with other forms of dating aggression, including psychological, physical, and sexual aggression (Reed et al., 2016).

The study was correlational and limited to college students. It cannot establish that one behavior causes another or tell us how common these experiences are among all U.S. adults.

Still, the warning signs deserve attention. The U.S. Office on Women’s Health identifies demands for passwords or immediate replies, pressured intimate images, and controlling a partner’s contact with other people as possible signs of dating abuse (Office on Women’s Health, 2025).

Someone wanting access to your private information is not the same as being entitled to it.

You also don’t need to participate in sexting, send intimate photographs, or agree to someone posting about you because the relationship has become more serious.

If digital monitoring or threats are happening, the safest response may involve specialist support rather than changing device settings or confronting the person on your own. The National Network to End Domestic Violence provides technology-safety information for people experiencing abuse (National Network to End Domestic Violence, n.d.).

Detailed digital safety and sexual-image sharing are covered later in the book.

Sexual boundaries: what you want, don’t want, and haven’t decided

Sexual boundaries concern which sexual experiences you would freely choose, with whom, under what circumstances, and at what pace.

They may involve decisions about kissing, sexual touching, sexual activity, sexual conversations, protection, privacy, or whether you want sexual contact at all.

You don’t have to want sex to date.

You can be attracted to someone without wanting sexual activity with them. You can want some kinds of intimacy but not others. You can feel curious about an experience and still decide not to try it.

You may also have preferences or limits that you won’t know until you have more information.

That’s a reason to slow down, not a reason to guess.

A relationship isn’t a standing agreement

Imagine you’ve been dating someone for several months and have enjoyed kissing them.

One evening, you don’t want to kiss.

You don’t owe it to them because you enjoyed it last week.

Similarly, being comfortable with kissing doesn’t establish that you want any further sexual contact.

RAINN’s U.S. consent guidance emphasizes that sexual choices must be freely made, that previous sexual activity doesn’t establish permission for future activity, and that silence or lack of resistance isn’t consent (RAINN, 2026).

The full rules of consent, including checking in and withdrawing agreement, come in the next chapters. For now, the question is what experiences you might willingly choose.

A qualitative study of 24 self-identified autistic adults in the United States found accounts of adapting sexual experiences to sensory needs and using planning and explicit communication (Barnett & Maticka-Tyndale, 2015).

That doesn’t establish one preferred approach for autistic people. It suggests that intimacy need not follow a single familiar pattern.

Your own boundaries might include wanting time to think, preferring particular surroundings, avoiding certain kinds of touch, or deciding that sexual activity isn’t something you want.

You don’t need to justify a sexual limit by citing a diagnosis, past experience, or sensory sensitivity.

Not wanting something is enough.

When you don’t know how you feel

Sometimes the hard part isn’t describing your boundary to another person.

It’s realizing you have one.

You might leave a date thinking everything went well, then notice later that you disliked a particular conversation.

You might agree to a plan because you genuinely think it sounds good, then realize afterward that it leaves you exhausted.

Or you might notice that something is wrong without knowing whether you’re anxious, tired, irritated, disappointed, or uncomfortable with the other person’s behavior.

You don’t have to identify the exact emotion before deciding to pause.

Not everyone recognizes feelings in the same way

Alexithymia is a term for difficulty identifying or describing emotions.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Kinnaird and colleagues examined 15 studies involving 366 autistic and 348 non-autistic participants. Difficulty identifying and describing feelings was substantially more common in the autistic groups, although it was not universal (Kinnaird et al., 2019).

This research doesn’t establish that autistic people recognize boundaries later than anyone else. It also doesn’t mean that difficulty identifying feelings makes someone unable to make decisions or give consent.

But if naming emotions is difficult for you, there is no reason to insist on an emotion-based method of discovering your limits.

You can start with observable facts.

For example:

You might not know whether you felt angry or anxious when your date asked several questions about your family.

You do know that you wanted the subject to change.

You might not know whether your discomfort during a hug came from surprise, touch, or feeling uncertain about the relationship.

You do know that you’d prefer to be asked before the next hug.

You can make a useful decision from those observations without solving the entire emotional puzzle.

You can understand an experience afterward

Consider Jordan, 38, who enjoyed dinner with his boyfriend but became very quiet during the drive home.

Jordan didn’t know what was wrong at the time. The next morning, he realized he had become uncomfortable when the conversation turned to his finances.

His boyfriend had asked ordinary questions about Jordan’s work and spending. Jordan hadn’t previously thought about financial privacy as a personal limit.

Now he knows he wants to share less detail until the relationship develops further.

Nothing requires Jordan to decide that his boyfriend was being intrusive. He can simply recognize that the conversation revealed a preference he hadn’t noticed before.

This is one reason it helps to revisit your experiences rather than relying exclusively on how you felt in the moment.

If you experience overload or shutdown, you may also need to stop an interaction before you can explain what you need. Pausing is an acceptable decision. You don’t need a perfectly worded explanation before leaving an uncomfortable situation.

When sexual activity is involved, uncertainty isn’t an invitation to continue and hope the answer becomes clear. Stop or wait until there is a freely given, clear agreement. The next chapters develop that skill.

If saying no has felt unsafe before

Working out your limits is harder when the honest answer used to be dangerous. You may reach for what keeps the peace before you reach for what you want.

Start where nothing is at stake: the film, the restaurant, the time you go home. A limit you’ve said out loud once is far easier to say again when it matters.

Notice who makes it easy. A person who takes a small no gracefully is showing you how a large one will go, and that’s information worth collecting early.

Make a boundary map

You don’t need to sit down and write a constitution for your dating life.

A simple map is enough to begin.

Take a page and write these six headings: physical, sensory, emotional, time, digital, sexual.

Under each heading, consider four possible answers:

Answer What it means
Yes This is something you currently welcome or want.
No This is something you don’t want or won’t accept.
Unsure You don’t have enough information to decide.
Depends Your answer changes according to the person, setting, timing, or other conditions.

These aren’t permanent categories or a psychological assessment. They’re a practical way to notice what you already know.

Here’s an example of what one person’s map could contain:

Area Possible discovery
Physical Hand-holding: yes. Unexpected hugs: no.
Sensory Quiet restaurants: yes. Very loud venues: no.
Emotional Discussing interests: yes. Detailed family history: unsure.
Time Weekly dates: yes. Spending every weekend together: depends.
Digital Sharing funny photographs: yes. Sharing passwords: no.
Sexual Kissing: depends. Other sexual activity: unsure.

A different person could have almost opposite answers, and that wouldn’t make either person’s map wrong.

If the sexual section doesn’t apply to what you want from dating, you can record that too.

Try it with one real experience

Choose one recent interaction, whether it was a date, a friendship, or a conversation with someone you care about.

Write down what happened without trying to decide immediately whether anyone was wrong.

For instance:

We spoke on the phone for two hours. I enjoyed the first hour. During the second hour I wanted to cook dinner, but I kept talking.

Then consider:

What did I enjoy? The conversation and the other person’s attention.

What did I not enjoy? Missing dinner and feeling unable to end the call.

What might I want differently? Shorter calls on work nights, or being more willing to end a conversation when I need to.

Notice how the conclusion is specific.

You don’t need to decide that the other person is too demanding. You don’t need to decide that you’re bad at relationships. You have discovered something about how you want to use your time.

You can do the same exercise after an enjoyable experience.

Perhaps you discover that you love having breakfast together, enjoy affectionate messages in the morning, or feel especially comfortable during dates that involve an activity rather than continuous conversation.

Boundaries are not only about avoiding unpleasant things. Understanding what helps you feel comfortable gives you more choices when building a relationship.

Separate preferences from firm limits

Once you’ve made your map, look at the answers again.

Some are preferences. You might prefer Saturday dates but happily agree to Friday instead.

Some are firm personal limits. You might decide that you won’t share your passwords or participate in sexual activity you don’t want.

Some are conditional. You might welcome a hug from a familiar partner but not from someone you just met.

And some are still unknown.

These distinctions aren’t medical or scientific categories. They’re simply a way of avoiding the mistake of treating every preference as non-negotiable, or every firm limit as something you should compromise on.

You can be flexible about one thing and firm about another.

When two people’s boundaries don’t match

Discovering your boundaries doesn’t guarantee that another person will have compatible ones.

One person may want a great deal of affectionate touch. Another may enjoy relatively little.

One may prefer frequent conversation. Another may want long stretches of quiet.

One may enjoy openly discussing personal experiences early in dating. Another may take much longer to feel comfortable.

These differences don’t automatically establish that either person is doing something wrong.

They do tell you something important about whether the relationship can meet both people’s needs.

A mismatch isn’t a failure

Suppose Alex, 45, likes spending most evenings with a partner. Their new date, Chris, 47, prefers seeing someone twice a week.

Alex might feel disappointed. Chris might worry about seeming uninterested.

The important questions are whether both can make room for what the other person needs, whether either is willing to make changes, and whether the resulting relationship would actually satisfy them.

They might find a schedule that works.

They might discover that their preferences are too different.

Neither outcome means someone has failed to try hard enough.

This is part of what dating is for: finding out whether two real people can build something both people want.

Respect doesn’t require identical preferences

The same principle applies when partners have different sensory needs.

One partner might love music while cooking. The other might find it difficult to concentrate with music playing.

They can explore arrangements that work for both, without declaring either person’s sensory experience incorrect.

When people have different ways of experiencing the world, the double empathy problem reminds you that misunderstandings can run in both directions.

The aim isn’t for one person to become the permanent interpreter of the other’s needs.

Both people can describe their experiences, listen, and consider what is possible.

Not every difference can be solved. But understanding the difference comes before deciding what to do with it.

If you're dating someone who struggles to name a limit

“I don’t know” can be accurate rather than evasive. Some people register discomfort slowly, and a decision demanded on the spot will be a guess.

Ask narrower questions, and offer the option of answering later: “Is this a no, or a not yet?” works better than “are you okay with this?” when the second is genuinely unanswerable in the moment.

Treat a limit that arrives late as valid rather than as a reversal. Hearing it a day afterward is the system working, not the person being inconsistent.

When the problem is more than a difference

There is a separate question that matters especially when dating someone new:

Do you feel free to decline?

You might dislike a particular kind of touch but feel completely safe telling your partner so.

That’s different from believing the person will punish, threaten, frighten, or humiliate you for refusing.

Someone might prefer frequent messages. That’s different from demanding immediate responses while monitoring whom you talk to.

Someone might be disappointed that you aren’t ready for sexual activity. That’s different from using pressure or threats to obtain it.

U.S. public-health guidance identifies isolation, controlling behavior, threats, and coercion as warning signs of relationship violence (Office of Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2026; Office on Women’s Health, 2025). RAINN also distinguishes freely chosen sexual contact from compliance under pressure or fear (RAINN, 2026).

You don’t have to find the perfect wording for a boundary before someone is required to treat you safely.

And you don’t have to announce a boundary before unwanted touching, threats, or abuse can be wrong.

If you’re afraid of what someone will do when you decline, the priority isn’t practicing a better boundary statement. It may be safer to seek confidential support from a trusted person or an appropriate domestic-violence or sexual-assault service. The later safety chapters explain these situations and the available U.S. resources.

If you notice yourself demanding access to another person’s private information, pressuring them for intimacy, or trying to control how they spend their time, stop those behaviors. Consider professional support if stopping is difficult. Finding your own boundaries doesn’t give you permission to override someone else’s.

Your boundaries can change without being meaningless

You may discover that some preferences change as you get to know a person.

A hug that felt uncomfortable on a first date might feel welcome after several months.

You might initially enjoy seeing someone once a week, then want to spend more time together.

Or you might discover that an activity you expected to enjoy isn’t something you want to repeat.

Changing your mind doesn’t mean your earlier boundary was fake.

A boundary describes what you will do or accept, and you can make a different choice when your needs or circumstances change.

Other limits may stay the same for years.

You don’t need to predict which ones will change. And you never have to change a boundary simply because a partner has been patient, kind, or affectionate.

The goal is to understand yourself well enough to make decisions you can stand behind.

That understanding won’t always appear immediately. Sometimes you’ll know exactly what you want. Sometimes you’ll need time. Sometimes you’ll realize what mattered only after an ordinary interaction has ended.

All three are useful starting points.

Return to Morgan and Sam.

Morgan now knows a few things that weren’t clear before: hand-holding is enjoyable in small amounts, workday messaging needs more space, and detailed childhood conversations can wait.

Sam has preferences too. Some may fit Morgan’s easily. Others may require a conversation, an adjustment, or an honest recognition that they want different things.

Morgan doesn’t need to present a complete set of rules for the relationship. Just a clearer understanding of what feels welcome, what doesn’t, and what is still uncertain.

Finding a boundary is the private part. Communicating it is the next skill.

The next chapter covers how to say those limits plainly, kindly, and firmly, without turning every preference into a confrontation or every disagreement into a demand.
