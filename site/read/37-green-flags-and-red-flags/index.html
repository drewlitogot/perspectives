Someone takes you to a wonderful restaurant. They remember your favorite dessert, ask thoughtful questions, and tell you how excited they are to see you again. You go home thinking the date went beautifully.

The next week, you explain that you can’t meet on Saturday because you already have plans with a friend.

They become angry. Suddenly, you feel that keeping your original plans will cost you something.

Which version of this person should you believe?

Both happened. The enjoyable date was real, and so was the reaction to your independent plans. But the second event tells you something important that the first could not: how this person behaves when your wishes differ from theirs.

Dating involves learning about someone before you know what life with them would be like. You cannot read their mind, predict their future behavior, or discover their character from a single conversation.

You can, however, pay attention to what they actually do.

A green flag is an observable behavior that suggests someone may be capable of building a respectful, healthy relationship. A red flag is an observable behavior that raises concern about disrespect, control, coercion, or harm.

Neither is a personality diagnosis. Neither reveals everything about a person.

And they aren’t equally important. Someone who remembers your birthday does not earn permission to threaten you. A collection of pleasant experiences cannot cancel out dangerous behavior.

The useful question isn’t simply, “Are they a good person?”

It is, “How do they treat me, and what happens when my needs, choices, or boundaries differ from theirs?”

Look at behavior, not the label

Dating advice often encourages people to sort potential partners into categories: good people, bad people, healthy people, toxic people.

Those labels can feel satisfyingly clear. They don’t always help you decide what to do.

Imagine telling a friend that your new partner is controlling.

Your friend might ask, “What do they do?”

You could answer:

  • “They demand access to my private messages.”
  • “They get angry when I spend an evening with friends.”
  • “They try to make decisions about how I spend my money.”
  • “When I say no to something, they keep pressuring me.”

Now your friend understands the concern without needing to agree about your partner’s personality.

The same principle applies to green flags.

Instead of saying someone is emotionally mature, describe what happened:

  • “They told me when their plans changed.”
  • “They listened when I explained something that bothered me.”
  • “They accepted that I wasn’t ready for physical affection.”
  • “They apologized for interrupting me and gave me time to finish.”

The National Domestic Violence Hotline describes healthy relationships in terms of respect, communication, honesty, trust, equality, boundaries, and personal independence. These are qualities that can be observed in everyday interactions, not secret characteristics you must learn to detect (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.a).

There is an important limitation: no established checklist of green flags can guarantee that someone will become a safe, compatible long-term partner.

People are complicated. Someone can behave beautifully on a first date and badly six months later. Someone else can be nervous and awkward at first but become a dependable partner.

Watch what happens over time, while taking serious warning signs seriously from the beginning.

Green flags: what healthy treatment looks like

A green flag doesn’t have to be romantic or impressive.

Sometimes it is a person doing something ordinary without making it difficult.

Your boundaries are accepted

You tell someone that you don’t want to stay out late.

They accept it.

You explain that you’re not ready to kiss.

They don’t argue.

You say that you need Sunday afternoon to yourself.

They respect your plans.

They may feel disappointed. They may ask a respectful question to understand your preference. But they do not treat your boundary as an insult or an obstacle they must defeat.

They also have boundaries of their own.

A healthy relationship isn’t one where the other person agrees to everything you want. It is one where both people can have limits without being punished for them.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline identifies mutual respect for boundaries, privacy, and independence as features of healthy relationships (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.a).

You can disagree without being afraid

Consider two people deciding where to eat.

One wants sushi. The other doesn’t like fish.

A disagreement has occurred. Neither person has done anything wrong.

They might choose somewhere else, take turns choosing restaurants, or decide to eat separately that evening.

What matters isn’t that they agree. It’s that neither person has to surrender their opinion to prevent anger, humiliation, or punishment.

The same applies to larger disagreements.

You might differ on religion, career plans, family involvement, or how much time to spend together. Those differences might eventually reveal incompatibility. They should still be discussable without fear.

You can tell someone, “I see this differently,” and remain confident that you will be treated with basic respect.

That is a meaningful green flag.

They take your needs seriously, even when those needs are unfamiliar

Nadia, 31, finds busy restaurants exhausting. She enjoys spending time with her girlfriend, Rachel, but sometimes noise and multiple conversations make it difficult to concentrate.

Rachel loves crowded restaurants.

Neither preference is wrong.

When Nadia explains the problem, Rachel doesn’t have to suddenly enjoy quiet places. Instead, they look for an arrangement that works for both of them. Sometimes they eat somewhere quieter. Sometimes Rachel goes to a crowded restaurant with friends.

The green flag is not that Rachel naturally shares Nadia’s sensory preferences.

It is that Nadia’s needs matter.

The same principle works in reverse. Someone who needs detailed plans, extra time to answer a question, or more physical space can respect a partner who prefers spontaneity, faster conversation, or more affection.

A difference becomes easier to live with when both people can discuss it without treating either person’s experience as defective.

They appreciate you

Appreciation can be expressed in many ways.

A person might thank you for helping with an errand, remember something important to you, recognize the effort behind a difficult task, or simply say they enjoyed spending time together.

A large analysis of 43 longitudinal couples datasets found that perceived partner commitment and appreciation were among the strongest relationship-specific factors associated with people’s current relationship quality. The analysis also identified perceived partner satisfaction, sexual satisfaction, and conflict as important factors (Joel et al., 2020).

These were largely people’s own reports about established relationships. The findings do not tell you how to predict a relationship from a few early dates.

Other research offers a closer look at appreciation. Across several studies, Gordon and colleagues found associations between feeling appreciated, appreciating one’s partner, responsive behavior, commitment, and relationship maintenance (Gordon et al., 2012).

This doesn’t mean a partner must constantly compliment you.

It means you should notice whether your effort and wellbeing seem to matter to them.

And whether you show them the same consideration.

Your good news matters to them

Marcus finally gets a promotion after months of applying for a better position.

His boyfriend, Theo, doesn’t fully understand Marcus’s industry. But he listens, asks what the new role involves, and shares Marcus’s excitement.

That response tells Marcus that his happiness matters to Theo.

In a study of 79 dating couples, researchers examined how partners responded when the other person shared good news. Supportive responses were associated with relationship wellbeing and outcomes measured over the following two months (Gable et al., 2006).

That is a small study with a short follow-up, not a rule for assessing every date.

Still, it points toward a useful everyday observation: does this person make room for the things that are important to you?

Someone may not express excitement the way you expect. They might offer a quiet congratulations, ask practical questions, or suggest a small celebration later.

The important distinction is between a different expression of care and repeated indifference or dismissal.

They can acknowledge a mistake

Nobody communicates perfectly.

People forget plans, say something awkward, misunderstand a request, or make a decision that inconveniences someone else.

The important question is what happens afterward.

Suppose your date arrives twenty minutes late because they mixed up the meeting time.

They acknowledge the mistake, apologize, and make a reasonable effort to avoid repeating it.

Compare that with someone who repeatedly arrives late, blames you for being upset, and insists that you should stop expecting them to consider your time.

The first situation may be a manageable mistake. The second gives you a reason to question whether your needs are being respected.

A genuine apology isn’t a demand for immediate forgiveness. And one apology does not erase a harmful pattern.

Look for responsibility followed by behavior that supports the apology.

Some differences are not red flags

This distinction matters enormously.

If everything you dislike becomes a red flag, dating turns into an endless search for someone who behaves exactly as you expect.

Consider these situations:

Someone takes several hours to reply to a message.

Someone doesn’t make much eye contact.

Someone prefers spending time at home.

Someone talks extensively about an interest.

Someone needs advance notice before changing plans.

Someone becomes quiet during a difficult conversation.

None of these behaviors, by itself, establishes that the person is disrespectful, untrustworthy, or dangerous.

You might find some of them incompatible with what you want. That is a valid reason not to date someone.

But incompatibility is not the same as mistreatment.

The same behavior can have different explanations

Sam, 42, has been dating Eli for a month. Sam likes daily messages. Eli often replies in the evening.

Sam wonders whether Eli is losing interest.

When they discuss it, Eli explains that messages are difficult to keep up with during work and that evenings are easier. Eli agrees to be clear about plans, while Sam explains that knowing when they will next talk helps.

They may find a comfortable arrangement, or they may decide their preferences are too different.

Neither possibility requires someone to be the villain.

Now imagine a different situation.

Sam has explained that unexpected changes cause significant stress. Eli repeatedly changes agreed plans without warning, mocks Sam for becoming upset, and refuses to discuss the problem.

The concern is no longer simply a difference in scheduling preferences.

It is repeated dismissal of an expressed need.

This is why observable behavior matters more than assumptions about someone’s intentions.

A person who communicates differently might need something explained directly. Someone who experiences overload or shutdown may need time before continuing a conversation.

Those differences deserve consideration.

But a communication difference does not make controlling or threatening behavior acceptable. And being neurotypical does not guarantee respectful behavior, either.

A poor match can still be a good person

Suppose one person wants marriage and children, and the other wants neither.

They might treat each other beautifully and still be incompatible.

Or one person wants frequent physical affection while the other prefers very little. Neither should be pressured to change a boundary.

It can be disappointing to meet someone kind, attractive, and enjoyable who cannot offer the relationship you want.

You don’t need to discover a red flag to justify deciding that the relationship isn’t right for you.

“This doesn’t work for me” is enough.

If calm has ever felt like something is missing

If early relationships were unpredictable, steadiness can register as flatness. Someone reliable may seem to lack chemistry, while someone who swings between intensity and withdrawal feels compelling.

Treat that reading as unreliable rather than as information about the person. The useful question is not “do I feel much?” but “what does this person actually do, and what happens when I say no?”

Boredom with someone kind is worth sitting with for a while before acting on. It is often the feeling of nothing going wrong.

Red flags: when your freedom starts to shrink

A red flag deserves attention because of what the behavior does, not because you have identified someone’s secret motive.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recognizes intimate partner violence as including physical violence, sexual violence, stalking, and psychological aggression intended to harm or exert control (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024).

Not every unhealthy interaction falls into one of these categories. But some warning signs are much more serious than an annoying habit or an ordinary disagreement.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline identifies behaviors such as humiliation, intimidation, isolation, control over finances or decisions, threats, and sexual pressure as warning signs of abuse (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.b).

You do not need to understand why someone behaves this way before taking the behavior seriously.

Your independent life becomes a source of conflict

At first, Devon’s new partner seems enthusiastic about spending time together.

After a while, Devon notices that seeing friends regularly results in arguments. Family visits become difficult. Activities Devon previously enjoyed start to feel like things that require permission.

Devon begins declining invitations to avoid conflict.

The concern is not that a partner wants time together. Wanting closeness is ordinary.

The concern is that Devon’s ability to maintain an independent life is being restricted.

A relationship should not require you to give up your friendships, ordinary privacy, or freedom to make personal decisions.

Your privacy is treated as something you must surrender

A partner repeatedly demands access to your phone, messages, accounts, or communications with friends.

They treat privacy as evidence that you have something to hide.

You begin feeling that maintaining normal personal boundaries will cause conflict.

Digital monitoring is a recognized concern in dating abuse. In a US survey of 365 college students, Reed and colleagues found that digital monitoring and other digitally mediated abusive behaviors were associated with other reported forms of dating violence (Reed et al., 2016).

The study was cross-sectional and involved college students. It cannot establish that a particular digital behavior will lead to future violence.

But you do not need that prediction to recognize an unwanted invasion of privacy.

Sharing information voluntarily is different from having it demanded through pressure.

You feel pressured to ignore your own limits

You have already said that you are uncomfortable with something.

The person continues pressuring you, dismisses your concerns, or treats your refusal as something you must justify repeatedly.

You start wondering whether agreeing would be easier than continuing to resist.

That is not the same as two people discussing a disagreement respectfully.

The earlier chapters on boundaries and consent explain the distinction in detail. Here, the point is simpler: someone’s desire does not override your choice.

The same applies when you are the person who wants something. If another person’s answer is no, uncertain, or not yet, you must respect it.

You are repeatedly humiliated or intimidated

A partner insults you, belittles your abilities, or makes you afraid of their reactions.

Perhaps you have begun choosing your words carefully because you expect to be mocked or threatened.

Perhaps they regularly insist that your discomfort is ridiculous.

You may still have pleasant evenings together. That does not make the harmful behavior unimportant.

The Hotline includes humiliation, threats, and intimidation among its warning signs of abuse (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.b).

A relationship is not safe simply because its harmful moments are separated by affectionate ones.

Money or practical dependence becomes a means of control

Money is a common source of disagreement.

People may have different spending habits, budgets, incomes, or ideas about sharing expenses.

Those differences are not automatically abuse.

The warning sign is when a partner uses money to restrict your ability to make decisions, obtain necessities, work, or maintain independence.

The Hotline identifies financial control as a form of abusive behavior (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.b).

If you are becoming financially dependent on someone, pay particular attention to whether you still have meaningful choices.

When a warning sign is an urgent safety concern

Some behaviors should not be treated as ordinary dating problems to discuss over dinner.

Threats to kill or seriously harm someone, threats involving weapons, physical violence, and severe intimidation deserve immediate attention.

One influential US study examined intimate-partner femicide, meaning the killing of a woman by a current or former intimate partner.

Researchers compared 220 cases of women killed by intimate partners with 343 women who had experienced nonfatal partner abuse across 11 US cities. Factors associated with increased danger included an abusive partner’s access to firearms, previous threats with weapons, threats to kill, and separation from a highly controlling partner (Campbell et al., 2003).

The study involved women aged 18 to 50 and primarily male perpetrators in relationships where abuse was already present. Its findings cannot be converted into a prediction about every new dating partner or applied as equivalent risk estimates for people of every gender.

The authors also found that some associations changed when additional factors were included in statistical models. Not every behavior examined was an independent predictor in every analysis.

The practical lesson is not that you should calculate your level of danger.

It is that certain threats and controlling behaviors warrant serious safety attention.

If someone is threatening you, has become violent, or is using a weapon to intimidate you, don’t treat this as a test of your communication skills.

Seek support from a domestic violence professional who can help you consider your circumstances and plan for safety. Leaving a controlling relationship can itself involve danger, so avoid assuming that a sudden confrontation or departure is the safest option.

You do not need to collect a certain number of red flags before seeking help.

If you are in immediate danger in the US, call 911 when it is safe to do so.

For confidential domestic violence support, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 800-799-SAFE (7233), text START to 88788, or visit https://www.thehotline.org/. If someone might be monitoring your devices or communications, consider how to seek help safely (The National Domestic Violence Hotline, n.d.b).

If you're dating someone who apologizes for things that aren't wrong

Frequent apologizing, checking whether you’re angry, or preemptive agreement usually come from somewhere other than this relationship. Reassurance alone rarely shifts it; evidence does.

Be predictable, and be explicit when nothing is wrong: “I’m not annoyed, I’m just tired.” Say what you’ll do and then do it. Over time that is what changes the expectation.

Don’t treat yourself as a repair project either. You can be steady without taking responsibility for undoing what somebody else did.

When you recognize the problem only afterward

Some people leave a relationship and later wonder how they failed to recognize what was happening.

They remember incidents that seemed confusing at the time but are now much easier to identify as controlling or harmful.

This experience deserves particular attention when you have often been told that your perceptions are wrong, that you misunderstand social situations, or that you should trust someone else’s interpretation over your own.

In interviews with 22 autistic adults who had experienced interpersonal violence, Gibbs and Pellicano documented participants’ difficulties recognizing abuse and danger, as well as uncertainty and self-doubt about harmful experiences (Gibbs & Pellicano, 2023).

Another qualitative study collected accounts from 43 autistic adults in the United Kingdom who had experienced interpersonal victimization by people they knew. Participants described harmful interactions that had become normalized, experiences of being invalidated, and difficulties identifying or responding to mistreatment (Pearson et al., 2022).

In a further UK study, Douglas and Sedgewick interviewed 24 autistic adults, including women, men, and nonbinary people. Participants described isolation, coercion, financial abuse, and experiences in which autism-related stereotypes were used to undermine their credibility (Douglas & Sedgewick, 2024).

These are relatively small, self-selected studies. They tell us about the experiences of participants, not about every autistic adult.

They do, however, challenge an unfair assumption: that recognizing abuse is simply a matter of being good at reading social signals.

Sometimes harmful behavior is difficult to identify because it has been normalized. Sometimes a person is repeatedly told that their interpretation is wrong. Sometimes they know what is happening but do not have a safe or practical way to leave.

Not recognizing a warning sign immediately does not make someone responsible for being mistreated.

And recognizing it later is still useful.

You don’t have to decode someone’s intentions

Suppose a partner says that you’re too sensitive whenever you explain that a comment hurt you.

You might spend hours wondering whether they meant to be cruel.

Perhaps they misunderstood. Perhaps they were defensive. Perhaps they believe they were joking.

You do not need to settle that question before deciding the repeated dismissal is unacceptable to you.

You can describe what happened:

“They made comments that hurt me. I explained the problem. They continued and dismissed my concerns.”

That description is enough to consider your next decision.

When you’re unsure about an experience, a trusted friend, counselor, or advocate may help you examine it. Choose someone who listens seriously rather than automatically defending the other person or telling you what you must do.

A useful outside perspective should help you understand your choices, not replace your judgment.

The evidence about different people’s risks has limits

It would be convenient if every person had a simple profile of warning signs tailored to their gender, brain, or life history.

The research is not that precise.

A systematic review by Gibbs and colleagues examined 16 studies of interpersonal violence involving autistic adults. Rates of reported victimization were often elevated compared with non-autistic groups, but findings varied considerably by the kind of violence measured, the questions used, and the populations studied. Among seven studies with comparison groups discussed in the review, five found elevated risk for at least one outcome and two did not (Gibbs et al., 2026).

Some studies found higher reported victimization among autistic women and gender-diverse people than among autistic men. However, the gender comparisons were limited, and the studies did not always measure the same experiences.

The review also identified substantial research gaps. Many samples underrepresented people with intellectual disabilities, nonspeaking people, people of color, and gender-diverse adults. Much of the evidence was self-reported and cross-sectional, making explanations about causes uncertain (Gibbs et al., 2026).

This means a handbook should neither ignore unequal experiences nor assume that a particular identity determines someone’s risk.

There is also no good basis in this source set for classifying delayed replies, forgetfulness, shutdown, or unusual social expression as predictors of whether a partner will become abusive.

A person’s neurotype may help explain how they communicate and what accommodations they need.

It does not tell you whether they respect your choices.

And trauma, neurodivergence, or difficult past relationships never grant permission to control another person.

The standard is mutual: both people’s boundaries, dignity, and safety count.

What if the same person shows green flags and red flags?

Most relationships contain both enjoyable experiences and frustrating ones.

The question is what kind of behavior you are seeing.

There is a difference between someone who forgets an appointment and takes responsibility, and someone who repeatedly disregards your plans and punishes you for objecting.

There is a difference between someone who feels jealous and discusses that feeling without controlling you, and someone who uses jealousy to restrict your friendships.

There is a difference between someone who needs space during a disagreement and someone who uses intimidation to control the outcome.

These differences cannot be resolved by adding up good and bad moments.

Imagine someone is thoughtful 90 percent of the time but threatens you during arguments.

That is not a relationship with a passing grade of 90 percent.

The threatening behavior remains serious.

Likewise, a person who has a different conversation style from yours is not automatically less safe or less caring, even if the difference sometimes frustrates you.

Green flags suggest possibilities. Red flags identify concerns. Serious danger signs require safety decisions, not balancing calculations.

A practical way to look at what happened

You do not need to create secret tests to find out what someone is like.

Don’t deliberately provoke jealousy, invent a crisis, or set up situations designed to catch a person failing. Those actions introduce dishonesty into the relationship and can create problems of their own.

Instead, use interactions that naturally occur.

When something leaves you uncertain, consider four questions:

  1. What actually happened? Describe the behavior without guessing what it means. “They canceled our plans twice without telling me until the last minute” is more useful than “They don’t value me.”
  2. Is this an isolated event or a pattern? For ordinary mistakes, notice whether the same problem continues. Serious threats or violence do not require repetition.
  3. How did it affect my choices or wellbeing? Were you inconvenienced, disappointed, confused, pressured, frightened, or prevented from doing something you had the right to choose?
  4. What happened after I expressed my needs or boundaries? Did the person listen, clarify, accept the boundary, and make an appropriate effort to change? Or did the behavior continue, with additional pressure or punishment?

These questions are practical aids to reflection, not a scientific screening test.

You might write down a few observations after a date, especially if you find it easier to identify feelings later.

For example:

What happened: The café was noisy. I said I was having trouble following the conversation.

Their response: They asked whether I’d prefer to sit outside. We moved, and the conversation became easier.

What I learned: They were willing to adjust our plans when I explained a need.

Or:

What happened: I said I couldn’t meet because I had already agreed to help a friend.

Their response: They became angry and said I should cancel my plans.

What I learned: My independent plans were not respected. I need to take that seriously.

You don’t need to record every interaction or analyze every sentence.

The purpose is to notice meaningful behavior, especially if strong attraction or uncertainty is making it difficult to see the overall picture.

And if a situation feels dangerous, don’t wait to finish an exercise or gather more observations. Seek appropriate support.

If you notice these red flags in yourself

You might recognize that you’ve demanded reassurance in a way that restricted someone else’s choices. Perhaps you’ve repeatedly dismissed a partner’s expressed needs, invaded their privacy, or treated their boundaries as negotiable.

Recognizing harmful behavior is an opportunity to stop it.

Do not ask the other person to excuse it because you feel insecure, misunderstood, lonely, or distressed.

Respect their decisions and boundaries. Accept responsibility without demanding forgiveness or continued contact.

If controlling, intimidating, or coercive behavior has become a pattern, seek appropriate professional help to address it.

Changing your behavior is your responsibility. The other person does not owe you another opportunity.

A green flag isn’t a promise, and a red flag isn’t a puzzle

You can spend a long time trying to determine whether someone likes you, whether they are secretly compatible with you, or whether an awkward encounter means something important.

Some of those questions are difficult because people communicate differently.

But respectful treatment should not depend on your ability to solve an elaborate social puzzle.

A partner can be direct or indirect, talkative or quiet, spontaneous or routine-oriented, outwardly affectionate or reserved.

There is room for all of those differences.

There is not room for threats, coercion, humiliation, or restrictions on someone’s basic independence.

You do not have to find the perfect person. You are looking for someone with whom mutual respect, safety, and a workable relationship are possible.

Notice the small, dependable behaviors that make life together easier. Notice the behaviors that make your choices smaller or your safety less certain.

You can appreciate someone without dating them. You can care about someone and still recognize that their behavior is harmful. You can end a relationship without proving that the other person is fundamentally bad.

And you can remain open to people who show care in ways different from those you expected.

The boundaries you’ve learned to recognize, express, and respect are not barriers to connection. They help make connection safer for both people.

Once you know what respectful treatment looks like, you have a stronger foundation for the next question: how to show someone you’re interested, and how to understand whether that interest is returned.
