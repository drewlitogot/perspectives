You meet someone at a board game night. They seem friendly. You would like to know them better.

You walk over, say hello, and ask whether they have played this game before.

“Once,” they say. “I’m terrible at it.”

You laugh. “Same. I usually spend half the game figuring out the rules.”

They smile. Then neither of you says anything.

Now what?

Maybe you start explaining the game. Maybe you ask three questions in a row. Maybe you decide the silence means they do not want to talk. Or perhaps you become so busy deciding what to say next that you miss what they say.

None of this means you are incapable of conversation. It means you are trying to perform several unfamiliar tasks at once: starting an interaction, understanding another person, deciding what to share, and figuring out when each person should speak.

Those tasks become easier when you stop treating conversation as a performance with a correct answer.

A conversation is something two people make together. You offer something. The other person responds in their own way. You notice what they offered and decide where to go next.

Sometimes it flows. Sometimes it is awkward. Sometimes you discover that you have little in common. All three outcomes can be perfectly ordinary.

You do not need a brilliant opening line

A lot of unnecessary pressure gets placed on the first sentence.

You might imagine that an interesting person needs an interesting introduction. You rehearse something clever, decide it sounds foolish, rehearse something else, and eventually say nothing.

But the first sentence has a much smaller job than you think.

It only needs to give the other person an opportunity to talk.

A useful opening usually has three qualities: it fits the situation, it is easy to answer, and it leaves the other person free not to continue.

At a community event, you might say:

“Have you been to one of these before?”

At a cooking class:

“Have you made this recipe before? I’m trying to figure out what it’s supposed to look like.”

At the start of a date:

“How has your day been?”

None of these sentences is particularly original. That is part of their usefulness. They do not require the other person to understand a joke, respond to a compliment, reveal something private, or pretend you already know each other.

A field experiment by Sandstrom and Dunn found that people who made brief friendly contact during a coffee purchase reported more positive feelings than people instructed to keep the transaction efficient. The experiment was about ordinary social contact, not romantic introductions, but it offers a useful reminder: a small interaction can have value without becoming anything more (Sandstrom & Dunn, 2014).

An opener is not a test of whether you are attractive, funny, or worthy of attention. It is simply an invitation.

Start with something you share

The easiest subject is often something both people can observe.

You are at the same event, waiting for the same activity, or looking at the same menu. You already have a little context in common.

That gives you somewhere to begin without guessing what the other person enjoys.

Consider two conversations:

Conversation A

“Tell me something interesting about yourself.”

“Um. I don’t know.”

Conversation B

“How did you hear about this event?”

“My sister comes here. She thought I’d like it.”

“Has she been right so far?”

“Mostly. I’m still deciding about the music.”

The second conversation offers a narrower, easier starting point. The other person does not have to summarize their personality.

It also gives you something to respond to. The conversation can move toward family, music, the event, or something else entirely.

A question is not required, either. A simple observation can give the other person room to contribute:

“This is my first time here. I wasn’t sure what to expect.”

They might respond with their own experience. They might ask a question. They might smile and say nothing.

A friendly response is not automatically romantic interest. It means that, at least in that moment, someone responded to you. Whether they want further conversation, friendship, or a date is a separate question.

Also consider the setting. Someone working, trying to concentrate, or clearly occupied may not be available for conversation. A greeting does not entitle you to their attention.

If a person gives a short answer and returns to what they were doing, allow the interaction to end. You have not failed. They have simply not accepted the invitation to continue.

Small talk has a purpose

“How’s the weather?”

“Busy day?”

“Have you been here before?”

If you prefer meaningful conversations, these questions may feel pointless. You already know whether it is raining. You do not particularly care how crowded the grocery store is.

Why spend time discussing things that do not matter?

Because the information is not always the main point.

Small talk is a brief, low-pressure conversation about familiar subjects. It can help people begin interacting before deciding whether they want a longer conversation.

Think of it as a social introduction rather than an exchange of important facts.

When someone says, “It’s cold out today,” they may not be trying to discuss meteorology. They may simply be offering an easy subject that either person can continue or leave behind.

You do not have to be fascinated by the weather to respond.

“Definitely. I thought spring had finally arrived.”

The other person might mention walking their dog, an upcoming trip, or how much they dislike winter. Now there is something more personal to discuss.

Small talk can give people a chance to notice how they interact without requiring either person to disclose private information.

That does not mean you must spend a certain number of minutes on it. There is no scientifically established minimum amount of small talk required before a conversation can become interesting.

And it is not a moral obligation. If someone does not enjoy casual chatting, that preference deserves respect.

When small talk feels like a script you never received

Perhaps you understand the words people use but not what they expect you to do with them.

Someone asks, “How are you?”

Do they want an accurate account of your day? Are they just saying hello? How much detail is appropriate?

A useful starting point is to treat a casual greeting as an opportunity for a brief answer, unless the person makes it clear they want more.

“Pretty good. I finally finished a big project. How about you?”

That answer gives a little information and makes it easy to continue.

You can also be direct about your preferences:

“I’m never sure what to talk about at these events. What got you interested in coming?”

Or:

“I’m better at talking about things we’re interested in than making small talk. What have you been enjoying lately?”

You are not required to imitate someone else’s conversational style perfectly.

In a small UK study, five autistic adults identified useful ways of making conversations more comfortable, including understanding the setting, the type of conversation, and how the exchange would be structured. Their familiar non-autistic conversation partners also had a role in making communication work (Silver & Parsons, 2022).

The study does not establish that autistic people generally dislike small talk. Some enjoy it, some prefer to skip it, and others may feel differently depending on the situation.

The practical lesson is to notice what helps you participate. Perhaps a shared activity makes conversation easier. Perhaps you prefer knowing whether the plan is casual chatting or a serious discussion.

Those are reasonable preferences to communicate, whether or not you identify as autistic.

A conversation needs more than questions

Imagine you are on a first date.

You ask what your date does for work. They answer.

You ask where they grew up. They answer.

You ask what music they like. They answer.

You ask whether they have siblings.

Eventually, it begins to feel like an interview.

You have learned several facts, but you have not necessarily learned what spending time together feels like.

Questions help start and continue conversations. They are not the whole conversation.

A useful pattern is:

  1. Ask or offer something. Give the other person a place to begin.
  2. Respond to what they said. Show that you heard their particular answer.
  3. Contribute something of your own. Let them learn about you, too.
  4. Leave room for their contribution. They may respond, ask something, change the subject, or finish.

You do not need to follow these steps mechanically. They are simply a way to notice whether you are participating together.

Follow-up questions show attention

A follow-up question asks about something the other person just said.

Suppose your date, Marcus, mentions that he recently started painting.

You could ask another unrelated question:

“Do you have any pets?”

Or you could follow what he said:

“What made you want to try painting?”

That gives Marcus an opportunity to tell you why painting matters to him.

Perhaps he has always wanted to make art. Perhaps he attended a class with a friend. Perhaps he discovered that painting gives him something enjoyable to do after work.

Each answer gives you a different picture of who he is.

In experiments involving getting-acquainted conversations, Huang and colleagues found that people instructed to ask more questions tended to be liked more by their conversation partners. Questions, especially follow-ups, appeared to communicate responsiveness: listening, understanding, and caring about what someone said (Huang et al., 2017).

There is an important disagreement about the dating portion of that research.

The original researchers reported that asking more follow-up questions was associated with more offers of second dates in a heterosexual speed-dating study. Kluger and Malloy challenged that analysis, finding that question-asking depended on both individuals and the particular pair, and disputing the claimed second-date benefit. The original authors defended their interpretation (Huang et al., 2017; Kluger & Malloy, 2019; Yeomans et al., 2019).

So do not turn this into a rule that asking more questions will make someone want to date you.

A better lesson is that following what someone actually says can help them feel heard.

The point is genuine attention, not counting questions.

Share something, too

Consider this exchange:

Marcus: “I started painting a few months ago.”

Alex: “What made you want to try it?”

Marcus: “My friend invited me to a class. I thought I’d hate it, but it’s surprisingly relaxing.”

Alex: “That’s interesting. I had a similar experience with pottery. I went expecting to be terrible, which I was, but I really enjoyed it. What kind of things do you paint?”

Alex has done two things: responded to Marcus’s answer and offered a piece of personal experience.

Now Marcus knows something about Alex. They have begun discovering what they share.

Compare that with an exchange where Alex never reveals anything:

“What made you start?”

“What do you paint?”

“How often?”

“Are you good at it?”

“Would you want to sell your paintings?”

Even sincere questions can become tiring when the other person is expected to provide all the material.

On the other hand, answering every question with a long story about yourself leaves little room for the other person.

Neither extreme gives both people much opportunity to participate.

You do not need equal speaking time down to the minute. You do need enough room for both people to be known.

When the other person does not ask questions

Some people show interest by asking questions. Others show it by sharing related experiences, making observations, or listening quietly.

If you expect everyone to show interest in exactly the same way, you may miss a different style.

Suppose you tell someone you visited a national park.

They respond:

“I went hiking last weekend. We got completely lost.”

You might think, They changed the subject to themselves.

But they may be offering a related experience, expecting you to respond with your own.

You could say:

“That sounds stressful. Did you find the trail again?”

Or return to your original thought:

“That reminds me. The park I visited had a trail I almost missed, too.”

You can also make space for yourself without waiting to be asked:

“I have a story about that, actually.”

If someone repeatedly shows little curiosity about you, that matters. The chapter on effort and reciprocity explains why you should not have to carry every exchange indefinitely.

But one missing question does not tell you everything about someone’s interest.

Turn-taking: knowing when to speak

Turn-taking is the way people share opportunities to speak and listen during a conversation.

It sounds simple until you try to do it consciously.

You are listening to someone’s story. They pause.

Are they finished?

You start talking. They start talking at the same time.

Who should stop?

You are midway through an explanation when they say, “Right, exactly!”

Is that encouragement, or are they trying to take over?

These moments are not always easy to interpret, even when both people have good intentions.

Researchers studying conversations across ten languages found that people commonly coordinate speaking turns quickly, while the timing also varies between languages and cultures (Stivers et al., 2009).

That means conversational timing is not random. But it also does not establish a universal rule for how long you should pause or when a person must speak.

A pause is not automatically a problem

Suppose you ask:

“What was your favorite part of growing up there?”

Your date looks down and becomes quiet.

You might think:

That was a stupid question.

Or:

They aren’t interested in me.

Perhaps they are thinking. Perhaps they are choosing which part of their childhood to describe. Perhaps they would rather not discuss the subject.

You cannot know which explanation is correct from the pause alone.

Give the person room to answer.

If the silence continues and you are unsure whether they want to respond, you can ask:

“Would you rather talk about something else?”

Or:

“Take your time. I wasn’t trying to put you on the spot.”

The other person can then tell you what they prefer.

In a small study of conversations between autistic adults and conversations between non-autistic adults, researchers found broadly similar overall turn timing. The autistic pairs had somewhat longer gaps near the beginnings of their conversations, but not a clear overall difference throughout (Wehrle et al., 2023).

Another small study examined autistic–autistic, non-autistic–non-autistic, and mixed-neurotype pairs working on a shared online task. The groups showed different patterns of gaps, overlapping speech, and brief listening responses. Those patterns did not have one consistent meaning across all pairings (Poursoroush et al., 2026).

These studies involved tasks and everyday conversations, not romantic dates. They do not tell you precisely how to handle a first-date silence.

They do challenge the idea that one style of timing is automatically correct.

Overlapping speech is not automatically rude

Sometimes two people speak at once because they are excited.

Sometimes a person begins speaking because they thought the other person had finished.

Sometimes an interruption prevents someone from completing an important thought.

The effect matters more than labeling every overlap as good or bad.

If you interrupt accidentally, you can say:

“Sorry, go ahead. You weren’t finished.”

If someone interrupts you repeatedly, you can say:

“There’s one more part I wanted to explain.”

If both of you enjoy fast conversation with overlapping comments, there is no reason to force an unnatural silence between every sentence.

But if one person keeps losing their chance to speak, something needs to change.

The purpose of taking turns is not perfect timing. It is making sure both people can participate.

If knowing when to speak is the hard part

Plenty of people who are good at conversation still can’t find the gap. By the time you’re sure someone has finished, the moment has moved on, and interrupting feels worse than staying quiet.

You can take the turn explicitly rather than timing it: “Can I come back to what you said about work?” reopens a thread without competing for a pause.

It’s also fair to say it plainly to someone you like: “I’m slow to jump in, not uninterested. Ask me things and you’ll get plenty.” Most people find that easy to work with.

When your conversational rhythms differ

A 2026 study by Poursoroush and colleagues found different turn-taking patterns depending on whether pairs consisted of two autistic adults, two non-autistic adults, or one of each. Mixed-neurotype pairs had longer gaps in the study’s collaborative task, while some other patterns were more common in matched pairs (Poursoroush et al., 2026).

These were small groups completing an online task, so the findings should not be treated as rules about dating.

Still, they illustrate the double empathy problem introduced earlier in the book. An interaction is shaped by both participants.

One person may experience a long pause as comfortable thinking time. The other may experience the same pause as uncertainty about whether the conversation is continuing.

Both are reacting to the same moment from different perspectives.

The answer is not to declare one person’s interpretation correct.

It is to find out what each person needs.

If you need extra time

You can make your conversational pace easier to understand:

“That’s a good question. Let me think for a second.”

Or:

“I sometimes need a little time before I can answer.”

You do not owe anyone an immediate answer to a personal question.

If several questions at once are difficult to process, you can ask for one at a time.

If noise, crowds, or competing conversations create overload, consider a quieter setting. The other person may also welcome a change.

And if you cannot find the words, you can say so. You do not have to invent an answer to keep the conversation moving.

If you’re dating someone who needs extra time

A thoughtful response does not always arrive quickly.

Your partner may pause before answering, speak slowly, or prefer a clear question over several implied ones.

Allowing that space can be as simple as not filling every silence.

You might say:

“There’s no rush.”

Or ask:

“Would it help if I asked that differently?”

Do not assume that a pause means your partner does not care. Equally, do not assume every pause is caused by neurodivergence. Ask what works for that particular person.

If your thoughts arrive faster than your turns

You may recognize a different problem: while someone is speaking, several related thoughts arrive at once.

You are afraid you will forget yours, so you interrupt. Or you start explaining something and realize several minutes later that the other person has barely spoken.

A questionnaire study of 179 multilingual adults found that participants with ADHD reported more difficulties with conversational regulation than participants without ADHD, including interrupting and talking at length. These were self-reports, not observations of dating interactions, and the findings do not describe everyone with ADHD (Köder et al., 2025).

Regardless of why it happens, you can make adjustments.

If you interrupt, give the other person their turn back.

If you are worried about forgetting your thought, you might briefly note a keyword, provided it fits the situation. Or you may decide that hearing the other person matters more than remembering every idea.

If you have been speaking for a while, you can check:

“I’ve been talking quite a bit. What do you think?”

You are not apologizing for having thoughts or enthusiasm. You are making space for another person to contribute.

If you're dating someone who pauses before answering

A gap before a reply usually means the answer is being assembled, not withheld. Filling it with another question restarts the process and makes the silence longer.

Let the pause run. If you’re unsure whether they’ve finished, ask once: “Is there more, or shall I go?” That’s easier to answer than guessing at each other.

Someone who takes a few seconds often gives you a more considered answer than someone who replies instantly. The pause is frequently where the good part comes from.

You do not have to hide your natural style

Imagine two autistic adults, Sam and Priya, having dinner together.

Sam begins telling Priya about a documentary. Priya recognizes the subject and starts discussing a related historical event.

They move rapidly between references, sometimes finishing each other’s thoughts. Neither feels the need to explain every detail.

Now imagine Priya talking about the same subject with someone who knows nothing about it.

She might need to explain who the people are, why the event matters, and how the details connect.

The conversation is different, but the difference does not make either person less capable.

In a study of interactions among autistic adults, Heasman and Gillespie described occasions when participants proceeded as though they already shared background knowledge. When that assumption worked, it could help create quick understanding. When it did not, parts of the conversation became confusing. The participants also showed ways of maintaining connection despite imperfect coordination (Heasman & Gillespie, 2019).

That study observed people gaming together. It was not a study of dating, and it does not establish one universal autistic communication style.

It does offer a useful question:

Does the person you’re talking with have enough context to follow what you’re saying?

If you are discussing an unfamiliar subject, a little introduction can help.

“This is about a documentary I watched on the early space program. The surprising part was how the teams handled failure.”

Now the other person knows the subject and why you find it interesting.

If they already know the topic, you can go further together.

A compatible conversation does not require identical brains. Nor does it require either person to spend the entire evening masking.

It requires room for two people to discover how they communicate.

The next chapter explores what happens when a subject matters so much to you that you want to explain every detail.

Moving beyond small talk

A conversation about weather or weekend plans can stay enjoyable for a long time.

But if you want to know someone better, eventually you may wonder how to discuss something more meaningful.

The change does not need to be dramatic.

Suppose your date says:

“I spent Saturday helping my brother move.”

You might ask:

“Do you two live close to each other?”

They answer:

“Not anymore. He moved about an hour away.”

You follow:

“Are you close?”

Now you have moved from weekend logistics toward family relationships.

Or suppose someone mentions taking a photography class.

You could ask:

“What do you enjoy about photography?”

Their answer might tell you something about creativity, patience, attention to detail, or the places they like visiting.

You are learning more about the person without demanding private information.

Deeper does not mean more invasive

A deeper conversation involves something personally meaningful: an experience, value, hope, opinion, or feeling.

It does not require someone to reveal painful memories or discuss their most private experiences.

Compare these questions:

“What do you usually enjoy doing on a free Saturday?”

“What makes a really good weekend for you?”

“What would you like to have more time for in your life?”

Each question offers an opportunity to share a little more, but none demands a particular level of disclosure.

In experiments conducted by Kardas, Kumar, and Epley, people tended to expect deeper conversations with strangers to feel more awkward and less connecting than they actually did. Participants often underestimated how interested other people would be in what they shared (Kardas et al., 2022).

That is encouraging if you worry that every personal question will make a conversation uncomfortable.

But the participants were having conversations arranged for research. The results do not mean that strangers or dates want to answer deeply personal questions without warning.

The useful distinction is between inviting depth and demanding disclosure.

You can ask:

“Would you be comfortable talking about something a little more personal?”

You can also offer your own experience first, without expecting the other person to match it.

For example:

“I’ve been thinking about changing careers. I like my work, but I’m not sure I want to do it forever.”

Your date might share a similar uncertainty. They might ask why. Or they might prefer to talk about something lighter.

All are acceptable responses.

If someone declines a subject, do not keep returning to it in the hope of getting a different answer.

Let depth develop in both directions

A meaningful conversation is not one person revealing everything while the other remains a mystery.

Suppose Elena, 43, tells her date that she recently moved to a new city after ending a long relationship.

Her date, Robin, listens and asks a gentle question about what she enjoys in her new neighborhood.

Elena answers, then asks Robin what brought them to the city.

Robin shares a little about their own life.

Neither person needs to tell their entire history. They are learning enough to decide whether they enjoy continuing.

Sometimes one person wants to go further than the other. That does not necessarily mean there is a problem.

It can simply mean that they prefer a different pace, or that a particular subject feels too personal right now.

A conversation can be meaningful without being intense.

What if you think the conversation went badly?

You finish talking to someone you liked.

On the way home, you replay everything.

You remember that you stumbled over a sentence, interrupted once, and took too long to answer a question.

You imagine that the other person must have been bored.

But you have not asked what they thought. You are evaluating the conversation from inside your own experience.

Remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book: the two of you occupied different positions and saw different things.

You heard every uncertain thought you had. The other person did not.

Boothby and colleagues found, across five studies of getting-acquainted conversations, that participants tended to underestimate how much their conversation partners liked them (Boothby et al., 2018).

This pattern is sometimes called the liking gap: the difference between how much someone thinks another person liked them and how much that person actually reported liking them.

That does not mean every person secretly enjoys talking to you. It is an average finding in particular research settings, not a promise about dating.

It does mean your most critical interpretation of a conversation is not automatically the most accurate.

Instead of asking, Was I awkward?, consider:

Did the other person have opportunities to talk?

Did you learn something about them?

Were you interested in what they said?

Did you feel reasonably comfortable being yourself?

Was there something you might do differently next time?

These questions give you information you can use.

If the person clearly said they did not want further contact, respect that. If neither person wants to continue, that is a valid outcome too.

You do not need to reinterpret a refusal as hidden interest just because people sometimes underestimate how well an interaction went.

Knowing when to end a conversation

Not every conversation needs to become longer.

Sometimes you have somewhere to be. Sometimes the other person is tired. Sometimes you simply have nothing more to discuss.

A conversation that ends is not necessarily a failed conversation.

You can finish clearly and kindly:

“It was nice talking with you. I’m going to head back to the group.”

Or:

“I should get going. Enjoy the rest of your evening.”

If you would like to keep talking, you can make that an invitation rather than an expectation:

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you like to keep chatting, or are you ready for a break?”

Notice that both answers are allowed.

If someone says they need to leave, let them leave. You do not need an explanation, and you should not make their departure difficult.

If they seem distracted or repeatedly give little response, you can allow the conversation to end without making a judgment about their character.

A brief, pleasant exchange can simply be brief.

And when you want a specific answer about romantic interest, conversation skill alone cannot provide it. Friendly conversation is not a promise of a date or a relationship. Later chapters explain how to show interest and ask about it clearly.

Practice without turning people into an assignment

The easiest place to practice conversation is somewhere the stakes are low and both people have a natural reason to be there.

Perhaps you attend a hobby group, meet an acquaintance for coffee, or chat with a friend before an activity.

Choose one small skill to practice.

You might decide to ask one follow-up question instead of changing the subject immediately.

Or you might notice whether you have shared anything about yourself.

Or you might practice allowing a pause without rushing to fill it.

For example:

You: “How was your weekend?”

Friend: “Good. I went to an outdoor concert.”

You: “What kind of music was it?”

Friend: “Jazz. I didn’t expect to like it so much.”

You: “That’s fun. I’ve never been to a jazz concert. What surprised you about it?”

Then listen.

If your friend wants to discuss the concert, continue. If they answer briefly and move on, follow that direction or let the conversation end.

Later, take a moment to notice what happened.

You are not grading the person or trying to produce a particular reaction. You are learning what it feels like to listen, respond, and contribute.

You can practice a different skill another time.

Do not force conversations on people who are busy, working, uncomfortable, or uninterested. Ordinary social contact works best as an invitation both people are free to accept or decline.

You can also practice with someone you know

If talking to unfamiliar people is especially demanding, start with a person who is willing to help.

You could say:

“I’m trying to get better at conversations. Would you be willing to practice with me for a few minutes?”

Ask them to discuss an ordinary subject. You can take turns asking questions, explaining your interests, and noticing when you interrupt or become quiet.

Afterward, ask what the conversation felt like for them.

Perhaps they appreciated your questions. Perhaps they wanted more time to finish their thoughts. Perhaps they enjoyed hearing your perspective but wanted a little more context.

Their experience may be different from yours.

That is useful information, not a verdict on your social ability.

The real goal is connection, not perfect technique

There will be people with whom conversation feels easy almost immediately.

There will be others who are kind and interesting but need time to become comfortable with you.

And there will be people with whom conversation never becomes enjoyable, even though neither person has done anything wrong.

You cannot guarantee a connection by selecting the right questions, timing your pauses, or presenting the most polished version of yourself.

You can make a conversation more welcoming.

You can show curiosity without demanding answers. You can contribute without taking over. You can explain your needs instead of hoping someone guesses them. You can allow different communication styles without assuming that one person must be corrected.

Most importantly, you can notice whether the other person is participating willingly.

The goal is not to convince someone that they should enjoy talking to you. It is to discover whether the two of you enjoy talking together.

Sometimes that discovery happens through a long discussion about the things that matter most to you.

Sometimes it begins with a very ordinary question about the weather.
