Perspective boxes

At 35, you might know how to manage a department, pay your bills, negotiate a lease, and handle a family emergency. But ask someone on a date? You’ve never done it.

Perhaps you had a crush in high school and never said anything. Perhaps your family didn’t allow dating. Perhaps you spent your twenties trying to get through school, working, dealing with your health, or taking care of someone else. Perhaps you wanted a relationship but couldn’t figure out how people went from talking to each other to actually dating.

Now people your age are getting married, raising children, divorcing, or starting second marriages. You are wondering how to ask someone for coffee without making it strange.

The uncomfortable part isn’t necessarily being single. It’s the feeling that everyone else received instructions you missed.

You might worry that a potential partner will ask about your previous relationships. You might not know whether you’re supposed to initiate a kiss, how often to text, or when spending time together becomes dating. You might have watched enough romantic movies to recognize a dramatic declaration of love but have no idea how two ordinary people arrange a second date.

You can be an experienced adult and a beginner at dating. Those two things are not contradictory.

Your experience in one area of life doesn’t determine your experience in another. Dating is something you participate in, not a qualification awarded when you reach a particular birthday.

There are things you haven’t practiced yet. That matters. But it is a much smaller problem than the conclusion that something must be wrong with you.

There is no deadline you missed

Dating is often presented as a predictable sequence: first crush, first date, first kiss, first relationship, first heartbreak, and eventually a serious partnership.

Real lives are less orderly.

In a 2019 US survey, Pew Research Center found that 35% of never-married single adults reported never having been in a committed romantic relationship. Even among never-married singles aged 40 or older, 21% reported no previous committed relationship (Brown, 2020).

That does not mean 21% of all adults over 40 have never dated. The survey asked a particular group about committed relationships, not about whether they had ever gone on a date. Still, it establishes something important: adults without a previous committed relationship are not an imaginary or vanishingly rare group.

Sexual experience is a different matter. A US longitudinal study followed people who had not had partnered sexual activity by age 18. By a follow-up when participants were about 28 on average, roughly 13% of the female participants and 15% of the male participants in that initially inexperienced group still reported no partnered sexual experience (Haydon et al., 2014).

Those numbers describe people who were already sexually inexperienced at 18. They do not tell you how many adults have never dated. The distinction matters because a person can date without having sex, have sex without a committed relationship, or have a committed relationship without sexual activity.

There are also signs that teenage dating experiences have changed. A US research synthesis reported that around half of twelfth-graders in a 2017 survey said they had never dated, compared with about 15% in 1992. The authors cautioned that the meaning of the word dating had changed, making a direct comparison difficult (Sevareid et al., 2022).

None of these studies establishes one correct age to begin, or tells an individual when they will find a partner.

They do challenge the assumption that everybody else completed a standard dating curriculum before adulthood.

Being single is not the same as being inexperienced

In a separate 2022 US survey, Pew found that 30% of adults were single, including 47% of adults younger than 30. Among single adults, 57% were not looking for dates or a relationship at that time (Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

A person who isn’t looking might be content alone, recovering from a breakup, busy with other priorities, uncertain about what they want, or simply uninterested right now. The survey doesn’t establish the reason for each person.

Likewise, a study using US data from 2000 through 2018 found increases in the proportion of some young-adult groups reporting no sexual activity in the previous year. That is evidence about recent sexual activity, not evidence that those people had never dated or never had sex (Ueda et al., 2020).

The point is not to turn singleness into a competition over statistics.

It is to stop treating your own timeline as proof of failure.

You don’t have to invent a relationship history to appear normal. You don’t have to rush into a relationship to reach an imaginary milestone. And you don’t have to explain to strangers why your life took the route it did.

How the practice years can disappear

Imagine three people.

At 33, Daniel has never dated. His family had strict rules about socializing, and dating wasn’t permitted while he lived at home. When he finally moved out, he concentrated on establishing a career. Now he can support himself, but he has little experience showing romantic interest.

At 41, Priya has never had a serious relationship. She spent years caring for relatives and maintaining a demanding work schedule. She has friends, hobbies, and a life she values. She also has a quiet wish to share that life with someone.

At 27, Eli has been on several dates but never understood what happened afterward. Conversations seemed promising, then people stopped responding. Sometimes Eli assumed friendliness meant romantic interest. Other times, uncertainty made it easier to disappear than to risk rejection.

All three are beginners in different ways.

Daniel needs opportunities to practice starting romantic conversations. Priya needs room in her life for getting to know someone. Eli needs practice making intentions clear and checking what the other person actually wants.

None needs to become a different person before dating.

These are illustrative examples, not research findings about why particular groups start late. The available studies do not establish how much delayed dating can be attributed specifically to sheltered upbringings, homeschooling, childhood trauma, or ADHD.

One US study published in 2026 compared college students who had never been in romantic relationships with those who had. The inexperienced students reported feeling less ready and having fewer romantic and sexual experiences, but they also reported greater optimism about future dating. Their accounts included apprehension, social circumstances, and uncertainty about their ability to participate (LeFebvre & Love, 2026).

The sample was 369 college students, so it cannot tell us what most first-time daters at 40, 50, or 60 think or experience.

It does offer a useful distinction: feeling unprepared is not the same as believing there is no future for you.

If growing up left little room for dating

Some families allow friendships but discourage romance. Others have firm expectations about when, whom, or whether their children may date.

You may have grown up without privacy, transportation, opportunities to spend time with peers, or permission to make your own romantic choices.

If that sounds familiar, it can help to separate the rules you grew up with from the choices available to you now.

Perhaps you were taught that expressing attraction was inappropriate. As an adult, you can choose to express interest respectfully.

Perhaps you were taught that every date should lead toward marriage. As an adult, you can decide what kind of relationship you want, while remaining clear with anyone who has different expectations.

Perhaps you learned that disagreement was disrespectful. You can practice telling someone what you prefer without demanding that they agree.

You don’t have to reject everything about your upbringing to make your own decisions. You do need to recognize which decisions are yours to make.

If past experiences make dating feel frightening or difficult to approach, support from a qualified mental health professional may help. You do not need a diagnosis or a particular explanation for your history to ask for support.

What you missed, and what you didn’t

Teenage dating can provide opportunities to practice asking someone out, tolerating uncertainty, navigating attraction, setting limits, and recovering when things don’t work.

It can also involve confusion, pressure, and habits people later have to reconsider.

An adult beginner may lack some experience, but that doesn’t mean every experienced dater has learned those skills well.

The youth research reviewed by Sevareid and colleagues describes romantic relationships as opportunities to learn about communication, trust, affection, disagreement, and healthy choices. It also emphasizes learning to recognize and leave unhealthy relationships (Sevareid et al., 2022).

That does not prove a specific adult dating course will work. It does suggest that useful relationship skills involve more than accumulating partners.

Consider two people.

Jordan, 38, has been in five relationships but frequently assumes a partner should know what Jordan wants without being told.

Morgan, 38, has never dated but regularly asks clarifying questions, listens when others disagree, and can admit a mistake without blaming someone else.

Jordan has more dating history. Morgan has some communication habits that could be useful in dating.

Neither person’s history guarantees how a future relationship will go.

Experience is information about what you’ve done. It isn’t a certificate proving you know how to treat someone well.

The same applies in reverse. Lack of experience doesn’t prove you will make a considerate partner. You still have to learn, pay attention, and take responsibility for your behavior.

Your first relationship doesn’t have to make up for lost time

When you’ve waited years for something, it can begin to carry extraordinary importance.

A first date might feel like your last opportunity. A person who returns your interest might seem uniquely precious, even before you’ve learned much about them. You might imagine the entire relationship after one enjoyable conversation.

Or you may feel pressure to accelerate.

If you’re 42 and have never had a relationship, you might think you cannot afford six months of getting to know someone. If you want marriage or children, your personal timeline may involve genuine practical considerations.

Those considerations matter. But urgency cannot replace mutual willingness.

Being older doesn’t mean you have to skip the ordinary process of getting acquainted.

A first relationship doesn’t have to last forever to have been worthwhile. A first date doesn’t have to produce a relationship to have been worth attending. And reaching a milestone won’t automatically resolve every feeling you had before it.

The previous chapter explained that dating helps people discover whether they want to continue together. That remains true when you’ve been waiting a long time.

You may want something serious. The person across from you may want something serious too. You still need to discover whether you want it with each other.

You can learn without pretending to be experienced

There is no secret set of instructions that everyone else received.

But there are specific skills you can practice.

Showing interest clearly

You don’t need a perfect opening line.

Suppose you enjoy talking with someone at a community event. After getting acquainted, you might say:

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you like to get coffee with me sometime, as a date?”

The phrase as a date is useful because it removes one possible misunderstanding.

The person might say yes, no, or that they need time to think.

Each answer deserves respect. If the answer is uncertain, you have not received a yes. Don’t begin arranging a date on the assumption that agreement will eventually follow.

If the answer is no, accept it and leave the invitation there.

This is a skill worth learning independently of whether the invitation succeeds.

Planning something simple

An early date doesn’t have to impress anyone through expense or originality.

You might suggest meeting at a café, walking in a public park during the day, or attending a short public event.

The invitation should make the basic plan understandable: what, where, and approximately when.

If crowded cafés exhaust you, choose somewhere quieter. If an activity requires substantial money or travel, make sure both people know what they’re agreeing to.

The goal is to create an opportunity to interact comfortably, not to demonstrate that you’re an expert at romance.

Checking rather than guessing

Suppose someone has been friendly and laughing at your jokes. You want to know whether they’re interested romantically.

Their friendliness alone doesn’t answer the question.

You can express your interest once and invite a response. You can also ask about something unclear instead of assigning it a meaning.

The sculpture-and-glasses picture from earlier in the book applies here. You can see a situation from your position; someone else may experience it differently. Describing what you notice and asking what they mean is more reliable than insisting your interpretation must be correct.

You can use direct communication even if you’ve dated for decades.

Learning to decline

A beginner also needs practice saying no.

Suppose you enjoy meeting someone but don’t want another date.

You can say:

“Thank you for meeting me. I enjoyed our conversation, but I don’t feel a romantic connection. I don’t want to continue dating.”

You don’t have to construct a detailed explanation of someone’s shortcomings. You also don’t have to agree to another date because the other person spent money, traveled a long distance, or seems disappointed.

They have the same freedom to decline you.

The ability to hear a no and the ability to give one are both part of dating.

Making room for mistakes

You might talk too long because you’re nervous. You might misunderstand an invitation. You might forget to ask someone a question because you’re concentrating on what to say next.

When you notice a mistake, you can acknowledge it.

“I realize I’ve been talking about my work for a while. I’d like to hear more about what you enjoy.”

You don’t need to announce yourself as terrible at dating or ask the other person to reassure you repeatedly.

Recognize what happened, make an appropriate adjustment, and continue if both people want to.

A mistake is something to learn from. It is not an automatic verdict on your future.

Practice people skills without turning people into practice partners

If you want to become more comfortable talking with unfamiliar people, ordinary social interaction gives you opportunities to practice.

You might join a hobby group, talk with people at a community event, invite a friend to an activity, or become more comfortable asking a question and listening to the answer.

These activities have value even if nobody becomes a romantic partner.

That distinction is important.

A friendship isn’t a failed romance. A person who shares your hobby isn’t automatically a potential date. And being kind to someone doesn’t create a future claim on their attention or affection.

Instead of treating every interaction as a romantic opportunity, practice being present with the person you’re actually talking to.

You can be curious about someone without deciding whether they might become your partner.

You can enjoy a conversation without needing a second one.

You can build a social life while continuing to look for romance.

This also gives dating a more manageable place in your life. Not every interaction has to carry the weight of your hopes for a relationship.

A small exercise: your dating starting point

Take a piece of paper and complete these three sentences:

Something I already know how to do with other people is:

For example, listen patiently, organize activities, discuss difficult subjects, or show consideration when someone is tired.

Something I haven’t had much opportunity to practice is:

For example, expressing romantic interest, recognizing when interest is mutual, arranging a date, or saying no without apologizing repeatedly.

One small thing I could practice next is:

Choose an action within your control. Not “get a partner this month,” but perhaps “practice inviting someone to an ordinary activity” or “say clearly what I prefer when making plans.”

You are looking for the next skill, not a verdict on your worth.

Do you have to tell someone you’ve never dated?

This question can feel enormous when you have little experience.

Imagine you’re 36, having a pleasant first date, and someone asks:

“So, what was your last relationship like?”

Your mind may immediately produce several fears.

They’ll think there’s something wrong with me.

They’ll assume I’m difficult.

They’ll stop being interested.

There is some evidence that sexual inexperience can attract stigma. Across three US studies, Gesselman and colleagues found that sexually inexperienced adults reported feeling stigmatized and that respondents were less willing to consider sexually inexperienced people as romantic partners. That reluctance also appeared among some sexually inexperienced respondents (Gesselman et al., 2017).

The studies measured sexual inexperience, not every form of limited dating experience. They do not establish that everyone will reject a person who hasn’t dated.

Some people may decide they don’t want to date an inexperienced partner. Others may be comfortable with it. You cannot control their preferences, and you don’t need to treat one person’s preference as a statement about your value.

Honesty doesn’t require a confession

You don’t need to begin every first date with a summary of everything you’ve never done.

But if someone asks a relevant question, you can answer truthfully.

For example:

“I haven’t been in a serious relationship before. Other things took priority for a long time, and I’m interested in dating now.”

Or:

“I’m fairly new to dating, so sometimes I ask things directly rather than guessing.”

You can also say:

“That’s a personal part of my history. I’d rather talk about it once we know each other better.”

That last answer is appropriate when someone is asking for intimate details you are not comfortable sharing yet. Privacy is different from inventing a false relationship history.

As a relationship develops, you should be honest about information that affects your shared decisions, boundaries, and consent.

If you’re approaching sexual intimacy without experience, for example, it’s reasonable to say what you are and aren’t comfortable with and to ask questions. Neither person’s experience level creates an obligation to do anything.

Being new to dating doesn’t mean someone else is responsible for making every decision for you.

You can ask for clarity while still taking responsibility for your own choices.

If you’re dating someone who is inexperienced

You may be the more experienced person in the relationship.

Perhaps your date is 44 and has never had a serious partner. Perhaps you’re neurotypical and dating an autistic person who prefers direct conversation. Perhaps you’re both autistic, but one of you has dated extensively while the other hasn’t.

Don’t assume the less experienced person needs to be directed through everything.

Ask what they prefer. Explain your own preferences. Give each other room to answer.

If your date asks whether an outing is romantic, that question isn’t inherently a problem. If someone wants to discuss whether a kiss would be welcome, asking is an appropriate way to establish consent.

And if one partner needs longer to think, both people can discuss what time and clarity they need. Neither has to guess the other’s intentions.

The more experienced partner doesn’t become the authority. Both remain equal participants with their own boundaries and choices.

Different people, different pressures

Gender can affect what people assume dating should look like. But no gender has one universal experience, and statistics about groups cannot explain an individual person’s dating history.

The following situations may sound familiar. They are not descriptions of every person in each group.

Perspective: Men

If you’re a man who has never dated, you may encounter the expectation that you should already know how to initiate romance or handle rejection confidently.

Pew’s 2022 US survey found that 63% of men under 30 reported being single, compared with 34% of women under 30. Those figures don’t tell us why the difference exists or how many people in either group have never dated (Gelles-Watnick, 2023). They certainly don’t establish that someone else is responsible for your relationship status.

You can learn to express interest, make plans, and handle uncertainty without pretending confidence you don’t feel. You also don’t need to treat dating as a contest in which another person’s affection proves your worth.

Perspective: Women

If you’re a woman who has never dated, someone may respond by assuming you are unusually selective, uninterested in relationships, or keeping a secret. Those assumptions don’t explain your life.

Among never-married single adults in Pew’s 2019 survey, men and women reported similar rates of never having had a committed relationship: 35% and 37%, respectively (Brown, 2020).

You don’t have to accept attention simply because someone offers it. Being inexperienced doesn’t make your preferences less important, and wanting a relationship doesn’t require accepting one that you don’t want.

Perspective: Non-binary

If dating advice assumes every couple consists of a man and a woman, you may find that the examples don’t help you understand your own situation. You may also have to navigate questions about identity or disclosure that aren’t addressed by standard dating advice.

The cited US surveys do not provide reliable separate estimates of how many nonbinary adults are beginning to date later in life. That absence of data is not evidence that your experience is unusual.

You can ask directly about someone’s interest, describe your own preferences, and choose what personal information to share at a pace that respects both people’s ability to make informed decisions. You don’t have to fit yourself into somebody else’s gendered dating script.

If your brain works differently, experience still isn’t destiny

You might need more time to understand social situations. You might find loud venues exhausting. Perhaps you communicate best when someone says exactly what they mean.

Or you might enjoy flirting and socializing but struggle when a conversation shifts unexpectedly.

These experiences can affect how you approach dating, regardless of whether you identify as autistic, ADHD, both, neither, or something else.

One survey of 2,386 autistic and nonautistic adolescents and adults found that autistic respondents were less likely to report ever having had sexual activity. Among respondents who had been sexually active, however, the average age at first sexual activity did not differ significantly between the groups (Weir et al., 2021).

The sample was self-selected and largely based in the UK. Diagnoses were reported by participants and were not independently verified. The study did not measure age at first romantic relationship.

So it would be inaccurate to conclude that autistic people necessarily begin romantic relationships later.

It would be equally inaccurate to assume that an autistic person wants less romance, needs a particular type of partner, or cannot learn unfamiliar dating skills.

If your needs differ from someone else’s, the useful question is not whether you can imitate their way of dating.

It is whether you can find a way to participate that respects both people’s comfort and choices.

For example, you can suggest a quiet first meeting rather than enduring an environment that causes overload. You can explain that you sometimes need additional time to answer questions. If you experience shutdown, a potential partner can learn that silence doesn’t automatically mean agreement or rejection, and that important conversations may need to continue when you can both participate.

You are not required to use masking to conceal every difference in order to be considered a suitable partner.

The same principle applies when dating another neurodivergent person. Two people may share a label and still have very different needs.

You discover those differences by communicating, not by assuming you already know.

When loneliness starts changing the story you tell yourself

There is another part of starting late that deserves attention.

Sometimes being inexperienced is frustrating. Sometimes it’s painful.

You might watch friends celebrate anniversaries and wonder why you haven’t experienced the same things. You might spend holidays alone when you would prefer company. You might want affection, physical closeness, or the ordinary comfort of being important to someone.

You are allowed to be sad about that.

You are allowed to feel disappointed, jealous, angry, or tired of hearing that a relationship will happen when you least expect it.

But loneliness can begin to change how you interpret other people.

A review by Cacioppo and Hawkley connected perceived social isolation with greater attention to potential social threats, more negative interpretations, and biases that can reinforce expectations of rejection (Cacioppo & Hawkley, 2009).

That review concerns loneliness generally, not specifically adult dating. It doesn’t prove lonely people inevitably become bitter or hostile.

It helps explain why someone who expects rejection might begin seeing evidence of it everywhere.

The difference between an experience and an explanation

Suppose Mateo, 32, asks someone on a date. They decline.

The experience is straightforward: Mateo expressed interest, and the other person did not want to date him.

The disappointment is real.

But Mateo might add a second story:

People always reject me. There must be something fundamentally wrong with me.

After several disappointing experiences, he might go further:

Everyone of that gender is shallow. None of them cares about people like me.

Notice what has changed.

A person’s decision has become a judgment about Mateo’s entire identity. Then it has become a judgment about an entire gender.

Neither conclusion follows from the original event.

Mateo cannot know every reason behind the rejection. Even if someone states a reason, one person’s preference doesn’t represent everyone else’s.

This matters because a painful explanation can begin directing future behavior.

If you expect everyone to reject you, you may approach conversations defensively. If you assume people are dishonest, you may interpret ordinary uncertainty as deception. If you believe romantic attention is something you have earned, another person’s refusal may begin to feel like an injustice.

Those interpretations deserve examination before they determine how you treat someone.

Nobody owes you a relationship

This is a difficult truth when you have waited a long time.

You can be kind, thoughtful, responsible, employed, funny, attractive, generous, and genuinely interested in someone.

They still do not owe you a date.

You can listen carefully, respect their boundaries, remember their birthday, or support them through a difficult period.

They still do not owe you romance or sex.

Friendship does not create romantic debt. Buying dinner does not create sexual debt. Waiting patiently does not create a future obligation.

The same rules protect you.

You do not owe a relationship to someone who likes you. You don’t have to continue dating a person because they’re disappointed, lonely, or inexperienced.

A relationship requires two people who freely want to participate. One person’s need cannot replace the other’s consent.

That principle remains true regardless of age, gender, disability, sexual orientation, neurotype, or previous relationship experience.

It also gives you a way to separate disappointment from entitlement.

I wish someone wanted to date me expresses a longing.

Someone should have to date me because I’ve waited so long claims authority over another person’s choice.

You can take the first feeling seriously without accepting the second belief.

When a community makes the loneliness worse

People who feel isolated often want somewhere to talk about it.

That can be useful. Being able to describe your experiences to someone who understands them may provide relief.

But not every community offers a healthy explanation for those experiences.

Some online spaces organized around romantic rejection or involuntary celibacy present other people, especially women, as responsible for their members’ unhappiness. The word incel is often used for a particular online identity and set of beliefs, but involuntarily lacking sex or romance does not automatically mean someone identifies with that ideology.

In a small study comparing 67 self-identified incel men with 103 other men, the incel group reported more loneliness, lower perceived support, and more psychological distress. They also endorsed certain antagonistic beliefs about women more strongly (Sparks et al., 2024).

The study used selected participants, primarily recruited in Canada during the COVID period. It did not establish that loneliness causes hostility. In its statistical analyses, loneliness and social support did not uniquely explain incel-group membership after accounting for other measured factors.

It cannot tell us what most lonely or inexperienced adults think. It cannot tell us that autistic people are especially likely to adopt those beliefs. And it cannot establish equivalent patterns across all genders.

The relevant lesson is narrower: a community can recognize your pain while still offering a harmful explanation for it.

If a group repeatedly tells you that an entire category of people is dishonest, cruel, or responsible for your unhappiness, notice what happens to your thinking after spending time there.

Do you feel more able to interact respectfully with others?

Do you recognize more choices you can make?

Or do you leave convinced that ordinary conversation is pointless and that strangers deserve your anger?

You don’t have to deny your loneliness to reject an explanation that encourages contempt.

If you recognize yourself becoming resentful

Perhaps you have already started thinking about dating in terms of blame.

Maybe you’ve complained bitterly about an entire gender. Maybe you have interpreted a lack of interest as a deliberate insult. Perhaps you’ve continued seeking contact after someone made it clear they didn’t want it.

Recognizing those patterns is an opportunity to change what you do next.

If someone has declined contact or blocked you, stop contacting them. Do not seek another route to reach them.

If you have been participating in conversations that encourage entitlement or hostility, step away from those conversations.

If you find yourself making sweeping claims about an entire gender, return to what actually happened with the individual people involved.

And if resentment, anger, or repetitive thoughts are dominating your life, consider speaking with a qualified mental health professional.

You can be accountable for harmful behavior without reducing your whole identity to it.

The goal is not to pretend that rejection feels good. It is to keep that pain from becoming permission to mistreat someone else.

A life that contains more than the search for romance

If you’re lonely, being told to “just make friends” can feel dismissive.

Friendship and romance are not identical. A close friend may not satisfy your wish for a romantic partner. A hobby group doesn’t replace the desire for intimacy.

Still, romantic relationships are not the only relationships that matter.

The US Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory identifies social connection as a public-health concern and emphasizes the importance of connections across family, friendship, community, and other areas of life (Office of the Surgeon General, 2023).

Building those connections isn’t a consolation prize for failing to find a partner.

It is part of having a life with people in it.

And if loneliness has begun affecting how you interpret interactions, there are different approaches to consider.

A 2011 meta-analysis of 50 studies found that interventions addressing unhelpful social thinking performed particularly well among the randomized studies it examined (Masi et al., 2011).

A later meta-analytic review involving 128 studies found that various loneliness interventions produced some benefit, but confidence in the estimates was low or very low. The reviewers did not find strong evidence that one approach was clearly preferable to all others (Lasgaard et al., 2022).

Neither review tested a guaranteed method for finding romance or a specific program for leaving gender-directed resentment behind.

The practical options remain worth considering, without pretending that any one of them is a cure.

You might reconnect with someone you enjoy talking to. You might participate regularly in an activity where people get to know each other over time. You might discuss painful assumptions with a counselor or therapist. You might make changes to how much time you spend in online spaces that leave you feeling hostile or hopeless.

You can work on loneliness even while you continue wanting a romantic relationship.

One effort doesn’t cancel the other.

Your life does not begin with your first partner

Consider someone who is 46 and has never had a serious relationship.

They may have spent years working, caring for relatives, studying, building things, making friendships, traveling, learning, surviving difficult circumstances, or finding out what matters to them.

Some parts of dating will be unfamiliar.

Other parts of adult life will not.

A relationship, if they enter one, won’t turn those earlier years into a meaningless waiting period.

The same is true if you’re 22 or 67.

A romantic partner can become an important part of your life. They cannot retroactively make the years before meeting them worthless.

You also don’t have to turn inexperience into a permanent identity.

You may be new to dating today. After a few dates, some parts will feel more familiar. Other parts may remain confusing. Dating someone new can bring unfamiliar situations even for people with extensive relationship histories.

What matters is whether you’re learning to participate with honesty, curiosity, and respect for both people’s choices.

A different measure of progress

Instead of counting only dates, relationships, kisses, or other milestones, consider questions you can actually answer.

Did you express yourself clearly?

Did you pay attention to the other person’s response?

Did you respect an answer you didn’t want?

Did you notice when you were making assumptions?

Did you make a decision that reflected what you genuinely wanted, rather than what you thought someone your age was supposed to have done?

Those are worthwhile things to notice whether or not the interaction becomes romantic.

For example, Hannah, 37, has never asked anyone on a date. She finally invites a woman she enjoys talking with to a museum.

The woman declines.

Hannah is disappointed. Nothing romantic happens.

But Hannah was clear, respectful, and able to accept the answer. She has now done something that previously felt impossible.

She has not earned the next person’s affection. She has learned that she can express interest without needing to control the outcome.

That is progress within her control.

Start where you are

You cannot go back and arrange a different adolescence.

You cannot recover missed opportunities by rushing another person. And you cannot guarantee that the next person you like will like you in return.

But you can begin with the life, abilities, needs, and experience you actually have.

You can learn what an invitation sounds like. You can ask for clarification. You can practice accepting rejection without turning it into a judgment about yourself or an entire gender.

You can decide how much of your history to share and when. You can build connections outside romance. You can find out what feels comfortable, what doesn’t, and what you would like to try again.

You don’t need to have started at 15 to begin at 35.

And you don’t need to have dated five people to treat the first person you date with care.

Being a beginner means there are things left to learn.

It does not mean you’re less deserving of respect, or that anyone else owes you a relationship.

The next step is ordinary and sometimes intimidating: getting to know another person, saying what you mean, listening to what they mean, and finding out whether your interest is shared.
