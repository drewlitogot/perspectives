Chapter 46: Dating apps and profiles

A dating app can put hundreds of potential partners within reach. It can also leave you staring at your phone, wondering why nobody replies, why everyone looks the same, or how you’re supposed to describe an entire person in three photographs and a paragraph.

For someone who finds approaching strangers difficult, an app can make introductions easier. You can read about someone before speaking, choose your words, and respond when you’re ready. You don’t have to recognize a smile across a crowded room or guess whether someone is available.

But the app introduces its own difficulties. A message can be ambiguous. A promising conversation can end without explanation. You might spend an evening deciding whom to contact and finish feeling less hopeful than when you started.

The important distinction is that a dating app helps you meet people. It does not determine who will be a good partner.

In a nationally representative Pew Research Center survey conducted in 2022, 30% of US adults said they had tried online dating. Among adults who were married or in a committed relationship, one in ten said they had met their current partner through a dating site or app. Among people who had ever used online dating, 53% described their overall experience as positive and 46% as negative (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

These figures describe an established way of meeting people, not a guarantee of success. They also describe experiences in 2022, not necessarily the current app market.

You can find a relationship through an app. You can find one without ever installing an app. Neither choice makes you more or less serious about dating.

What an app actually does

Most dating apps begin with a profile: a small collection of pictures and information about you. You can usually look at other people’s profiles and indicate whether you’re interested in getting to know them.

On many apps, a match means both people have indicated some interest. Depending on the service, one or both people may then be able to send a message.

That is all a match establishes.

It does not mean the other person wants a relationship with you, is available to meet immediately, or has agreed to anything beyond the interaction the app allows. You are still strangers learning about each other.

Dating apps offer three different things:

  • Access: You can encounter people you might never meet in ordinary life.
  • Communication: You can exchange information before deciding whether to meet.
  • Matching: The app may recommend people based on preferences, behavior, or its own system for ranking profiles.

A major review of online dating research distinguished these functions. It recognized the value of broader access while questioning whether commercial matching algorithms could reliably identify long-term compatibility. That review appeared in 2012, so it cannot establish how well any particular algorithm works today (Finkel et al., 2012).

The practical lesson still matters: a suggested match is an introduction, not a compatibility verdict.

Two people might both love science fiction, want children, and live ten miles apart. They might nevertheless find conversation exhausting together. Two others might have very different hobbies but enjoy each other’s company, respect each other’s needs, and want the same kind of life.

A profile gives you clues. The relationship, if one develops, is where you learn whether those clues add up to something good.

Choosing an app that fits your life

The best app for you is not necessarily the one with the most advertisements, the most users nationwide, or the cleverest promise about finding your perfect partner.

It is one that gives you a reasonable opportunity to meet people you’re interested in, while allowing you to participate comfortably.

Start with what you’re looking for

Before choosing a platform, decide what kind of connection you want.

Someone seeking a committed, monogamous relationship needs different information from someone seeking casual dating. Someone interested in a nonmonogamous relationship needs to know whether potential partners want a compatible arrangement. Someone exploring dating for the first time may need space to say they are still figuring out their goals.

A platform is more useful when it lets you express your intentions accurately and recognize other people’s.

Look at what the app actually allows you to say. Can you identify your relationship goals? Can you describe your daily life? Does the app provide enough information to tell whether a conversation might be worth starting?

Do not rely only on a platform’s reputation.

In Pew’s 2022 survey, Tinder was the most commonly reported platform among people who had ever used dating apps, followed by services including Match and Bumble. Hinge, eharmony, and OkCupid also appeared in the results. Their use varied by age, and platforms such as Grindr and HER served particular communities (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

Those figures describe who had used each service, not which service produced the best relationships. An app’s popularity in 2022 is not proof of its effectiveness for you in 2026.

Compare the experience, not just the name

Consider six practical questions.

Are there people you could realistically date? An app with millions of users nationally may have few compatible people near you. Distance matters differently depending on whether you drive, use public transportation, or are comfortable with a long-distance relationship.

Can you represent yourself accurately? Look at the available gender, orientation, relationship, and personal-description options. If an important part of your identity or relationship preferences cannot be expressed appropriately, that matters.

Does the app provide enough information? If you prefer learning about someone before expressing interest, a service with written profiles or detailed prompts may feel more comfortable than one emphasizing quick reactions to pictures.

Can you filter for important incompatibilities? You might need to know about children, relationship structure, smoking, or geographic distance before beginning a conversation.

Can you control the experience? Consider notifications, visibility settings, messaging controls, accessibility, reporting options, and whether the interface creates overload.

What can you actually do for free? Compare free features and paid limitations before subscribing. Paying for extra visibility or features should not be confused with buying a better relationship.

You do not need to investigate every app in existence.

Pick one that appears to fit your needs, explore its available features, and notice how using it feels. If it doesn’t suit you, choosing a different platform is an ordinary adjustment.

For a first attempt, one app is enough.

Your photographs are an introduction, not an examination

A profile picture has a straightforward job: help another person recognize you and get an initial sense of your life.

It does not have to prove that you’re beautiful, adventurous, wealthy, popular, or effortlessly confident.

In a Dutch laboratory study involving 48 young adult participants, photographs tended to attract early attention when people viewed dating profiles. But participants also spent substantial attention on written information. Both pictures and words contributed to impressions (Zanden, Mos, et al., 2022).

This was a small experiment using mock profiles, not a test of which photographs lead to successful relationships. It offers a useful correction to the claim that nobody reads profiles.

Choose pictures that tell the truth

Imagine someone looking at your photographs and then meeting you for coffee.

Would they recognize you?

That is a good starting question.

Use a reasonably recent, clear photograph that shows what you ordinarily look like. You can choose a picture in which you feel comfortable and attractive without digitally changing important features of your appearance.

Other photographs can tell someone a little about your interests or ordinary life.

A photograph of you gardening might reveal more about your weekends than a carefully posed picture in an unfamiliar luxury hotel. A picture at a museum, with a pet, on a hiking trail, or enjoying a hobby can give someone a natural subject for conversation.

You don’t have to show exciting vacations. If you enjoy quiet weekends, your photographs can reflect that.

Nor do you need to remove the things that help you function comfortably. Glasses, mobility aids, hearing protection, and clothing chosen for sensory comfort are parts of ordinary life, not defects to conceal.

If you dislike posing, ask someone you trust to take a few relaxed photographs while you’re doing something you enjoy. If you prefer taking pictures yourself, that is fine too.

A picture should not require you to perform a personality you cannot comfortably maintain.

Be mindful of what is visible in the background. Avoid unnecessarily revealing private information, such as your home address or workplace details. The next chapter covers online safety more fully.

What if you don’t like how you look in pictures?

You might notice every detail you dislike about yourself while looking at a photograph.

Someone else does not know the list of things you have been criticizing.

Your task is not to persuade everyone to find you attractive. It is to present yourself truthfully so people who might enjoy knowing you can recognize you.

A person who isn’t attracted to you is not a failed outcome. Physical attraction matters, and different people will respond differently.

You are choosing potential partners, too.

Writing a profile without becoming a salesperson

Many dating profiles sound remarkably alike.

“I’m laid-back.”

“I love having fun.”

“I like movies, music, food, and traveling.”

None of those statements is necessarily false. The problem is that they tell a stranger very little about what spending time with you would actually involve.

An experiment involving 1,234 users of Dutch dating websites found that more original profile texts received more favorable impressions on certain measures. Concrete personal information and individual writing style were associated with perceived originality (Zanden, Schouten, et al., 2022).

Most participants were older adults, and the study measured impressions and stated dating interest, not actual relationship success. It does not establish that everyone needs to write creatively or make jokes.

The useful idea is simpler: specific details help another person imagine your ordinary life.

Show what your words mean

Instead of:

“I enjoy spending time outdoors.”

Try:

“Most Saturdays start with coffee and a walk through a park. A quiet afternoon beats a crowded festival.”

Instead of:

“I’m a huge nerd.”

Try:

“I repair old radios, enjoy science-fiction novels, and can happily spend an afternoon learning how something works.”

Instead of:

“I’m looking for someone loyal who doesn’t play games.”

Try:

“I’m looking for a committed relationship where both people communicate directly and make time for each other.”

The revised versions don’t sound impressive on purpose. They make the person easier to understand.

Someone who prefers loud parties might recognize that the quiet-park person isn’t their best match. Someone who loves learning how things work might recognize a possible connection.

Both outcomes are useful.

Three things a profile can communicate

A short profile often has room for three kinds of information.

What is your ordinary life like? Mention a few things you genuinely do, enjoy, or care about. Choose things you would be happy to discuss.

What kind of connection do you want? Say whether you’re looking for a long-term relationship, casual dating, companionship, or something else you can describe honestly.

What would help someone start a conversation? Include a detail they can ask about, such as a hobby, favorite place, or something you’re learning.

Consider this example from a fictional 44-year-old man named Daniel:

Weekends usually mean cooking something new, taking my dog to the park, and catching up with friends. Looking for a steady relationship with someone who enjoys both conversation and quiet time. Currently learning to make decent bread. Results have been unpredictable.

Daniel hasn’t claimed to be unusually interesting. He has offered a recognizable picture of his life.

Now consider Priya, a 32-year-old woman interested in dating women:

Museum afternoons, cooperative board games, and long conversations about books. I’m happiest with plans made ahead of time and plenty of time to recharge afterward. Looking for a serious relationship. What’s a book you’ve wanted to talk about with someone?

Priya has described her preferences without apologizing for them.

These are examples, not formulas. You can write something shorter, more formal, more playful, or more direct.

If humor comes naturally, use it. If it doesn’t, don’t manufacture it.

You do not have to turn yourself into a comedian to be worth meeting.

Be accurate about the life you’re offering

One temptation is to write about the person you wish you were instead of the person you are.

Perhaps you would like to travel extensively someday, but currently prefer staying home. Perhaps you’re trying to become more active but don’t yet enjoy vigorous hiking.

It’s fine to mention aspirations. Just distinguish them from your current habits.

“Learning to enjoy outdoor activities” is different from “Always out hiking.”

Likewise, don’t claim to enjoy constant spontaneity because you think it sounds appealing if unexpected changes leave you exhausted.

The goal is not to win attention from people who would dislike your real life.

It is to make it easier for someone compatible to recognize you.

Proofread for clarity, not perfection

Before publishing, read your profile once to check whether a stranger could understand it.

Correct confusing wording if you notice it. You can ask a trusted friend to check it, or use a writing aid.

But an occasional typo is not a measure of intelligence, character, or worth. Dyslexia, language differences, disability, and individual writing styles do not disappear because someone is dating.

Clarity is the goal. Perfection isn’t required.

How much should you tell strangers about yourself?

A dating profile is public-facing information, not your complete personal history.

You can be honest without telling every stranger about your health, family difficulties, past relationships, trauma, or neurotype.

You might also prefer to disclose something early because it matters to how you want to be understood.

Both approaches are reasonable.

Deciding whether to mention autism

A 2026 survey of 102 autistic adults who had used dating platforms found that 54.5% had not disclosed being autistic on their profiles. Some shared that information later. Reasons for disclosure included wanting to be authentic, preventing misunderstandings, and identifying compatible people. Reasons for not disclosing included concerns about stigma and safety (Gibbs et al., 2026b).

The sample was self-selected and mostly Australian, so the percentage should not be treated as a rule for autistic people generally.

Reported reactions varied. Some people responded with acceptance or shared that they were autistic themselves. Others responded with stereotypes or ended contact. Interviews from the same research project described disclosure as a decision whose results could go in different directions (Gibbs et al., 2026b, 2026a).

There is no universal correct moment.

If you want to mention autism in your profile, you can. If you’d rather wait until you know someone, you can do that too.

Not publicly announcing a diagnosis or identity is not dishonesty.

You may also describe practical preferences without using any diagnosis:

“I appreciate direct communication.”

“I usually need a little time to think before answering.”

“I enjoy quieter places where conversation is easy.”

“I prefer making plans ahead of time.”

These sentences communicate things that matter to dating. They’re useful whether you’re autistic, ADHD, neurotypical, uncertain about a label, or simply know what suits you.

If someone asks about something you’d rather discuss later, you can say so. They are also free to decide what information they need before continuing.

Chapter 25, Disclosure, explores that decision in more detail.

If someone shares a neurotype with you

Someone disclosing that they’re autistic or ADHD isn’t asking you to diagnose them or explain their personality back to them.

You can respond with ordinary interest:

“Thanks for telling me. Is there anything about communication or planning dates that you’d like me to know?”

Listen to the actual answer.

An autistic person might prefer written communication and need extra processing time. Another autistic person might love spontaneous conversation. A neurotypical person might need just as much time to reply because of work, fatigue, or personal preference.

Two autistic people dating each other still need to learn one another’s needs. Sharing a label does not mean sharing identical experiences.

Recall the sculpture-and-glasses picture: knowing something about another person’s glasses doesn’t tell you everything they can see.

If you're dating someone who needs time to answer

A pause between messages does not automatically mean someone has lost interest. They may need time to find words, consider a question, manage other responsibilities, or recover energy. This can apply to anyone.

Rather than guessing, ask what messaging pace feels comfortable. You can explain your preferences too. A mutually workable arrangement respects both people’s needs instead of assuming either person’s communication style is the correct one.

Filtering: decide what matters before you start choosing people

Dating apps may show you more potential partners than you could realistically get to know.

That makes filtering useful. It also makes it easy to confuse selecting a partner with shopping for a product.

A filter should help you identify important compatibility, not make a person satisfy every imaginable preference.

Start with the distinction from Chapter 13, Compatibility: a dealbreaker is something that makes a relationship unsuitable for you, even if you like the person. A preference is something you enjoy but might reconsider.

For example, Avery is 43 and knows they do not want children. Someone who strongly wants to become a parent is probably not a suitable long-term partner for Avery.

That difference matters more than whether they both listen to the same music.

Avery might prefer someone who enjoys museums but could have a wonderful relationship with someone who prefers gardening.

It helps to decide on your important incompatibilities before looking through profiles, rather than inventing a new requirement every time someone seems slightly different from an imagined ideal partner.

Read profiles as invitations to learn

Suppose a profile says:

“Love being outdoors.”

Does that mean daily ten-mile hikes? Gardening? Sitting beside a lake? Taking a short walk after dinner?

You cannot know.

If the person otherwise interests you, you can ask:

“When you say you love being outdoors, what do you usually like doing?”

That question is more useful than silently deciding what the phrase means.

Likewise, “family-oriented” might mean wanting children, being close to siblings, regularly caring for relatives, or something else.

The app provides a starting point. Conversation fills in the missing information.

A 2012 review questioned how much long-term compatibility could be inferred before people interacted, and small qualitative studies of autistic app users likewise found that profiles and messaging could help with screening while leaving considerable uncertainty (Finkel et al., 2012; Gibbs et al., 2026a; Safran & Hurwitz, 2026).

Don’t assume a lack of instant excitement means incompatibility

A photograph or two sentences may not inspire a strong feeling. That does not tell you everything about how you would experience the person in conversation.

At the same time, you do not owe anyone a conversation because they look suitable on paper.

You are allowed to be selective.

The question is whether you are making choices based on what matters to you, or rejecting people for not matching a fantasy built from very little information.

Starting a conversation without performing

Once you match, you may need to send the first message.

You do not need an extraordinary opening line.

You can mention something from the person’s profile and ask a genuine question.

For example:

“Your profile mentions restoring old furniture. What’s something you’ve worked on recently?”

Or:

“You mentioned that you prefer quiet weekends. What does a good Saturday look like for you?”

If there isn’t much information in the profile, a simple greeting and question are enough:

“Hi, nice to match with you. How has your week been?”

These are openings, not methods for making someone respond.

A good conversation requires interest and participation from both people.

Someone might reply quickly, slowly, briefly, or not at all. A reply is an opportunity to continue learning about each other, not proof of attraction or commitment.

If someone doesn’t respond, you do not need to keep sending messages until they do. If someone declines further contact, accept that decision.

Chapter 23, Texting and messaging, covers response times, tone, and misunderstandings. The same principles apply here.

When the conversation feels different from what you expected

In interviews with 20 autistic US adults aged 18–29, participants described how dating apps made introductions possible without eliminating communication difficulties. Challenges appeared in interpreting messages, presenting themselves, dealing with rejection, and handling uncertainty (Safran & Hurwitz, 2026).

This is a useful reminder that exchanging written messages is still a social interaction.

If somebody says, “Maybe we could meet sometime,” you might not know whether that is a genuine invitation or a polite expression of interest.

You can ask:

“Would you like to make an actual plan, or would you rather keep chatting for now?”

They can choose either option.

If the conversation has been comfortable and you want to meet, you can make one straightforward invitation:

“I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you be interested in meeting for coffee sometime next week?”

A clear yes makes it possible to discuss a plan. Uncertainty means asking what the other person wants or giving them time. A no means no.

You don’t have to turn a conversation into a date immediately, nor do you need to maintain a long conversation that isn’t moving toward anything both people want.

Different experiences on the same apps

One person’s app might be nearly silent. Another person’s might be filled with messages they don’t have the energy to answer.

Neither person is necessarily using it incorrectly.

In Pew’s survey, among people who had used online dating during the previous year, 55% reported feeling insecure at least sometimes because they received too few messages. Another 36% reported feeling overloaded by too many messages (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

The survey also identified differences between men’s and women’s average reported experiences.

These are different positions around the same sculpture, not descriptions of what every man or woman experiences.

Perspective: Men

Among male dating-app users in Pew’s past-year sample, 64% reported feeling insecure at least sometimes because they received too few messages, compared with 40% of women (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

If you receive few responses, you may begin interpreting that silence as evidence that you’re unattractive or unwanted. But the number of messages you receive through one app is not a measure of your worth. You can review whether your profile represents you clearly, consider another way of meeting people, and give yourself permission to stop using an experience that is hurting you. A stranger’s silence is not an obligation they have failed to fulfill.

Perspective: Women

Among female dating-app users in Pew’s past-year sample, 54% said they felt overloaded by too many messages at least sometimes, compared with 25% of men (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

If you receive more attention than you can comfortably manage, you do not have to answer everyone or turn every match into a conversation. You can limit how many conversations you maintain, choose what information you share, and stop engaging when an interaction doesn’t feel right. More attention does not necessarily mean better options, and receiving a message does not create an obligation to continue.

Perspective: Non-binary

A dating app’s identity categories and matching options matter when they do not describe you accurately or when you are unsure how your information will be shown to other users.

The Pew report did not separately analyze transgender and nonbinary users, and the gender-diverse groups in the available autism-specific studies were small (Gibbs et al., 2026b; McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023). There is not enough evidence here to assign you a particular pattern of messages or experiences.

When choosing an app, consider whether its identity options, visibility settings, and intended dating community allow you to participate in a way that feels accurate and comfortable. You can decide what personal information you want to share publicly and what belongs in later conversation. If a platform consistently makes you uncomfortable or misrepresents you, you can choose not to use it.

People of every gender can experience too little attention, too much attention, rejection, uncertainty, and unwanted behavior.

The point of recognizing differences is to understand why another person’s experience may be unlike yours, not to decide whose experience is harder.

When the app starts consuming your energy

You open the app while waiting for dinner.

Twenty minutes later, you’re still there. You’ve looked at dozens of profiles, reread one unanswered message, and checked whether someone has replied.

You haven’t learned much about anyone, but you feel worse.

Perhaps the next day you do the same thing.

Dating apps can become draining even when you’re genuinely interested in meeting people.

Pew found that 79% of past-year users felt excited about online dating at least sometimes, while 88% felt disappointed at least sometimes (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023). Those findings describe the same broad user population. Dating apps can produce hope and disappointment together.

A longitudinal study following dating-app users over twelve weeks reported increasing emotional exhaustion and feelings of inefficacy. Participants with greater preexisting depression, anxiety, or loneliness also tended to report more difficulties. The study began with 493 participants but retained only 190 at its final wave, limiting what can be concluded (Sharabi et al., 2026).

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis covering 27 studies and 21,263 adults found associations between swiping-based dating-app use and several measures of psychological distress and appearance-related concerns. General wellbeing differences were small and not statistically significant. Most underlying studies were observational and had important limitations (Cela & Wood, 2026).

Neither finding establishes that using an app causes a particular mental health problem. Someone feeling lonely may use apps differently, and app experiences may interact with feelings that were already present.

What matters in your daily life is whether your current approach is helping you meet people without consuming more energy than you can spare.

Give dating apps a place in your schedule

Rather than opening an app whenever you feel bored, lonely, or uncertain, decide when you want to use it.

For example, you might check messages for a short period in the evening and leave the app alone during work or before bed.

You can also decide how many active conversations you can comfortably maintain.

One thoughtful conversation might be enough. Another person may enjoy several at once.

You do not need to maximize your activity.

Your attention, time, and energy are resources you can choose how to spend.

If you lose track of time or forget to reply

If you regularly open an app for five minutes and discover much more time has passed, try using an ordinary phone reminder or a planned checking period. If messages disappear from your attention when you close the app, a reminder to check your conversations may help.

These are practical options, not proven dating-app treatments. You might have ADHD, another reason for difficulty with attention, or no diagnosis at all. The aim is to make participation easier without requiring constant availability.

If too many conversations become overload

You may find that a small number of conversations is comfortable, but several simultaneous exchanges become difficult to follow. You might need time to process what each person said, especially when their messages are vague or emotionally intense.

It’s reasonable to keep fewer conversations active, silence unnecessary notifications, and take time between replies. You can tell someone that you prefer a slower messaging pace. The other person can decide whether that works for them.

Notice when you’re using the app for reassurance

There is a difference between opening an app because you want to meet someone and opening it because you desperately want evidence that someone wants you.

You might check repeatedly for a new match after a difficult day. You might find yourself comparing your message count with a friend’s, or judging your appearance based on who responds.

A small amount of attention can feel wonderful in the moment. Its absence can feel terrible.

If this happens, ask yourself:

“Am I looking for someone I might enjoy getting to know, or am I looking for relief from feeling unwanted?”

You don’t need to condemn yourself for the answer.

But if the app has become your main way of measuring whether you’re desirable, a break may help. Spending time with friends, pursuing interests, resting, or meeting people in other settings can give dating a more manageable place in your life.

Taking a break isn’t giving up

You can pause an app, stop notifications, remove it from your phone, or deactivate your profile if the service provides that option.

You can return later, try a different service, or decide that online dating isn’t right for you.

Taking a break does not mean you’ve missed your chance.

Nor does being determined to find a relationship require enduring every unpleasant feature of a platform.

In qualitative studies, autistic adults described the benefits of apps, including wider access to people and time to communicate, alongside exhaustion, stigma, and difficult interactions (Gibbs et al., 2026a; Neumann et al., 2024).

A tool can be useful and still be unsuitable for you at a particular time.

What success looks like

It’s tempting to measure dating-app success with numbers:

How many people liked your profile? How many matches did you get? How many people answered? How many conversations did you start?

Those numbers can tell you something about activity on the platform. They cannot tell you whether you’ve met a compatible partner.

Imagine two people.

Nico, 29, receives several matches each week. He enjoys the excitement but finds that most conversations lead nowhere. He begins feeling pressured to keep checking, even when he’d rather spend time with his friends.

Renee, 51, receives fewer matches. She has one conversation with a woman who shares her interest in history and prefers direct communication. They enjoy exchanging messages and decide they would like to meet.

The numbers alone would make Nico appear more successful.

But Renee has made progress toward what she actually wants: getting to know someone who might be compatible.

Nico may benefit from changing how he uses the app. Renee may benefit from continuing at a pace that suits her.

Neither person’s experience establishes what will happen next.

The purpose of dating isn’t to accumulate approval. It is to discover whether two people want to spend time together.

Sometimes that discovery leads to a relationship. Sometimes it leads to a respectful decision not to continue.

Both provide information.

A small exercise: build a profile you can actually live with

Before creating or revising your profile, take a few minutes away from the app.

Write down three things:

An ordinary part of your life. What do you genuinely enjoy doing in a typical week? It can be quiet, unusual, simple, or highly specific.

The connection you’re seeking. What do you know about the kind of relationship or dating experience you want right now?

Something another person could ask about. What interest, activity, or experience would you be comfortable discussing with a stranger?

Use those answers to write a short paragraph.

Then check it with one question:

“If someone liked this profile, would I feel comfortable being the person it describes?”

If the answer is no, revise it.

You can do the same with your photographs. Choose pictures that a person could recognize as you and that represent aspects of your actual life.

Finally, decide how you want to use the app: how often you’ll check it, how many conversations you can comfortably maintain, and when you’ll take a break.

You do not need to create a perfect profile before you begin. You need one that’s honest enough to start a real conversation.

The person is more important than the profile

Eventually, someone may interest you.

Their pictures are appealing. Their profile mentions something you love. Their replies seem thoughtful.

It’s easy to begin imagining what a relationship with them might be like.

Remember what you actually know.

You know what they’ve chosen to share and what you’ve experienced in conversation. You do not yet know how you feel together in person, how they behave across different situations, or whether the two of you can build a relationship that works for both.

You are allowed to feel hopeful without treating that hope as a promise.

Keep your curiosity attached to the real person rather than an imagined future.

And before moving a connection beyond the app, take online safety seriously. The next chapter covers scams, false identities, and protecting your information. Chapter 48 covers safety when meeting strangers.

After that comes the next practical question: you’ve met someone you’d like to see. How do you plan a date that works for both of you?
