Twenty minutes into a first date, Morgan is running out of things to say.

The conversation started well. Morgan likes the person sitting across the table. They seem thoughtful, funny, and genuinely interested. But the restaurant has become crowded. Music is playing overhead, dishes are clattering, and conversations from three nearby tables keep interrupting Morgan’s thoughts.

Their date, Alex, asks a perfectly reasonable question.

“So, what do you usually do on weekends?”

Morgan knows the answer. They have plenty of interests. But right now, choosing an answer, finding the words, and following Alex’s expression while trying to ignore the noise feels almost impossible.

“Not much,” Morgan says.

Alex smiles politely. The conversation slows.

On the way home, Morgan thinks, Why am I so bad at dating?

Alex has a different question: Did Morgan even like me?

Neither person has the full picture.

Morgan wasn’t necessarily uninterested. Alex wasn’t necessarily asking too much. They experienced the same conversation under different conditions, then interpreted what happened using the information available to them.

Change one thing, perhaps moving that date to a quiet café, and the evening might have unfolded differently.

This is a useful place to begin learning about dating: your experience of a situation is not necessarily the same as someone else’s, even when you’re both sitting at the same table.

Understanding that difference can change how you understand yourself, how you interpret other people, and what you do when something doesn’t go as expected.

There is more than one way to experience the world

Think back to the sculpture-and-glasses picture from How perspectives work. Two people can look at the same thing and notice different details.

Your brain is part of those glasses.

One person walks into a crowded party and immediately notices who they know. Another notices the music, the temperature, and the difficulty of moving between people. Someone else notices a person standing alone and wonders whether they want company.

None of those observations tells the whole story.

The same is true of conversation.

Some people think while speaking. Others need to think before speaking. Some remember the exact words someone used but miss the tone. Others notice emotional changes almost immediately yet struggle to remember details of what was said.

Some people enjoy a spontaneous invitation. Others enjoy spending time with someone but need advance notice to prepare.

These differences become important when dating because dating asks you to do several things at once: meet someone new, pay attention, communicate, make decisions, notice your own reactions, and consider another person’s feelings.

That can be enjoyable. It can also be demanding.

You don’t have to experience those demands in the same way as everyone else.

And you don’t have to understand every part of your brain before you begin.

Five differences worth noticing

There is no single test that tells you what kind of dater you are. A more useful starting point is to notice what makes everyday interactions easier or harder.

Five areas are particularly worth exploring.

1. What you notice and feel around you

Imagine meeting someone at a concert.

For one person, the music creates excitement. The crowd adds energy. Standing close to someone they like feels wonderful.

For another, the volume makes conversation impossible. The flashing lights are exhausting. Being bumped by strangers makes it difficult to relax.

The first person may leave feeling energized. The second may leave needing hours of quiet.

Neither response tells you how much either person liked their date.

People differ in how they process sound, light, smell, touch, movement, and other sensory information. These differences are particularly well documented among autistic people, although they are not exclusive to autism. A meta-analysis found substantial associations between sensory processing differences and emotional or behavioral difficulties in autistic samples, without establishing that one causes the other. (Chen et al., 2024)

Some people find particular sensations too intense. Others barely notice them or actively enjoy seeking them out. A person can experience both, depending on the sensation.

You might dislike unexpected touch while enjoying a firm hug from someone you trust. You might seek excitement but dislike loud background conversations.

This isn’t a contradiction. Your preferences don’t have to fit one simple category.

For now, pay attention to the circumstances under which you can actually enjoy another person’s company.

Chapter 3 will help you explore your sensory profile in greater detail.

2. What captures and holds your attention

During a conversation, what happens to your attention?

Perhaps you become deeply interested in one subject and can discuss it for an hour. Perhaps the movement behind your date keeps drawing your eyes away. Maybe you’re listening carefully until a new thought arrives, and suddenly you realize you missed part of their sentence.

Attention isn’t always something you can direct perfectly.

ADHD, for example, can involve difficulties sustaining attention, managing distractions, or regulating impulsive behavior. But those experiences alone don’t establish that someone has ADHD. (National Institute of Mental Health, 2024)

Attention also doesn’t tell you everything about caring.

Someone may feel strongly interested in another person and still have trouble following a conversation in a distracting setting.

At the same time, your date cannot automatically know what’s happening inside your head.

If you appear distracted, they may reasonably wonder whether you’re interested.

A brief explanation can help:

“There’s a lot going on around us, and I’m having trouble concentrating. Could we move somewhere quieter? I’d like to hear what you were saying.”

That tells the other person what’s happening without asking them to guess.

3. How you understand other people

Consider this exchange:

“Would you like to get coffee sometime?”

“Yeah, that could be fun.”

One person hears an invitation that has been accepted. Another hears a friendly but uncertain response. A third hears an expression of politeness rather than genuine interest.

The words are the same. The interpretations differ.

People vary in how they read facial expressions, tone, implied meaning, pauses, and indirect statements.

They also differ in how much confidence they place in those signals.

You might notice subtle changes in someone’s expression but struggle to understand what they mean. You might focus on the literal words and overlook a possible implication. Or you might assume a meaning that the other person never intended.

The important distinction is between what happened and what you concluded from it.

What happened: Someone said coffee could be fun.

What you concluded: They definitely want a date.

That conclusion might be correct. It might not.

A useful next step is to ask a clear, respectful question:

“Would you like to choose a day, or would you rather leave it open?”

The person can answer either way.

Clear communication isn’t permission to keep asking until you get the answer you want. Once someone declines, withdraws interest, or asks for space, respect that answer.

The goal is understanding, not persuasion.

4. How you remember and organize things

Suppose you meet someone you really like.

You remember that they have a dog named Pepper, that they dislike horror movies, and that they’re preparing for an interview.

But you forget to reply to their message.

Or you remember the date perfectly but arrive fifteen minutes late because you underestimated how long getting ready would take.

Memory, planning, time management, and organization can affect relationships in ways that aren’t immediately obvious. These are among the areas that can be difficult for people with ADHD, although similar experiences can have other explanations. (National Institute of Mental Health, 2024)

Your intentions matter, but so does the effect of your behavior.

If you repeatedly arrive late, another person may feel that their time doesn’t matter. If you forget plans, they may wonder whether you want to see them.

Explaining a difficulty can make your behavior more understandable. It doesn’t automatically remove the inconvenience for someone else.

The useful question is not simply, Do I care?

It’s also, What will help me show that I care?

That might mean setting reminders, writing plans down immediately, choosing realistic meeting times, or agreeing on a way to confirm arrangements.

If remembering is harder than caring

You may feel intensely interested in someone and still forget a message, an appointment, or something they asked you to do.

Don’t use your feelings as the only measure of your reliability. Find a practical system that helps your actions match your intentions.

If an arrangement matters, put it somewhere you’ll actually see it. If you make a mistake, acknowledge the effect on the other person and explain what you’ll change. They can understand your difficulty without being required to absorb every consequence of it.

5. How strongly and quickly you react

Someone takes longer than usual to respond to your message.

You might barely notice.

You might wonder whether they’re busy.

Or your attention might return to the unanswered message repeatedly, making it difficult to concentrate on anything else.

People differ in their emotional responses, including how quickly reactions develop and how difficult they are to manage.

A clinical review found that difficulties regulating emotions are common among people with ADHD, though researchers have disagreed about their precise role in the condition. (Shaw et al., 2014)

Emotional intensity can also have other explanations.

The important point isn’t to judge your feelings by how somebody else would react. It’s to recognize the difference between an emotion and what you decide to do with it.

Feeling anxious because someone hasn’t replied doesn’t establish that they’ve rejected you.

Feeling irritated because plans changed doesn’t make the other person inconsiderate.

Feeling excited after one wonderful conversation doesn’t establish that you’ve found a lifelong partner.

Your feelings provide information about your experience. They don’t automatically provide information about someone else’s intentions.

You can take those feelings seriously without treating every interpretation as a fact.

And you can decide to wait, ask a reasonable question, or make a different choice without dismissing what you feel.

Sometimes the problem is the amount coming in

Think about a day when several small demands arrive together.

The room is hot. Someone is talking quickly. Your phone is vibrating. You’re trying to remember a question while also deciding how to answer it.

One more question may suddenly feel impossible.

Overload means there is more coming in, whether noise, light, people, questions, or feelings, than you can handle at once.

You might become irritable, find words harder to retrieve, lose concentration, or need to leave.

Some people experience shutdown, when their brain and body seem to pull inward and slow down or stop, often after overload. Speaking may become particularly difficult.

The way this looks from outside can be misleading.

A quiet person may not be angry. Someone who leaves a noisy room may not be rejecting their companion. Someone who cannot answer immediately may still be listening.

But these are possibilities, not conclusions.

You shouldn’t diagnose another person’s silence or decide what it means without asking them.

You also don’t have to expect a date to understand an experience you’ve never explained.

If conversation becomes difficult in busy places

You may enjoy meeting someone while finding the surroundings increasingly difficult to manage.

Consider explaining the situation before you reach overload: “I like talking with you, but the noise is making it hard to follow the conversation. Could we take a short break?”

If someone agrees, that gives you both a better opportunity to enjoy the interaction. If they can’t accommodate the change, you can decide whether to continue, suggest another time, or end the date respectfully.

If you're dating someone who needs time to answer

A pause isn’t necessarily uncertainty, dishonesty, or disinterest. Your partner may be organizing their thoughts, processing your question, or finding the right words.

You can ask, “Would you like some time to think?” Then give them the opportunity to answer.

Don’t assume every quiet moment has the same explanation, and don’t feel you must guess what your partner needs. Ask, listen, and allow them to tell you.

A misunderstanding doesn’t always have a villain

When communication fails, people often look for the person who did something wrong.

But two reasonable people can interpret the same interaction differently.

Consider Taylor and Sam.

Taylor prefers direct questions. When Sam says, “It’s getting late,” Taylor assumes Sam is commenting on the time.

Sam intended to suggest ending the evening.

Taylor continues the conversation. Sam becomes frustrated.

Later, Sam says, “I thought you would realize I wanted to go home.”

Taylor answers, “I didn’t know that’s what you meant.”

Neither person has to be dishonest or uncaring for that misunderstanding to occur.

Sam can learn to state a preference more directly. Taylor can learn to check whether a remark is also a request.

This is a small example of a broader idea called the double empathy problem: misunderstandings between people who experience and interpret the world differently can go both ways.

Research on this idea is still developing.

A 2020 experiment found that chains of autistic participants passed along information about as accurately as chains of non-autistic participants, while mixed groups lost more information. But a larger 2025 experiment did not find the same disadvantage for mixed groups. Both studies involved controlled communication tasks, not romantic relationships. (Crompton et al., 2020; Crompton et al., 2025)

The results don’t establish that autistic people always communicate better with one another, or that different neurotypes make poor partners.

What they do support is caution about assuming communication difficulties belong entirely to one person.

Understanding is something people build together.

This applies whether both partners are autistic, both are non-autistic, or their brains work differently.

Even two people with the same diagnosis may have very different preferences.

Imagine two autistic partners, Ana and Elise. Ana enjoys long conversations in lively public places. Elise prefers quiet settings and shorter conversations with breaks.

Sharing a diagnosis doesn’t erase those differences.

They still need to ask what works, consider each other’s preferences, and respect each other’s choices.

And if they disagree about physical affection, the solution isn’t to assume that either person’s sensory preferences matter more. Any touch must be something both freely agree to.

The names people give these differences

You may already have a name for some of your experiences.

You might know that you’re autistic, that you have ADHD, or that both descriptions apply to you.

You might recognize yourself in stories about those experiences without having been assessed.

You might have a history of difficult relationships or painful experiences that affect how comfortable you feel with other people.

Or you might have none of these explanations.

A label can be useful, but it’s not the beginning of your experience.

Neurodivergent is a broad term for people whose neurological development or functioning differs from what is considered typical. Neurotypical generally refers to people who do not have those identified neurological differences.

These aren’t two boxes that tell you everything about a person.

Among autistic people, some enjoy loud music and some can’t tolerate it. Some have little difficulty organizing a schedule and others need substantial support. Some love physical affection and others prefer limited contact.

The same variation exists among people with ADHD, people with other conditions, and people who have no diagnosis.

A diagnosis may help explain a pattern. It doesn’t determine your personality, your values, what kind of partner you want, or whom you can love.

You aren’t alone in discovering these things as an adult

A US study estimated that approximately 2.2% of adults were autistic in 2017, including potentially undiagnosed people. That estimate came from statistical modeling, not direct screening of every adult. (Dietz et al., 2020)

A separate US survey from 2023 found that approximately 6% of adults reported having a current ADHD diagnosis. About half of those adults said they had first been diagnosed at age eighteen or older. (Staley et al., 2024)

The figures measure different things and shouldn’t be treated as interchangeable.

But they illustrate something important: these experiences concern millions of adults, and an explanation may arrive well after childhood.

You might have spent years believing that certain difficulties were simply personal failures.

Perhaps you were repeatedly told to pay attention, speak more naturally, stop being sensitive, or try harder.

Looking at your experiences differently may help you distinguish what you can change through effort from what requires a different approach, useful support, or a more suitable environment.

That distinction isn’t an excuse to disregard other people.

It’s a way of making your efforts more effective.

More than one explanation can be true

People sometimes talk about autism, ADHD, and trauma as though they were three competing explanations for the same experience.

Life is not that tidy.

Autism and ADHD can occur together. A major systematic review estimated that approximately 28% of autistic people had co-occurring ADHD, although the studies differed greatly in who participated and how diagnoses were determined. Other publications have reported much higher estimates, partly because definitions and sampling methods differ. (Hours et al., 2022; Lai et al., 2019)

A person can also be autistic and have anxiety, depression, or another condition.

And having experienced trauma doesn’t prevent someone from also being autistic or having ADHD.

These possibilities matter because different explanations can produce similar-looking behavior.

Someone may avoid a crowded place because of the sensory environment, because it reminds them of a frightening experience, or for reasons unrelated to either.

Someone may find unexpected changes difficult because they need predictability, because uncertainty triggers fear, or because several experiences are interacting.

A professional consensus study examining autism, complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD), attachment disorders, and borderline personality disorder identified important overlaps in emotional and relationship difficulties. Clinicians emphasized looking at developmental history, when difficulties appeared, and the broader pattern rather than relying on a single behavior. That was expert agreement, not proof that any one feature can distinguish the conditions reliably. (Sarr et al., 2025)

This means a dating handbook cannot tell you which diagnosis fits by asking whether you dislike loud restaurants, get attached quickly, or struggle to interpret messages.

It would be irresponsible to pretend otherwise.

What you can do is begin noticing patterns.

Were certain experiences present throughout your life? Did they change after something significant happened? Are they difficult in every setting, or mostly in particular circumstances?

Those are useful questions to bring to a qualified professional if you want to investigate further.

Chapter 2 looks more closely at how childhood experiences and trauma may influence what feels familiar in relationships.

What if someone got the explanation wrong?

People aren’t always recognized accurately the first time they seek help.

In one Dutch study of 1,211 autistic adults, approximately one quarter reported having received an earlier psychiatric diagnosis that they now believed was mistaken. The proportion was higher among women than men in that sample. (Kentrou et al., 2024)

That does not mean researchers confirmed that one quarter had been medically misdiagnosed.

Participants were describing their perceptions. Some earlier conditions may have been real and co-occurring, rather than incorrect.

Still, the finding helps explain why an adult might have questions about an explanation that never seemed to account for their whole experience.

Recognition has also differed across demographic groups.

A systematic review of childhood autism studies found that estimates of the male-to-female ratio were lower when researchers actively screened populations than when they relied on existing diagnoses. This suggests that some girls meeting diagnostic criteria were being missed. The evidence concerned children and binary sex categories, not every adult gender identity. (Loomes et al., 2017)

Researchers have also studied masking, which means hiding or suppressing your natural traits to fit in.

One study of autistic adults found differences in reported masking-related behaviors by sex, gender identity, and diagnostic timing. But a later systematic review found inconsistent evidence about whether masking actually predicts a later diagnosis. (Khudiakova et al., 2026; McQuaid et al., 2022)

The practical message is not that any particular gender is naturally more difficult to diagnose.

It’s that familiar stereotypes can leave people unrecognized.

You don’t have to fit someone else’s image of an autistic person, a person with ADHD, or someone affected by trauma for your experiences to deserve attention.

Nor should you conclude that a previous diagnosis is wrong simply because another description feels familiar.

If a professional explanation seems incomplete, you can ask what evidence supports it, what else was considered, and whether further evaluation would be useful.

Do you need a diagnosis?

No diagnosis is required to notice your needs, communicate your preferences, or use this handbook.

If a loud restaurant makes conversation difficult, you can suggest somewhere quieter.

If you need extra time to answer personal questions, you can say so.

If written plans help you remember commitments, you can use them.

You don’t have to prove why something helps before you’re allowed to ask for it.

At the same time, self-identification and a professional diagnosis are not the same thing.

A 2026 US study compared adults who identified as autistic without a formal diagnosis with adults who had received one. The groups reported similar levels of autistic characteristics on a screening measure, but the self-identifying group described greater unmet support needs. Many wanted an assessment and reported barriers to obtaining one. The study did not determine whether the self-identifying participants would meet diagnostic criteria in a comprehensive evaluation. (Ahuvia et al., 2026)

A broader systematic review also documented barriers to adult autism diagnosis and disagreements about the role of self-identification. It did not establish how accurately people could diagnose themselves. (Khudiakova et al., 2026)

A professional assessment can help examine your developmental history, consider overlapping conditions, and identify treatment or support options where appropriate.

You might want that clarity. You might not be ready to pursue it. Access can also depend on cost, location, insurance, and available professionals.

Whatever your circumstances, you can begin by describing your experiences accurately.

Consider these three different statements:

  • Observation: “I often lose track of time when I’m concentrating.”
  • Possible explanation: “I wonder whether an attention-related difference contributes to that.”
  • Diagnosis: “A qualified professional assessed my history and concluded that I meet the criteria for a particular condition.”

Those statements answer different questions.

You can be certain about the first without being certain about the second or having pursued the third.

That is enough to begin making practical decisions.

Your needs matter, and so do theirs

Learning about your brain can make your experiences easier to understand.

It can also create a temptation to explain every difficult interaction through those experiences.

Suppose you find replying to messages difficult.

It’s useful to recognize that difficulty. You may need reminders, a different communication arrangement, or a partner who is comfortable with less frequent messaging.

But someone else may reasonably need more consistent contact to feel comfortable pursuing a relationship.

Neither person has to be wrong.

Similarly, if you strongly prefer advance plans and someone else enjoys spontaneity, you can discuss whether there is an arrangement that works for both of you.

If there isn’t, that may be a difference in compatibility, not evidence that someone is defective.

A request asks another person to do something, and they can say no. A boundary is a limit on what you will do or accept.

You might request quieter date locations. You might also decide not to attend events where you know you cannot comfortably participate.

Your date can agree, suggest something else, or decline.

You aren’t entitled to make another person meet every preference. They aren’t entitled to make you ignore your needs.

This distinction becomes particularly important when differences involve touch, intimacy, or personal space. Neither affection nor a diagnosis overrides consent. If someone doesn’t want contact, that decision stands.

And when a misunderstanding happens, an explanation can help repair it. An explanation isn’t a substitute for acknowledging hurt, following through on commitments, or respecting the other person’s decisions.

Being understood and being accountable can happen together.

Try this: make a map of your own experience

Before worrying about what a future partner might want, spend a few minutes noticing what helps you feel comfortable and connected.

You don’t need to complete a personality test. Just think about situations you’ve experienced, including friendships, family gatherings, work conversations, or dates.

If you’ve never dated, those other experiences are perfectly useful starting points.

Choose one recent interaction that felt easy and one that felt difficult.

For each, consider these questions:

  1. Surroundings: Where were you? Was it quiet, crowded, bright, unfamiliar, or comfortable? Did anything around you make conversation easier or harder?
  2. Attention and memory: Could you follow what was happening? Did you have enough time to think? Were there details or plans you struggled to keep track of?
  3. Communication: Did you understand what the other person meant? Did they seem to understand you? Was there anything you were assuming rather than knowing?
  4. Feelings: What did you notice during the interaction? Did your feelings become clearer later? Did anything make it difficult to respond in the moment?
  5. What helped: Was there something you or the other person did that made the interaction better?

Don’t use the answers to decide whether you’re suited for dating.

Look for one small thing you could try next time.

For example:

“I find it easier to get to know someone when there isn’t background music.”

“I like making plans at least a day ahead.”

“I sometimes need a minute to think before answering a personal question.”

“I can forget arrangements, so I put them in my calendar immediately.”

“I’d rather ask what someone means than guess.”

Each statement describes something specific.

None requires you to apologize for existing.

None requires another person to agree automatically.

And none needs a diagnosis attached.

If you’re already dating someone, you might invite them to do the exercise separately and compare answers. You may discover that a situation you found relaxing was tiring for them, or that you both had trouble interpreting the same conversation.

The point isn’t to find the person with the correct experience. It’s to make both experiences easier to understand.

You are more than the things that are difficult

It’s easy to make a list of everything that goes wrong.

Too much noise. Forgotten plans. Missed signals. Strong reactions. Awkward pauses.

But those aren’t the only things you’re bringing to a relationship.

You also bring your interests, curiosity, humor, preferences, knowledge, values, and ways of showing affection.

You may remember small details that matter to someone. You may enjoy discussing subjects other people rarely get to explore. You might be thoughtful about commitments, eager to learn, or particularly comfortable with quiet companionship.

You may also have strengths that have nothing to do with how your brain works.

Don’t turn your diagnosis, suspected diagnosis, or difficulties into your entire identity as a potential partner.

And don’t reduce someone you’re dating to theirs.

You are getting to know a person, not evaluating a collection of traits.

Understanding differences helps you ask better questions about what spending time together might actually feel like.

It won’t automatically tell you whether someone is kind, interested, available, or compatible with you.

Those things have to be learned through the relationship itself.

Start with what you know

You don’t need to arrive at your first date with a complete explanation of your brain.

You can start with something much simpler:

“This is easier for me.”

“That takes more effort.”

“I need a little time.”

“I’m not sure what you meant. Could you explain?”

“Would you prefer something different?”

Those statements give another person a chance to understand you. They also create room for that person to describe their own experience.

Sometimes the differences will be easy to accommodate. Sometimes they’ll require discussion. Sometimes you’ll discover that what works for one person doesn’t work for the other.

None of those outcomes requires either person to be wrong.

Dating doesn’t demand that two people experience the world identically. It asks whether they can get to know each other, respect each other’s choices, and find a way of being together that both genuinely want.

Your brain is part of what you bring to that possibility.

It’s worth getting to know.
