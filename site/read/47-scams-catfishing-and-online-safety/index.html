You have been talking to someone online for three weeks. They remember details about your life. They ask about your day. They seem interested in the things you care about, and you look forward to hearing from them.

Then they ask to borrow money.

You don’t want to be suspicious of someone who has been kind to you. Their explanation sounds serious. You wonder whether refusing would hurt their feelings, or whether someone who genuinely cared about them would help.

Here is something important: you do not have to decide whether someone is lying before deciding what you will and will not do.

You can care about someone and refuse to send money. You can enjoy a conversation and decline to share private information. You can hope a connection is genuine while acknowledging that you do not yet know the person well enough to trust them with certain parts of your life.

Online dating requires some trust. It does not require unlimited trust.

What a scam looks like from your side

Three terms are useful to understand.

Catfishing means presenting a false or misleading identity online. Someone might pretend to be another person or misrepresent important parts of who they are. Not every misleading profile is part of a financial scam, but a false identity is a reason to reconsider whether you want to continue communicating.

A romance scam is a form of fraud in which someone uses the appearance of romantic interest or a relationship to obtain money, valuable information, or other benefits dishonestly.

Sextortion means threatening to expose someone’s intimate images or other sexual material unless they meet a demand.

These are different problems, although they can overlap.

The FBI and Federal Trade Commission warn that romance scams can involve false online identities, financial requests, and pressure to disclose information or use unfamiliar payment or verification services (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Kansas City Field Office, 2026; Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

From your position, you might notice that an interaction suddenly involves something you never agreed to: sending money, sharing financial information, revealing private images, or proving your affection through an unwanted action.

You do not need to understand exactly how a scam operates to recognize that something is wrong.

And you do not need courtroom-level proof of dishonesty to stop participating in an interaction.

How common is this?

In a 2022 Pew Research Center survey, 52% of US adults who had ever used online dating said they had encountered someone they thought was trying to scam them. Another 38% reported receiving sexually explicit messages or images they had not requested (McClain & Gelles-Watnick, 2023).

Those numbers describe people’s reported experiences. They do not mean that 52% encountered a confirmed criminal scam.

The FTC’s 2026 analysis of fraud reports documented $298 million in reported 2025 losses from romance scams originating on social media. Among people who reported losing money to romance scams and provided the relevant contact information, nearly 60% identified social media as the starting point (Federal Trade Commission, 2026c).

These figures come from fraud reports, not a count of everyone using dating apps. Many experiences may never be reported, and reported losses are not the same as all losses that occurred.

The point is not that most people you meet online are dishonest.

It is that dishonesty is common enough to justify ordinary precautions, even when a conversation feels promising.

You can trust someone without trusting them with everything

Trust is not one decision you make at the beginning of a relationship.

You may trust someone to have an enjoyable conversation without trusting them with your home address.

You may trust them to respect a preference without trusting their investment advice.

You may believe they enjoy talking to you without agreeing to send money.

These are separate decisions.

Think of trust as something that becomes more specific as you learn about a person. The question is not simply, Do I trust them?

It is, What am I comfortable trusting them with, based on what I actually know?

A new connection can feel unusually meaningful when dating has been difficult or when someone seems to understand you in ways other people have not.

Those feelings are real. They are not proof that every claim the person makes is true.

The same distinction applies when someone appears especially compatible with your interests, values, or communication style. Shared interests are a reason to talk, not a reason to abandon your boundaries.

When someone’s words are difficult to question

Some people naturally take statements at face value. If someone says something clearly and confidently, you may assume they mean exactly what they say.

There is nothing wrong with preferring direct, honest communication.

The difficulty arises when you treat a person’s statement as verified information simply because they stated it sincerely.

Someone saying they are trustworthy is not evidence that they are trustworthy.

Someone saying they need money does not establish that you must provide it.

Someone saying they love you does not give them permission to disregard your boundaries.

A 2024 scoping review of eight studies involving autism and online dating found limited, mixed evidence concerning recognition of misleading profiles and deceptive behavior. One small study suggested difficulty recognizing deception, while another identified excessive suspicion of genuine profiles (Neumann et al., 2024).

That is not evidence that autistic people are inherently gullible. It also shows why trying to identify dishonesty through personality or intuition alone is not a reliable solution.

The practical alternative is to use consistent boundaries and independent checks.

If you tend to believe what people tell you

If someone gives you a detailed explanation, you might spend a lot of time deciding whether every part makes sense. You may also feel that accusing someone of dishonesty would be unfair unless you can identify a specific lie.

You do not have to accuse anyone. You can decide in advance that you will not send money, account information, or intimate images to someone you are only beginning to know. Those boundaries remain valid whether the other person’s story is true, false, or impossible to verify.

If you feel unsure, explain the situation to someone you trust without asking them to diagnose the stranger’s intentions. A second perspective can help you examine the decision itself.

Warning signs are reasons to slow down, not tools for diagnosing strangers

There is no single personality type that identifies a scammer.

A person can sound warm, awkward, articulate, nervous, affectionate, or unusually formal without any of those traits proving whether they are honest.

Likewise, someone can have legitimate reasons for protecting their privacy, struggling with video calls, or living far away.

Instead of trying to read someone’s character from a handful of clues, pay attention to what the interaction asks of you.

Be particularly cautious when an online acquaintance:

  • Requests money, gifts, investments, or financial assistance.
  • Wants access to your bank accounts, passwords, verification codes, or identity documents.
  • Sends unfamiliar links requiring financial information or account credentials.
  • Pressures you to keep financial requests secret from people you trust.
  • Responds to reasonable boundaries with guilt, threats, or repeated demands.
  • Claims to have established their identity while resisting reasonable, voluntary ways of resolving serious inconsistencies.

These are protective warning signs described in federal consumer and law-enforcement guidance, not a checklist that can reliably determine whether someone is committing fraud (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Kansas City Field Office, 2026; Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

A person who avoids video calls is not automatically dishonest.

A person who agrees to a video call is not automatically trustworthy.

The central question is whether the interaction remains respectful, voluntary, and compatible with your boundaries.

Checking whether someone is real

When you meet someone through friends or a shared activity, you often have some context for who they are.

A dating profile may provide almost none.

That does not mean you should investigate someone’s private life. It means you can make reasonable checks before becoming deeply involved.

Start with the information they have chosen to share

Read the profile carefully.

Does it provide a reasonably consistent account of the person’s interests and relationship intentions? Do important details change without explanation?

An inconsistency may be a misunderstanding, an outdated profile, or a sign that the information is unreliable.

You can ask a respectful question.

You do not have to demand an explanation or attempt to catch someone in a lie.

You can also decide that you lack enough reliable information to continue.

Check photographs without becoming a detective

The FTC recommends using an independent reverse-image search when a profile picture raises concerns (Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

A reverse-image search checks whether a picture appears elsewhere online. If a photograph appears under another identity, that gives you a reason to question the profile’s accuracy.

But an image search has limits.

A photograph that does not appear elsewhere has not been proven genuine. A photograph that appears in multiple places may have an innocent explanation, especially if the person has a public online presence.

Use what you find to inform your own decision, not to conduct an investigation into someone’s relatives, employer, home, or private activities.

What about verification badges?

Some dating platforms offer built-in identity or photograph verification.

These features can provide additional information, but they do not establish that someone will treat you honestly or respectfully.

Be especially cautious about unfamiliar external verification links.

The FBI has warned that fraudulent dating-related verification websites can request sensitive personal or payment information (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Kansas City Field Office, 2026).

You are not required to follow a link sent by a stranger, enter a credit-card number, or provide identification to continue a romantic conversation.

If a platform has a verification feature, examine it directly through the platform rather than through an unfamiliar message.

Does a video call prove anything?

A voluntary voice or video conversation may help you feel more comfortable communicating with someone.

It can also help you learn whether conversation feels natural.

But it is not a guarantee that someone is honest, financially safe, or genuinely interested in a relationship.

And people may have legitimate reasons for declining video, including privacy, accessibility, anxiety, sensory needs, or simply personal preference.

You cannot require someone to prove themselves in a particular way.

You can, however, decide that you are not comfortable continuing a connection without enough information.

The other person has a right to privacy. You have a right to caution.

Those rights can coexist.

Money and romance: keep the decisions separate

Consider Jean, 61, who has been talking online with someone for a month.

The conversations have been enjoyable. They share an interest in gardening, and Jean feels excited about dating again.

Then the person asks for help with an unexpected expense.

Jean wants to be generous. But Jean has a personal rule: no lending or transferring money to someone known only through an online connection.

That rule does not require Jean to prove that the request is dishonest.

It simply protects Jean’s finances while the relationship is still unfamiliar.

The FTC advises against sending money or gifts to online love interests you have not met in person (Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

Meeting in person, of course, does not automatically make a loan or financial request safe. Even established relationships can involve financial pressure.

What counts as a financial request?

Money requests do not always look like an ordinary loan.

They may involve paying an expense, purchasing gifts, participating in an investment, sharing financial information, or allowing another person to use your accounts.

The FBI and FTC specifically warn about romance-related requests involving money, investments, and sensitive financial information (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Kansas City Field Office, 2026; Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

You can use a simple boundary:

I don’t send money or make investments for people I’m getting to know online.

You don’t have to add a long defense.

A compatible person can feel disappointed and still respect that decision.

If someone treats your refusal as proof that you do not care about them, the pressure itself matters.

Kindness does not create a debt

Suppose someone has spent hours listening to you. They have remembered your birthday, praised your work, or helped you through a difficult evening.

You can appreciate their attention without owing them money.

You can care about their problems without becoming financially responsible for solving them.

You can also decline requests that would put your own financial stability at risk.

This is not a test of whether you are generous enough to deserve love.

A relationship should not require you to endanger your finances to keep the other person interested.

If you sometimes decide before you have time to think

When an urgent request arrives during an exciting conversation, you may find yourself ready to act before considering the consequences.

Make a rule for yourself before that situation happens: no financial decisions during an emotionally charged conversation. Stop, leave the conversation, and review the request later.

If necessary, speak with someone you trust before taking any action. This is a practical safeguard for anyone who makes quick decisions, not evidence that ADHD itself causes romance scam victimization.

When a conversation turns sexual

Sexual attraction and private image sharing are not inherently dishonest or abusive.

Adults can choose to exchange intimate messages or images when everyone involved freely agrees.

But consent is specific.

Someone agreeing to flirt does not mean they agree to send photographs.

Someone sending an intimate photograph does not mean they consent to having it shared with others.

Someone expressing interest does not mean they must continue a sexual conversation.

The consent principles from Part 4 apply online just as they do in person.

You never owe anyone an intimate image

No amount of affection, patience, or previous sexual conversation creates an obligation to share intimate material.

You can say:

I’m not comfortable sending intimate pictures.

Or simply:

No.

You do not need to explain your history, appearance, or reasons.

If the person continues pressuring you, you can end the conversation.

You also have a responsibility to respect the other person’s choices. If someone declines to send or receive sexual material, stop asking. Do not treat hesitation or silence as agreement.

What is sextortion?

Sextortion happens when someone threatens to expose intimate material unless demands are met.

The person making threats may claim to possess an intimate image, threaten to distribute one already shared, or use altered or fabricated material.

The existence of an image does not give anyone permission to threaten you with it.

Image-based abuse can also involve creating or distributing sexual images without consent, including images altered or generated using artificial intelligence. The FTC explicitly includes such material in its guidance about image-based abuse (Federal Trade Commission, 2026b).

If someone threatens to distribute intimate material, do not send more images, pay demands, or provide additional account access. Seek help and report the incident (Federal Trade Commission, 2026b, 2026a).

You do not need to negotiate with someone who is threatening you.

What if the image is fake?

A fabricated image can still cause distress or harm.

Someone may falsely claim that a sexual image depicts you. The fact that the image is artificial does not make a threat acceptable.

You do not have to prove that the image is real for the situation to deserve attention.

Federal guidance recognizes nonconsensual intimate images, including covered altered or AI-generated material, as a serious form of abuse (Federal Trade Commission, 2026b).

If a person has created or threatened to distribute an intimate image without consent, treat that as a safety problem, not as an ordinary disagreement about the relationship.

If an intimate image has been threatened or shared

You may feel frightened, angry, embarrassed, or unsure what to do.

You might also worry that reporting the situation will expose something private.

Those concerns deserve to be taken seriously.

A person who consented to a private image did not thereby consent to its distribution.

If an image was obtained dishonestly or created without permission, the person depicted does not become responsible for the abuse.

RAINN provides guidance on safely documenting and reporting technology-enabled sexual abuse (RAINN, 2026).

When safe, preserve relevant messages, account details, threats, and links before blocking or reporting the account. You do not need to send explicit material to friends to prove that something happened.

If an image involves someone who was under 18 when it was created, do not download, forward, or redistribute it to collect evidence. Follow guidance from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children instead (National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, n.d.).

Two different image-removal resources

These names are easy to confuse, so it helps to separate them.

StopNCII.org is a resource for adults seeking to prevent the spread of certain nonconsensual intimate images on participating services.

Take It Down, at takeitdown.ncmec.org, is a service from the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children for intimate images taken when someone was under 18. You can use it even if you are now an adult, provided the image was created before you turned 18.

These services use image-matching technology to help participating platforms identify and address material without requiring you to distribute the image through the reporting process (Federal Trade Commission, 2026b; National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, n.d.).

They cannot guarantee removal from every website or private channel.

There is also a separate federal process involving covered online platforms.

Under the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act’s covered-platform process, a platform receiving a valid removal request for covered nonconsensual intimate content must act within 48 hours. If the covered platform does not comply, a complaint about the platform can be made through TakeItDown.ftc.gov (Federal Trade Commission, 2026b).

This FTC complaint process is not the same as NCMEC’s Take It Down service, and filing a complaint does not guarantee that all copies of an image will disappear.

State laws also vary. Someone seeking legal remedies should obtain advice appropriate to their state and circumstances.

If something has already happened

Maybe you sent money before recognizing the warning signs.

Maybe you shared personal information.

Maybe someone is threatening you with an intimate image.

Perhaps you are still unsure whether the other person has committed fraud.

You can take protective action without waiting for certainty.

The most useful next step depends on what has happened.

A practical response checklist

  1. Protect your immediate safety. If you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services. Otherwise, stop responding to threatening or financially coercive requests. Do not send additional money, images, or sensitive information.

  2. Preserve relevant information safely. Keep transaction records, account names, messages, dates, and other details that may help a report. Do not forward or download intimate material involving minors.

  3. Contact the relevant financial service immediately if money was sent. Depending on the transaction, this may be your bank, card issuer, payment app, wire-transfer company, gift-card issuer, or cryptocurrency exchange. Explain what happened and ask whether a reversal, recall, dispute, or other remedy is available. Recovery is not guaranteed (Federal Trade Commission, 2026d).

  4. Secure exposed accounts. If you shared passwords or login information, change compromised credentials and enable multifactor authentication where possible. If sensitive identity information was exposed, visit IdentityTheft.gov for situation-specific guidance (Federal Trade Commission, 2026d).

  5. Report the account or incident. Use the dating app or social platform’s reporting tools. For suspected fraud, you can report to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center at IC3.gov. Local law enforcement may also take a report (Federal Bureau of Investigation, Kansas City Field Office, 2026; Federal Trade Commission, 2025).

  6. Seek appropriate support for intimate-image threats or distribution. RAINN provides survivor support and information at RAINN.org. For material involving someone under 18 when it was created, consult NCMEC’s Take It Down service or CyberTipline. For adult material, consult the FTC’s image-based abuse guidance and StopNCII.org (Federal Trade Commission, 2026b; National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, n.d.; RAINN, 2026).

You do not need to complete every item in exactly that order. The first priority is preventing further harm and contacting financial services promptly when money may still be recoverable.

Reporting does not guarantee repayment, prosecution, or removal of material everywhere.

It creates an opportunity for the appropriate service or authority to respond.

Be careful with people offering to fix everything

After an upsetting incident, an offer to recover money or remove images can sound reassuring.

But be cautious about anyone promising guaranteed results in exchange for payment.

The FTC has warned about deceptive paid services marketed to people facing sextortion and advises using trustworthy sources of assistance (Federal Trade Commission, 2026a).

An additional payment to an unfamiliar service can create another financial risk.

If you are unsure where to begin, start with the official resources rather than a person who contacts you unexpectedly promising a complete solution.

Telling someone can help

Romance fraud can cause emotional harm as well as financial loss.

A scoping review of 12 studies found that victims’ experiences could involve distress, betrayal, and shame. The research was heterogeneous and cannot establish how commonly any one response occurs among US victims (Coluccia et al., 2020).

A painful part of discovering possible deception is realizing that the connection may not have been what you believed.

You may miss the conversations even while understanding that continuing them would be unsafe.

You may feel angry at the other person, embarrassed by your own decisions, or reluctant to tell anyone.

Those reactions do not change your right to seek help.

A friend, family member, counselor, or victim-support professional can help you consider the facts and decide what to do next.

You do not have to share every intimate detail to ask for support.

You might say:

Something happened with someone I met online. I think they may have been dishonest with me, and I need help deciding what to do next.

You can ask the person to listen without judging you or making decisions for you.

If the first person responds poorly, you can choose someone else.

If pressure makes it hard to refuse

If saying no makes you afraid of losing someone

If you have experienced relationships where disagreement led to punishment, anger, or rejection, an urgent request may leave you focused on keeping the other person calm. You may know you do not want to agree but still find it difficult to say so.

You can make the decision outside the conversation. Put down your phone, allow the immediate pressure to pass, and talk with a trusted person before responding. You do not need to explain your past or argue about the request to maintain your boundary.

This is practical guidance for an experience that can have many causes, not a claim that trauma history predicts romance fraud.

You are allowed to stop even when you are uncertain

Suppose Maya, 43, has been messaging another woman on a dating app.

The conversation is enjoyable, but Maya notices that several important details in the profile no longer make sense.

She asks a respectful question. The answer does not resolve her concern.

Maya does not know whether the other person is lying, confused, protecting their privacy, or simply communicating unclearly.

She has a choice.

She can continue if she feels comfortable and wants to learn more.

She can also decide that she does not have enough confidence in the connection to continue.

She does not need to investigate the person’s life. She does not need to make an accusation. She does not need to obtain a confession.

A straightforward ending is enough:

I don’t think I’m comfortable continuing this conversation. I wish you well.

If someone has threatened, harassed, or attempted to defraud you, you do not owe them a closing message. You can preserve the information needed for reporting and use the platform’s blocking and reporting tools.

Chapter 44 covered accepting rejection. The same principle works here from the other direction: you are permitted to stop a romantic interaction.

The other person’s disappointment does not cancel that choice.

If you recognize yourself crossing the line

Online safety also means respecting the person on the other side of the conversation.

If you have been demanding money, pressuring someone to share images, accessing private information without permission, or threatening to expose someone’s material, stop.

Do not excuse the behavior because you felt rejected, frightened, jealous, or desperate for reassurance.

Do not continue contacting someone who has told you to stop. Do not distribute or threaten to distribute intimate material.

Take responsibility and seek appropriate professional or legal guidance about preventing further harm.

A relationship does not entitle anyone to another person’s money, privacy, body, images, or continued attention.

A simple safety plan before your next conversation

You don’t need to approach every profile expecting deception.

You can be open to people while deciding in advance what you will protect.

Before beginning another online conversation, consider these questions:

Money: Will you lend, transfer, or invest money on behalf of someone you’re only getting to know online?

Information: Which details about your identity, location, accounts, or daily life are you comfortable sharing?

Images: What kinds of photographs or messages do you want to keep private?

Verification: What information would help you feel comfortable continuing, and what will you do if it remains uncertain?

Support: Who could you ask for a second opinion if a request feels urgent or confusing?

You do not need complicated answers.

For example:

I won’t send money or share account access. I’ll take time before making important decisions. If something worries me, I’ll ask someone I trust before acting.

Your plan can change as you gain experience, but it should not depend on someone else’s approval.

A person who is right for you does not have to agree with every preference. They do need to respect your boundaries and your freedom to make decisions.

Safety should help you connect, not keep you afraid

It is possible to know about scams without treating every new person as a threat.

Most of the everyday skills involved are familiar: asking questions, noticing consistency, respecting boundaries, allowing trust to develop, and being willing to stop when something does not feel right.

The goal is neither blind trust nor constant suspicion.

You can enjoy someone else’s attention without immediately giving them access to your finances or private life. You can ask for clarity without interrogating them. You can make room for different communication styles without overlooking financial pressure or threats.

You can also recognize that a person may be genuine and still be incompatible with you.

The most useful question is not always Can I prove this person is trustworthy?

Sometimes it is simply Do I know enough to feel comfortable taking the next step?

If the answer is no, you can slow down, ask a reasonable question, or decline.

If the answer is yes, you can continue getting to know them while keeping your boundaries intact.

Online identity and information are only part of dating safety. Once you decide you might want to meet someone, another set of practical questions becomes important: where to meet, how to get there, how to stay comfortable, and how to leave if you need to.

That is where the next chapter begins.
