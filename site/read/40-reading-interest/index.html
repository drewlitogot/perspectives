Someone laughs at your jokes. They remember something you said last week. When you talk, they seem genuinely happy to see you.

Are they interested in you romantically?

Maybe.

That answer can be frustrating, especially when you’ve spent years trying to understand what other people mean without asking. You might have learned that eye contact means attraction, that someone who texts first must like you, or that a person who touches your arm is flirting. Perhaps you’ve missed interest before and don’t want to miss it again. Or you’ve mistaken friendliness for attraction and would rather not repeat the experience.

The problem is that none of those behaviors tells you exactly what another person feels.

Reading interest is not about learning a secret language that everyone else understands. It’s about noticing what someone actually does, recognizing what you cannot know, and deciding whether a question would help.

You do not need to become an expert at reading faces to date someone. You need enough information to make respectful choices.

A clue is not an answer

Imagine two people having coffee.

Alex keeps asking questions, laughs several times, and suggests visiting a bookstore nearby. Morgan notices these things and wonders whether Alex wants a date.

There are several possibilities. Alex might be attracted to Morgan. Alex might simply be enjoying the conversation. Alex might be curious about the bookstore. Alex might not have decided how they feel.

All four possibilities fit the same behavior.

This is why a useful distinction is between what you observe and what you interpret.

An observation describes something that happened:

  • “They asked whether I was free Saturday.”
  • “They smiled when I arrived.”
  • “They said they enjoyed talking with me.”
  • “They suggested getting coffee again.”

An interpretation gives that behavior a meaning:

  • “They must have a crush on me.”
  • “They are trying to flirt.”
  • “They definitely want to date me.”
  • “They are only being polite.”

The observations might be accurate. The interpretations could be right, wrong, or partly right.

You are allowed to make guesses. Everyone does. The mistake is treating your guess as something the other person has confirmed.

Notice what happened. Stay curious about what it means. Don’t turn a possibility into a certainty.

This matters in both directions. A person who doesn’t smile much might be interested. Someone who seems delighted by your company might not be.

You cannot reliably settle either question with body language alone.

How good are people at recognizing attraction?

You might imagine that people who date frequently develop a nearly automatic ability to recognize romantic interest.

The research offers a less reassuring picture.

In a 2009 study, researchers showed observers videos of speed dates and asked them to judge whether the people in the videos were romantically interested. Observers performed better than chance in some circumstances, particularly when judging men’s interest. But recognizing women’s interest was considerably harder, and people varied in how clearly their interest came across (Place et al., 2009).

A later Dutch study tested whether observers could recognize attraction from short, silent video clips of speed dates. Across three experiments, observers generally struggled to identify who wanted another date. Their performance was often around chance level (Samara et al., 2023).

The studies did not get identical results. Place and colleagues found some situations where observers could recognize interest better than guessing. Samara and colleagues found much less reliable detection.

That difference is worth keeping. The studies used different videos, tasks, and ways of measuring interest. Neither establishes that attraction is always invisible or always obvious.

Another study examined whether people could detect flirting during conversations and from videos. Participants were substantially better at recognizing when someone wasn’t flirting than when someone was (Hall et al., 2015).

Again, this wasn’t an experiment showing that everyone misses romantic interest. Flirting and romantic attraction are different things. The study tested particular conversations and observers, not every dating situation.

Taken together, these studies suggest something useful: social cues contain information, but people are much less accurate at interpreting that information than a simple list of dating signs would suggest.

There is another limitation. Much of this research involves young adults, heterosexual pairings, brief meetings, or observers watching strangers on video. Those findings cannot automatically tell you how accurately a 45-year-old recognizes interest from someone they’ve known for months, or how cues work between two non-binary people.

Your own situation may be different from the situations researchers studied.

That is another reason to avoid treating the results as a formula.

The familiar signs: useful, but not proof

You have probably encountered lists of signs that someone likes you. Eye contact. Leaning closer. Laughing. Remembering details. Finding reasons to talk. Starting conversations.

These behaviors can be worth noticing.

They do not come with a guaranteed meaning.

Eye contact

In one Austrian speed-dating study, researchers used eye-tracking equipment to measure where participants looked during five-minute conversations. Patterns of giving and receiving eye contact helped statistically predict whether someone wanted to meet a date again (Hoffmann et al., 2024).

This is evidence that eye contact can be associated with attraction in a particular dating situation.

It does not mean you can watch someone’s eyes and know what they want.

The study involved 60 mostly heterosexual young European participants in a structured setting. The equipment measured gaze more precisely than an ordinary observer could, and the findings were statistical patterns across interactions rather than certain predictions about individuals.

Someone looking into your eyes could be interested. Someone looking away could also be interested.

You do not need to count seconds of eye contact.

Smiling and laughing

A smile tells you that someone smiled.

It does not tell you whether they want a romantic relationship.

Laughter might accompany attraction, enjoyment, nervousness, friendliness, or politeness. You can appreciate a pleasant interaction without assigning it a romantic meaning.

If someone laughs at your jokes, enjoy the conversation. You do not have to decide what their laughter means.

Flirting back

This one can feel especially convincing.

You say something playful. The other person responds playfully. The conversation becomes warmer. You might think that because both people are participating, they must want the same thing.

But reciprocal behavior is not necessarily reciprocal attraction.

In a speed-dating study involving 382 singles, researchers found that flirting behavior was strongly reciprocal. People tended to respond to flirting with flirting. Yet their actual choices about wanting to see one another again were not similarly reciprocal (Back et al., 2011).

Two people could participate in the same enjoyable exchange without making the same romantic decision.

This doesn’t make either person dishonest.

It means an interaction can be mutual while the feelings, intentions, and desired next steps are different.

Flirting back is not a promise to date.

Neither is accepting a compliment, enjoying someone’s company, or having a long conversation.

And none of those behaviors establishes sexual consent.

Making time for you

Suppose someone frequently initiates conversations, remembers your plans, and suggests spending time together.

Those are observable choices. They tell you that the person is putting some effort into interacting with you.

That is useful information.

It still does not settle what kind of connection they want.

Instead of turning these behaviors into a score, consider them reasons you might reasonably wonder about romantic interest. If the question matters and the situation is appropriate, you can ask.

You don’t need to collect more clues until you’re convinced.

When behavior and intentions don’t match

Priya and Elena, both in their thirties, have been enjoying getting to know each other.

During their last conversation, Elena was animated, asked lots of questions, and said she had a wonderful time.

Priya feels encouraged.

Then Elena says she isn’t interested in dating.

Nothing about Elena’s earlier enjoyment has to be false. She may genuinely have had a wonderful time. Priya’s impression wasn’t necessarily foolish, either.

The mistake would be insisting that Elena’s warmth must mean more than her answer.

If someone tells you they don’t want to date, that is the information to act on.

The same principle works in the other direction.

Imagine that David seems reserved on a date with Marcus. He doesn’t laugh often, his answers are short, and he occasionally looks toward the window.

Marcus assumes David isn’t interested.

Later, David tells him that he’d enjoy meeting again.

Marcus can accept that answer without requiring David to behave more enthusiastically first.

What a person clearly communicates about their own interest deserves more weight than your preferred interpretation of their behavior.

This is especially important when someone does not express emotion in ways you’re accustomed to seeing.

Not everyone shows connection in the same way

Some people talk rapidly when they’re excited. Others become quieter. Some make frequent eye contact. Others listen while looking somewhere else.

These differences can create misunderstandings between people whose brains work differently.

A UK study examined conversations among autistic people, non-autistic people, and mixed pairs. The researchers looked at behaviors such as face-looking and brief acknowledgments during conversation.

They found that these behaviors related to rapport, meaning how connected and comfortable an interaction felt, differently across the groups. For example, fewer listening acknowledgments could coexist with better rapport in autistic–autistic conversations (Rifai et al., 2022).

This study was not about romantic interest. Participants were passing stories along, not going on dates.

It does, however, provide an important warning against assuming that conventional conversational cues mean the same thing in every interaction.

Consider two autistic people getting to know one another. One listens carefully without looking directly at the other’s face. The other speaks at length about something they both enjoy. They might feel connected even though their conversation doesn’t resemble the pattern someone else expects.

Or consider a mixed-neurotype pair. One person expects frequent eye contact to signal attention. The other finds looking away makes listening easier.

Both could be interested. Both could also misunderstand the interaction.

The double empathy problem, discussed earlier in the book, is relevant here: different ways of communicating can create misunderstandings in either direction.

If you tend to interpret eye contact, facial expressions, or conversational enthusiasm literally, keep an alternative possibility available:

This person may show engagement differently from what I expect.

That possibility is not proof of attraction, either.

The solution is not to replace one assumption with another. It’s to avoid deciding what someone feels solely from the way they look, speak, or move.

You also don’t have to disclose a diagnosis or ask someone else’s diagnosis to have a clear conversation.

And if you are the person who is hard to read, there is nothing wrong with describing your interest in words. The previous chapter explored how to do that.

If other people's signals arrive as noise

Tone, timing, eye contact and phrasing are supposed to combine into a reading. If they arrive as separate pieces that don’t resolve, you are not missing an obvious conclusion; the conclusion genuinely isn’t there for you.

Rather than reading harder, collect plainer evidence. Do they start conversations? Do plans get made and kept? Over a few weeks, behavior you can count tells you more than any single moment you could misread.

When it matters enough, ask. One awkward question costs far less than weeks of interpretation built on a guess.

Why people don’t always say what they mean

If direct communication can clear up uncertainty, why do people sometimes hint instead?

One possible explanation comes from research on indirect speech.

Pinker and colleagues proposed that people sometimes communicate indirectly because saying something outright could change a social relationship or create an awkward situation. Indirect language can leave a sensitive possibility unspoken, but it also leaves room for misunderstanding (Pinker et al., 2008).

This was a theoretical analysis, not a study proving that indirect romantic invitations work better.

Still, the idea helps explain why indirect communication isn’t necessarily a deliberate game.

Someone might want to express interest without making the interaction uncomfortable. Someone else might be unsure of their own feelings. A third person might simply have a habit of speaking indirectly.

You cannot reliably distinguish those possibilities by interpreting the hint more intensely.

For example:

“There’s a new restaurant I’ve wanted to try.”

That could be an invitation waiting to happen.

It could also be ordinary conversation about restaurants.

If you want to go together, you can express that interest. You don’t need to figure out whether the remark was secretly intended as an invitation.

Similarly:

“We should get together sometime.”

That sentence might express genuine enthusiasm, a general wish to stay connected, or a polite ending to a conversation.

Unless the person makes their intentions clearer, you do not have to assign it a definite romantic meaning.

Ambiguity is missing information, not an invitation to assume the answer you want.

The danger of looking for the answer you hope to find

Suppose you’ve developed a crush on someone.

You begin noticing how often they look at you, what they say in messages, and whether they seem happier around you than around other people.

If they smile, it feels encouraging. If they don’t reply, you wonder whether they’re shy, busy, or afraid to show their feelings.

You could spend hours fitting each new detail into a story.

This is a useful moment to separate three different things:

What you know: They smiled when you arrived.

What you suspect: They might be attracted to you.

What you want: You hope they’re attracted to you.

All three can exist at the same time.

The problem starts when wanting something makes your suspicion feel like knowledge.

You cannot remove that uncertainty by finding a more complicated interpretation of their behavior.

You can, however, notice when you’re no longer learning anything useful.

If you’ve reviewed the same conversations repeatedly and still don’t know whether someone is interested, you probably don’t need another hour of reviewing them.

You need either a respectful opportunity to ask or a decision to leave the question unresolved.

The same applies when you are convinced someone dislikes you.

A short reply, limited eye contact, or a quiet conversation does not prove romantic rejection. If their intentions matter, you can seek clarity without demanding reassurance.

For people who have experienced rejection, mistreatment, or difficult relationships, uncertainty may feel especially uncomfortable. You do not need to diagnose why you feel that way before choosing a respectful next step.

A strong feeling is real. It is not necessarily an accurate reading of another person’s feelings.

When someone seems reluctant

There is another reason to be careful about interpreting interest: people do not always find it easy to say no.

In a 2014 study, Joel and colleagues compared people’s willingness to reject unsuitable potential romantic partners in hypothetical situations with decisions participants believed were real.

Participants were less willing to reject someone when the situation seemed real. Concern about hurting the other person’s feelings helped explain that difference (Joel et al., 2014).

This does not establish that every hesitant answer is secretly a rejection.

It also doesn’t mean you can reliably recognize a reluctant person’s true feelings by analyzing their voice or expression.

What it does show is that rejecting someone can involve social pressure and concern for their feelings.

Imagine asking someone whether they’d like to go on a date.

They respond:

“Um … maybe. I’ll have to see.”

You don’t know whether they’re interested, undecided, uncomfortable, or genuinely checking their schedule.

What you do know is that they haven’t clearly agreed.

You can say:

“That’s okay. No need to decide now.”

Then leave the decision with them.

Don’t start explaining why the date would be enjoyable, asking whether they’re sure, or trying to resolve every possible obstacle. A person shouldn’t have to defend their uncertainty to you.

A hesitant answer might eventually become a clear yes. It might also remain uncertain.

You can accept either outcome without pursuing the question.

There is a difference between someone freely choosing to date you and someone agreeing because declining feels difficult.

Your job isn’t to identify which hidden feeling they have. Your job is to avoid adding pressure and to act only on what they’ve clearly chosen.

If you're dating someone who will not guess

Someone who asks you outright whether you’re interested is not being blunt or skipping a step. They may have learned that guessing produces expensive mistakes.

Answer the question as asked. A clear “yes, I like you and I’d like to keep seeing you” is worth more than a warm but ambiguous reply, which will land as a no or as nothing.

If you’re not sure yet, say that instead. “I’m enjoying this and I don’t know yet” is a real answer and much easier to hold than silence.

When to stop interpreting and ask

You don’t have to ask every time you notice a possible sign of interest.

Sometimes you’re simply enjoying a conversation and don’t need to decide where it’s going.

Sometimes you’d rather get to know the person better before considering romance.

Sometimes the circumstances make it inappropriate to raise the question. The chapter on when and where to make a move will cover those decisions more fully.

But when you actually want an answer, asking can be more useful than collecting clues indefinitely.

A simple question can communicate three things:

  1. You’re interested in finding out whether dating is possible.
  2. You’re willing to hear the person’s actual preference.
  3. You aren’t treating their earlier behavior as a commitment.

For example:

“I’ve enjoyed getting to know you. Would you be interested in going on a date?”

Or:

“I’d be interested in a date with you, but I’m not sure if you see things that way. Is that something you’d want?”

Or, if you need to clarify an earlier conversation:

“When you mentioned getting dinner together, were you thinking of a date or something else?”

These are examples, not mandatory scripts. The best wording is something you can say naturally and the other person can answer freely.

Avoid turning the conversation into an investigation.

You don’t need a long introduction about everything you’ve noticed. You don’t need to explain that they smiled three times, initiated two conversations, and looked at you across the room.

Their behavior doesn’t need to be entered as evidence.

You can simply describe your interest and ask about theirs.

Is direct asking actually welcome?

There is an important research gap here.

The sources reviewed for this chapter do not include a study establishing whether people generally prefer being asked directly about ordinary romantic dates rather than approached through hints.

The closest evidence involves sexual consent, which is a different decision.

In a study involving autistic and non-autistic undergraduate students in the northeastern United States, many participants expressed comfort with explicit verbal sexual consent. Some believed this preference was uncommon, even though the study found substantial support for it (McKenney et al., 2026).

That is useful evidence against assuming that explicit conversation is always unwanted or awkward.

It does not prove that everyone enjoys direct date invitations, or that a particular question will be welcomed.

The recommendation to ask plainly is therefore a practical one, grounded in clarity and respect, not a promise that it will improve your romantic chances.

Direct communication can still feel uncomfortable.

You can be direct without expecting an immediate answer. You can give someone room to decline. And you can decide not to ask when the circumstances don’t allow that freedom.

The point is understanding, not obtaining agreement.

What to do with the answer

Asking is only half of the skill. The other half is accepting the response.

If the answer is yes

Great. You have clearer information about what the person wants.

You still don’t know everything about their feelings, their expectations, or how the connection will develop.

A yes to one date means agreement to that date. It isn’t a promise of a relationship, exclusive commitment, physical affection, or sex.

You can enjoy the opportunity without turning it into a larger commitment than either person has made.

If the answer is no

Believe them.

You do not need to determine whether they’re secretly interested, afraid, confused, or waiting for you to be more convincing.

Their answer settles whether you should pursue that invitation.

“Thanks for being clear” is enough.

The chapter on rejection will cover how to deal with the feelings that can follow.

If the answer is uncertain

Treat it as uncertainty.

“Maybe,” “I don’t know,” “I’m not ready,” and an unanswered invitation are not clear agreements.

You can acknowledge the answer and stop asking.

You don’t have to wait indefinitely or keep your plans open. You can move on with your own life.

If the other person later chooses to express interest clearly, that creates a new situation. You don’t need to arrange for that to happen.

If their words and behavior seem to disagree

Give priority to the boundary or refusal.

If someone says they don’t want a date but continues to be friendly, friendliness doesn’t cancel the no.

If someone says yes but then seems uncomfortable, you can check whether they still want to continue without pressuring them. If they do not clearly want to continue, stop.

You do not need to establish what they really meant before respecting their comfort.

Interest is not consent

This distinction is important enough to state separately.

Someone can be attracted to you without wanting to date you.

Someone can want a date without wanting a relationship.

Someone can want a relationship without wanting any particular kind of physical contact.

And someone can change what they want.

RAINN’s sexual-consent guidance explains that flirting, silence, a lack of resistance, and past sexual encounters do not establish consent to sexual activity. Hesitation, withdrawal, or indications of discomfort require stopping rather than assuming permission (RAINN, 2026).

This chapter is about reading romantic interest, not deciding sexual consent. The earlier chapters covered consent and physical boundaries in detail.

But the distinction carries across situations:

A possible sign of attraction is not permission to take the next step.

Even a clear expression of romantic interest only tells you what the person has actually expressed.

Don’t use eye contact, laughter, a previous date, or the fact that someone seemed enthusiastic yesterday as permission for something they haven’t agreed to today.

Reading interest may help you decide whether to ask a question. It cannot replace the person’s answer.

A small practice: separating facts from guesses

Think of an interaction where you weren’t sure what someone meant. It could be romantic, friendly, or completely ordinary.

Write down three sentences.

1. What happened?

Use only details another observer could reasonably describe.

“Jordan asked me what I was doing this weekend and suggested meeting for lunch.”

2. What meaning did you give it?

“I thought Jordan might want to date me.”

3. What else could it mean?

“Jordan might want a friendly lunch. Jordan might be interested romantically. Jordan might not have decided.”

Then ask yourself:

Do I actually need to know right now?

If not, you can leave the question unanswered and continue interacting normally.

If yes, consider whether there’s a comfortable opportunity to ask one clear question.

This exercise isn’t meant to remove every feeling or possibility. It’s meant to keep observation, interpretation, and desire from becoming the same thing.

You can practice with an encouraging interaction or one you interpreted negatively.

You might discover that you often jump toward hope. You might discover that you usually assume disinterest. You might do both, depending on the person.

Neither pattern means you’re incapable of understanding people.

It gives you something specific to notice before you act.

You don’t have to become a mind reader

There will be times when someone likes you and you don’t notice. There will be times when you think someone likes you and they don’t. There will be times when both of you are interested but express it differently.

Those possibilities are part of dating.

The research doesn’t offer a dependable formula for detecting attraction, and it doesn’t establish that people with a particular gender or neurotype can be understood through a special list of signs.

Some studies have found average differences between men and women in recognizing romantic or sexual interest. Others have found different patterns depending on whether researchers measured flirting, attraction, or a wish for another date (Hall et al., 2015; Perilloux et al., 2012; Place et al., 2009).

Those findings aren’t instructions for interpreting a particular person. Much of the research used binary-gender, heterosexual study designs and didn’t establish comparable results for people of other genders or orientations.

Ask the person in front of you, not a stereotype about their gender.

You can also remember the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of the book. Two people may be looking at the same interaction through different experiences and expectations. Describing what you see and asking what they see gives you a way to compare those views.

You are responsible for your own actions, not for perfectly decoding someone else’s inner life.

When interest is possible, you can remain open to it.

When interest is unclear, you can ask or wait.

When someone declines, you can respect their answer.

And when someone clearly wants the same thing you do, you can enjoy finding out what comes next without pretending you knew the answer all along.

One particularly confusing situation remains: when you enjoy a close connection but don’t know whether the other person sees it as friendship or romance. That’s where the next chapter begins.
