You meet someone you like. The conversation goes well. You want to ask them on a date.

But they’re at work. Or you’re classmates. Or they’re about to leave. Or you’ve only spoken twice, and you’re not sure whether asking would make things uncomfortable.

The question isn’t just whether you’re allowed to ask someone out. It’s whether the other person can comfortably say no.

Consider two situations.

At a Saturday board-game gathering, Lena and Priya spend an evening playing together, talking between games, and discovering that they share an interest in old science-fiction movies. As people begin packing up, Lena asks whether Priya would like to get coffee sometime, as a date.

Priya can accept, decline, or say she needs to think. Either way, she can go home. Lena has no authority over her. Both have a choice about what happens next.

Now imagine Lena makes the same request to a barista who has served her coffee several mornings that week. The barista smiles, remembers her order, and asks how her day is going.

But the barista is working. Being pleasant is part of the job. Leaving the conversation isn’t necessarily an option. Even a polite invitation can put the employee in an uncomfortable position.

The words might be identical. The situation isn’t.

A respectful move is one that gives the other person a genuine choice, including the choice not to participate.

What does “making a move” actually mean?

In dating, making a move means doing something that communicates romantic interest or invites the relationship to become romantic.

That can mean asking someone on a date, telling them you’re interested, or asking whether they’d like to explore something beyond friendship.

For this chapter, the focus is making one clear romantic invitation.

It doesn’t mean surprising someone with a kiss, moving closer until they have to pull away, or using physical touch to find out whether they’re interested.

Those actions involve another person’s physical boundaries. As earlier chapters explained, an invitation to spend time together and permission to touch someone are different things.

You don’t need to create a dramatic moment. You don’t need to make someone guess your intentions. And you don’t need to prove your confidence by acting before you’re sure the situation is appropriate.

A simple question can do the job.

The harder skill is recognizing when asking that question is fair to both people.

Choose a place where no is a real option

Before asking someone out, think about the situation from their position.

Imagine that you have no interest in dating the person who’s approaching you. You might enjoy talking to them. You might want to remain friends. Or you might simply want to finish what you’re doing.

How easy would it be to decline?

Consider four questions.

  1. Can they say no without losing something important? Their job, grade, housing, professional opportunities, access to care, or other necessities shouldn’t depend on your approval.
  2. Can they leave the conversation? They shouldn’t be trapped in a vehicle, blocked in a doorway, isolated in a room, or unable to get away from your attention.
  3. Can they return to this place comfortably afterward? If you share a workplace, class, club, or friendship group, will you continue treating them normally if they decline?
  4. Will they understand what you’re asking? They should know whether you’re suggesting a date or an ordinary social activity, rather than having to discover your intentions after agreeing.

These aren’t scientifically established rules for successful approaches. They’re practical questions for deciding whether an invitation respects someone’s freedom.

Notice that the third question is about what you will do, not what you can persuade the other person to believe.

You can’t guarantee that nobody will ever feel awkward. But you can decide not to punish someone for declining, turn a shared activity into repeated attempts at romance, or make them responsible for protecting your feelings.

A public place doesn’t automatically make an invitation appropriate, either. Someone alone on public transportation may have nowhere to go until their stop. A person wearing headphones and reading in a waiting room may be trying to have time to themselves.

Being physically near someone isn’t an invitation to interrupt them.

A setting that makes sense

At a local photography gathering, Marcus, 46, talks with Daniel, 43, about their cameras and favorite hiking trails. They’ve had several conversations over a few weeks.

One evening, after the group finishes, Marcus says:

“Daniel, I’ve enjoyed talking with you. Would you be interested in getting dinner together sometime, as a date?”

Daniel says he’s flattered but isn’t interested romantically.

Marcus accepts the answer. When they see each other at the next gathering, he treats Daniel as a fellow photography enthusiast rather than someone who owes him another chance.

The invitation didn’t create an obligation.

Of course, asking someone from a group you both enjoy still carries some possibility of awkwardness. That’s worth considering. Neither person should have to give up a valued community because the romantic interest wasn’t mutual.

The aim isn’t to find a place where rejection can’t happen. It’s to choose circumstances where rejection can happen safely.

Work is a place to be especially careful

People meet romantic partners through work. But sharing a workplace changes what an invitation can mean.

A coworker may have to see you every day. You might share meetings, projects, shifts, or professional relationships. Refusing an invitation doesn’t necessarily end your contact.

A 2026 study interviewed 87 workers in the Silicon Valley technology industry about romantic advances in the workplace. Participants disagreed about what was appropriate. Some believed people should avoid making advances at work. Others favored indirect approaches or a clear statement of interest. Workers also raised concerns about professional and reputational consequences (Hart, 2026).

The study doesn’t establish one correct way to approach a coworker. It shows why the issue is complicated.

An invitation that seems harmless to the person asking can carry very different consequences for the person receiving it.

When you have authority over someone

Suppose you’re a manager who would like to date an employee you supervise.

You might believe you’re offering a genuine choice. You might even be willing to accept a refusal gracefully.

But you have influence over that person’s work. They may worry about their schedule, evaluations, assignments, promotion opportunities, or future treatment.

Your intention to be fair doesn’t erase the power difference.

The same concern applies when one person grades another’s work, decides whether they receive a recommendation, or controls access to an important opportunity.

A professor asking out a student they teach is not in the same situation as two students taking the same class.

For someone with authority, the responsible choice is not to make a romantic approach to a person whose opportunities depend on them. Follow the institution’s professional boundaries and relationship policies.

For example, Cornell University’s relationship policy prohibits or restricts several kinds of romantic relationships involving academic or supervisory authority, including relationships the participants might otherwise describe as consensual (Cornell University, 2025).

Cornell’s policy is one university’s rule, not a law governing every American college. Universities, employers, and professional organizations have different requirements.

In US workplaces, federal law also addresses sexual harassment. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission explains that unwelcome sexual advances can amount to unlawful harassment under particular circumstances, including when employment decisions depend on them or when conduct is sufficiently severe or pervasive. Harassment can involve supervisors, coworkers, customers, or clients (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, n.d.).

That doesn’t mean every isolated romantic invitation is illegal. It means workplace conduct has consequences beyond ordinary dating etiquette.

Check the rules that apply to your workplace or institution. State laws and professional requirements may also differ. If you’re uncertain about a particular situation, seek appropriate workplace or legal guidance rather than assuming a relationship is permitted.

What about a coworker who’s your equal?

Imagine two colleagues who work in different departments. Neither supervises the other, and neither controls the other’s opportunities. Over time, they get to know each other outside their immediate work duties.

A romantic relationship may be possible, depending on workplace rules and the circumstances.

But being at the same level doesn’t automatically make an invitation welcome.

Before considering it, ask whether there has been genuine personal conversation beyond work tasks, whether the other person can decline without workplace consequences, and whether you can preserve the professional relationship afterward.

Don’t use a work meeting as an excuse to introduce romantic pressure. Don’t turn professional contact information into a way to pursue someone privately. And don’t continue looking for an opening when someone has already shown discomfort or declined.

Sometimes the best decision is to appreciate a coworker without asking them out.

Attraction doesn’t create an obligation to act.

When someone is paid to be friendly

A server remembers your favorite meal. A receptionist smiles whenever you arrive. A bartender asks about your weekend.

These can feel like personal moments, especially if you find ordinary social situations difficult and feel relieved when someone seems comfortable talking with you.

But the interaction has a purpose that isn’t dating.

Customer-facing employees are often expected to be friendly even when they’re tired, busy, or not personally interested in a conversation.

A study combining a workplace survey and an experiment found that dependence on tips and expectations of friendliness could contribute to workers’ vulnerability to customer sexual harassment (Kundro et al., 2022).

Another research project examined two samples of US women in customer-facing jobs who had experienced customer harassment. It associated those experiences with unfavorable work and well-being outcomes (Morganson, 2022).

These studies examine harassment, not whether every individual request for a date is harmful. They do, however, give good reason to take the worker’s position seriously.

The safest general rule is not to treat someone’s professional friendliness as a romantic opportunity.

Don’t ask out an employee while they’re serving you, especially when declining might affect their ability to do their job comfortably. Don’t use customer records, employee contact details, or information from workplace systems to seek personal contact.

And don’t arrange repeated visits or encounters in an attempt to turn a working relationship into a romantic one.

If someone independently expresses personal romantic interest outside the customer relationship, that is a different situation. But don’t assume this is happening merely because the conversations have become familiar.

Perspective: Women

If you receive unwanted attention while working

Two studies in the research reviewed for this chapter examined women in customer-facing jobs who experienced sexual harassment. They support taking unwanted customer attention seriously, but they don’t establish that only women encounter it (Morganson, 2022).

If you’re working and someone makes an unwanted romantic approach, you don’t owe them a personal explanation or a friendly performance to protect their feelings. Your workplace should not expect you to tolerate harassment as part of serving customers. The same principle applies to workers of every gender.

Classes, clubs, and other places you keep returning to

Some settings are neither entirely professional nor entirely social.

You might attend a college class, volunteer at a museum, participate in a hobby group, belong to a sports club, or take lessons with the same people every week.

Meeting someone through a shared interest can give you a natural subject to talk about. But it also means your choices affect a place that matters to both of you.

Classmates

Rosa, 29, and Jamie, 32, are taking an evening art class. They sometimes talk while cleaning up their supplies and have enjoyed conversations about exhibitions around town.

Neither has authority over the other.

Rosa could reasonably consider asking Jamie on a date, provided Jamie has room to decline and there’s no indication that the attention is unwanted.

Now change one detail: Rosa is Jamie’s instructor and will evaluate the work that determines whether Jamie passes.

That changes the situation.

The relevant question is not whether Rosa believes she would grade fairly. It’s whether Jamie can freely refuse someone who has authority over an important part of their life.

Professional boundaries apply even when attraction seems mutual.

Shared activities

Imagine you’ve become interested in someone from your weekly hiking group.

You may be tempted to interpret every friendly conversation as encouragement. But as Chapter 41 explained, friendship and romantic interest can look similar. People join groups to enjoy the activity and its community, not necessarily to find a partner.

If you have a genuine connection and the circumstances allow an easy refusal, one invitation may be reasonable.

If they decline, the hiking group is still their hiking group.

Don’t make your continued participation conditional on getting another chance. Don’t ask other members to intervene or investigate the person’s feelings for you. Don’t turn group events into opportunities to revisit the invitation.

A shared community should remain available to both people.

When is the right time to ask?

There isn’t a universal amount of time you must talk to someone before asking them on a date.

Not three conversations. Not ten minutes. Not after someone laughs at your joke.

What matters is whether you’ve had a genuine interaction and whether the other person has a reasonable opportunity to decide what they want.

One study examined questions in live conversations, including a speed-dating setting, and found that asking more follow-up questions was associated with greater liking (Huang et al., 2017).

That doesn’t prove that asking questions creates romantic interest, or that conversation should follow a special formula.

The practical lesson is simpler: have an actual conversation with the person rather than treating the conversation as a hurdle before asking for a date.

Listen to what they say. Give them opportunities to talk. Notice whether you’re both participating.

You may learn that you have something in common. You may discover that you don’t. Both are useful outcomes.

Signs that this isn’t the moment

Consider waiting or leaving the person alone when they’re:

  • Working, studying intensely, or handling an important task.
  • Trying to leave, rushing somewhere, or ending the conversation.
  • Visibly uncomfortable, distracted, distressed, or overloaded.
  • Unable to step away because of their surroundings.
  • Responding only because the situation requires politeness.

These are not clues to overcome with a better approach. They’re reasons to respect the situation.

You don’t need to know exactly why someone seems unavailable. They may be exhausted, uninterested, anxious, or simply focused on something else.

The reason doesn’t change your responsibility.

Don’t wait for a perfect romantic moment

The opposite mistake is believing you need unmistakable chemistry before you can ask.

You might spend months trying to determine whether someone is interested without ever saying what you mean.

But no amount of analyzing smiles, laughter, or eye contact can tell you everything.

In two surveys of young adults in opposite-sex friendships, participants sometimes projected their own sexual or romantic interest into what they believed their friends felt (Koenig et al., 2007).

That research doesn’t cover every kind of friendship or romantic relationship. It illustrates one reason guessing can be unreliable.

If the setting is appropriate and there’s been a genuine personal connection, you can ask clearly without pretending you already know the answer.

The purpose of asking isn’t to confirm a decision you’ve made for both people.

It’s to find out whether you’re interested in the same thing.

What one respectful invitation sounds like

A good invitation doesn’t require an elaborate speech.

It should communicate three things: your interest, what you’re proposing, and that the other person has a choice.

For example:

“I’ve really enjoyed talking with you. Would you be interested in getting coffee sometime, as a date?”

Or:

“I’d like to get to know you romantically. Would you be interested in going out together?”

Or, with someone you already know:

“I value our friendship, and I’ve realized I’m interested in you as more than a friend. Would you be open to a date? It’s completely okay if you’d rather stay friends.”

None of these sentences is guaranteed to produce a good response.

That’s not their purpose.

They make the invitation understandable without requiring the other person to interpret hidden intentions.

Notice that the invitation identifies a date.

“Do you want to hang out sometime?” can be a perfectly good invitation to spend time together. But if what you really mean is a romantic date, leaving that unsaid may create confusion.

You don’t need to use formal language. You do need to be honest about what you’re inviting someone to do.

And saying “no pressure” doesn’t automatically remove pressure. Your behavior and the situation must make that promise real.

One clear ask, not an argument

After asking, give the other person room to answer.

Don’t continue explaining why the two of you would be compatible. Don’t offer a list of reasons they should consider dating you.

If they say yes clearly and willingly, you can discuss a plan.

If they say no, accept it.

If they hesitate, avoid answering, seem uncomfortable, or don’t reply, don’t interpret that as permission to keep asking.

A useful response to uncertainty is:

“That’s okay. You don’t have to decide right now.”

Then leave the decision with them. Don’t set a deadline or keep checking for an answer. If they want to revisit the invitation, they can choose to do so.

You have already communicated your interest.

There’s nothing to be gained by trying to extract a response.

Why a yes doesn’t always mean what you think

It’s tempting to assume that if someone agrees to a date, they must have wanted one.

Often they do.

But agreeing to something and feeling entirely free to decline aren’t always the same.

In two studies, researchers found that single people who considered a potential date in a more realistic situation were less willing to reject someone who didn’t meet their preferences than participants imagining a hypothetical situation. Concern about hurting another person’s feelings partly explained the difference (Joel et al., 2014).

That doesn’t mean a positive answer should automatically be doubted. It means people sometimes consider the other person’s feelings when deciding whether to accept.

This is another reason to make declining easy.

Don’t demand an immediate response. Don’t make a public spectacle of the invitation. Don’t use other people’s presence to put someone on the spot.

And don’t assume a polite or hesitant response is an enthusiastic yes.

A date isn’t permission for anything else

Suppose someone accepts your invitation to dinner.

They’ve agreed to dinner.

They haven’t agreed to kissing, holding hands, sex, a relationship, or another date.

RAINN’s sexual-consent guidance explains that consent must be freely given, specific, and ongoing. Silence, freezing, earlier flirting, or previous consent do not create permission for sexual activity (RAINN, 2026).

The same commitment to respecting a person’s choices should guide romantic invitations, even though asking someone to dinner isn’t the same as seeking sexual consent.

If you want something new, don’t treat an earlier yes as a blank check.

When someone doesn’t answer directly

A refusal isn’t always spoken as a clear “no.”

Someone might say they’re busy, change the subject, stop responding, or avoid making plans.

You don’t need to decide whether these responses mean disinterest, discomfort, uncertainty, or something else before respecting them.

Without a clear yes, you don’t have a date.

Sometimes a person chooses an indirect refusal because they are worried about the other person’s reaction.

A 2022 study found that safety concerns could influence people’s willingness to use indirect romantic rejection, including ghosting. The findings about the rejected person’s gender were less consistent (Freedman et al., 2022).

That doesn’t establish why any particular person has stopped replying. Silence can have many explanations.

But it does show why demanding a direct rejection isn’t always fair.

Someone who doesn’t answer isn’t obligated to provide a satisfying explanation.

If you’re the person who made the invitation, your responsibility is to stop pursuing an answer.

If you’re at work or in a shared activity, continue behaving respectfully without treating the refusal as a problem to solve.

If you recognize yourself repeatedly returning to the same person, looking for chances to ask again, or feeling entitled to an answer, stop the pursuit. Chapter 32 explains why unwanted persistence can become harassment and where to seek help changing that behavior.

Chapter 44 will address the emotional side of rejection, including what to do when a refusal hurts or leaves a friendship feeling awkward.

For now, the behavioral rule is enough: one invitation, then respect the answer or the absence of one.

When people communicate differently

Not everybody expresses interest in the same way.

One person might be very direct. Another might speak cautiously or need time to understand an unexpected question. Someone might enjoy talking but find it difficult to tell whether a conversation is becoming romantic.

None of those patterns alone tells you what the person wants.

Research on autistic and non-autistic social interaction also cautions against treating one communication style as universally correct.

A small 2020 study found that information was transferred similarly through autistic-only and non-autistic-only groups but less effectively through mixed groups. However, a much larger 2025 registered replication did not find the same mixed-group disadvantage, although some differences in rapport remained (Crompton et al., 2020; Crompton et al., 2025).

A separate study of short conversations between autistic and non-autistic adults found that some social experiences varied according to the pairing (Morrison et al., 2020).

These studies weren’t about romantic invitations. They don’t establish that autistic people need a special approach, that neurotypical people always understand hints, or that mixed-neurotype relationships are inherently harder.

What they reinforce is the importance of not assuming that your interpretation is the only possible one.

A person who needs literal wording may appreciate knowing that you mean a date.

A person who needs processing time may appreciate not having to answer immediately.

A person who becomes overloaded by unexpected attention may appreciate a brief question rather than an intense emotional declaration.

These are practical considerations, not rules tied to a diagnosis.

If someone needs time to answer a personal question

An unexpected romantic invitation may take someone a moment to process, especially if they prefer direct communication or find surprise conversations difficult.

Ask clearly, then allow silence without filling it with additional arguments or questions. If the person seems unable or unwilling to respond, end the interaction respectfully. Don’t treat quietness, delayed processing, or shutdown as agreement.

They may choose to respond later, or they may not. You can respect either outcome without deciding what their behavior means based on a diagnostic label.

If your own feelings make it difficult to choose the moment

You might experience attraction so intensely that you feel you must act immediately.

Or you might be so worried about getting the timing wrong that you keep rehearsing conversations without making a decision.

Neither feeling determines whether the situation is appropriate.

You can acknowledge your excitement or anxiety without making it the other person’s responsibility.

If you’re unsure, give yourself time to consider the four questions about freedom and choice. You don’t have to act on every feeling as soon as it appears.

And if someone else’s reaction is difficult to read, remember the double empathy problem: people can misunderstand each other in both directions.

The answer isn’t to guess more aggressively.

It’s to communicate clearly when the setting allows it, and to respect uncertainty when it doesn’t.

When not asking is the right decision

Imagine you’ve developed a crush on the person who checks you in at a medical office.

You’ve exchanged friendly comments for months. They remember your name, and you look forward to seeing them.

You may genuinely feel a connection.

But their friendliness occurs while they’re carrying out a professional responsibility. They’re not in the same position as someone voluntarily socializing with you at a party.

You can enjoy those conversations without turning them into an invitation.

Or imagine you’re a graduate teaching assistant who finds one of the students in your section attractive.

You might wonder whether waiting until the semester ends would make the situation acceptable. But depending on the institution’s rules, future evaluative authority, ongoing academic connections, or other professional obligations could still matter.

The appropriate choice is to follow those boundaries, not look for a convenient interpretation that allows an approach.

Sometimes restraint is a more meaningful expression of respect than romantic courage.

You can feel attracted to someone and choose not to act.

Attraction isn’t a command. And declining to pursue a romantic possibility doesn’t mean you’ve failed at dating.

A small practice: choosing the setting

Imagine these three situations.

Situation A: You meet someone at a community event. You’ve spent time talking, they’re participating willingly, and the event is ending. Neither of you has authority over the other.

Situation B: You visit a shop regularly. The employee recognizes you and starts friendly conversations while serving customers.

Situation C: You work with someone you find attractive. They’re a peer, but you’ve noticed they’ve recently been limiting conversations to work tasks and declining opportunities to socialize.

Which situations leave room for a respectful invitation?

In Situation A, a single clear invitation may be appropriate. The other person has an opportunity to decline, leave, and make their own decision.

In Situation B, friendliness is part of the employee’s professional role. Don’t turn their shift into a dating situation.

In Situation C, the changed behavior is a reason not to pursue a romantic invitation. You don’t need to investigate whether it means disinterest. Respecting distance is enough.

These examples aren’t an exhaustive rulebook. Real situations can be more complicated. The point is to assess the other person’s freedom, not search for technical permission to ask.

When you’re uncertain, it’s acceptable not to make a move.

Your responsibility ends where their choice begins

You cannot control whether someone is attracted to you.

You cannot guarantee that an invitation will be welcome, that the timing will be perfect, or that both people will interpret the interaction the same way.

But you can control how you approach.

You can choose a setting where the other person can leave. You can avoid power differences. You can respect the fact that someone is working or participating in an activity for reasons unrelated to dating.

You can communicate your interest plainly, once, without turning the answer into a negotiation.

And you can continue treating someone with dignity regardless of whether they say yes.

That’s what a respectful move looks like.

Dating requires putting your interest into words sometimes. It doesn’t require putting someone else’s freedom at risk.
