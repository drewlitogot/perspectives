The conversation had been going well. At least, that was what both people thought.

Leah, 38, was having coffee with Dani, 41, after meeting through a mutual friend. They had talked about work, favorite movies, and the terrible parking downtown.

Then Leah asked, “What made you decide to move here?”

Dani looked toward the window and went quiet.

Five seconds passed. Then ten.

Leah began wondering whether she had asked something inappropriate. Perhaps Dani was bored. Perhaps the question had brought up something painful.

Dani, meanwhile, was remembering three different reasons for moving and deciding which one would make the most sense to explain first.

Leah tried to rescue the conversation by changing the subject.

Dani felt interrupted.

By the end of their coffee, Leah worried that Dani hadn’t enjoyed the date. Dani wondered why Leah kept changing subjects before a conversation could develop.

Neither person understood what had happened.

Leah interpreted silence as a possible social message. Dani used silence to think. Each person made an assumption based on how they understood conversations.

The problem wasn’t that one person lacked feelings or that the other couldn’t communicate.

They were interpreting the same interaction through different expectations.

That is the central idea behind the double empathy problem.

Understanding goes both ways

For a long time, discussions about autism often approached social misunderstanding as a problem located inside the autistic person. If a conversation went badly, the explanation tended to focus on what the autistic person had failed to understand.

In 2012, autistic scholar Damian Milton challenged that framing.

Milton proposed that when people experience and interpret the world in substantially different ways, misunderstandings can happen in both directions. An autistic person may struggle to understand a non-autistic person’s intentions, while the non-autistic person may be equally uncertain about the autistic person’s intentions (Milton, 2012).

This is the double empathy problem: a gap in mutual understanding between people whose ways of experiencing the world differ.

The word double matters.

It means that understanding is not something only one person must provide. Both people bring their own assumptions, expectations, and experiences into an interaction.

Imagine two people looking at the sculpture described in the opening of this book. They stand in different places and wear different glasses.

One describes a curved surface. The other sees a straight edge.

They may both be accurately describing what is visible from their positions.

If each insists that the other must be mistaken, they will argue about something they could understand better by comparing their views.

Communication has a similar problem.

You know what your own words mean to you. You do not automatically know what those words mean to someone else.

You know why you paused before answering. You do not automatically know what another person’s pause means.

You may understand why you prefer quiet places or spontaneous plans. You do not necessarily understand why someone else feels differently.

And the other person does not automatically understand you.

This idea is especially useful in dating because two people are trying to learn about each other while knowing relatively little about how the other person thinks.

You may be attracted to someone, enjoy their company, and still misunderstand their intentions.

That doesn’t necessarily mean you are incompatible. It means there is something you haven’t learned about each other yet.

But the double empathy problem is not a rule that every misunderstanding is equally shared. It does not mean both people are always equally responsible when something goes wrong.

One person may have made an ordinary mistake. Another may have been careless, dismissive, or deliberately hurtful.

Understanding a difference does not excuse harmful behavior.

Why one person often gets blamed

Suppose you are talking with someone who communicates differently from most people you know.

Perhaps they rarely look directly at you while speaking. Their facial expressions may be unfamiliar. They might speak in an unusually formal way, ask detailed questions, or respond more slowly than you expect.

You might conclude that they are uninterested or uncomfortable.

But what evidence do you actually have?

You have noticed differences in how they communicate. You have not necessarily learned what those differences mean.

Research suggests that these judgments can have real consequences for autistic people.

In a series of experiments, Sasson and colleagues studied how non-autistic observers formed first impressions of autistic and non-autistic people.

Observers often rated autistic people less favorably after very brief exposure to their facial expressions, voices, or other aspects of their presentation. They were also less willing to interact with them.

When observers evaluated only transcripts of what people had said, the group difference in ratings was not statistically significant (Sasson et al., 2017).

That distinction matters.

The research did not show that autistic people had nothing interesting to say. Instead, observers responded differently to aspects of how people presented themselves.

The study cannot identify one particular expression, gesture, or vocal pattern as the cause. It also cannot tell you whether someone will be attracted to a particular autistic person.

But it challenges the assumption that a negative first impression must reflect something wrong with the person being judged.

Imagine meeting a woman who speaks enthusiastically about a topic but uses relatively little facial expression.

You might assume that her voice sounds excited while her face looks bored.

Someone who knows her well might recognize that this is how she normally expresses enthusiasm.

The difference lies partly in what each observer expects enthusiasm to look like.

The person being judged may not be having the same experience

A later study by Jones and colleagues asked 102 non-autistic American undergraduate students to evaluate video recordings of conversations involving autistic and non-autistic adults.

The observers rated mixed autistic and non-autistic conversations as less successful than other pairings. They also tended to evaluate autistic participants less favorably than the people who had actually spoken with them did (Jones et al., 2024).

In other words, watching a conversation and participating in it were not equivalent experiences.

An outside observer might see an interaction as awkward even when the participants themselves experienced it more positively.

This does not mean that every awkward conversation is secretly successful. Sometimes people genuinely are uncomfortable together.

It means you should be careful about treating your first interpretation as a fact.

If someone doesn’t laugh at your joke, they may not have understood it. They may have understood it and not found it funny. They may have enjoyed it without showing the reaction you expected.

You cannot determine which explanation is correct just by looking at them.

And the same applies in reverse.

Someone else might interpret your concentrated expression as anger when you are simply listening carefully.

A communication difference can create two mistaken impressions at once.

What happens when people actually talk?

The double empathy problem began as a theory. Researchers have since tried to examine it through experiments and real conversations.

The results are interesting, but they are not as simple as saying that autistic people communicate perfectly with other autistic people and poorly with everyone else.

In a 2020 experiment, Crompton and colleagues arranged 72 adults into groups of eight.

The researchers used a task resembling a familiar game: one person hears a story, tells it to the next person, and the story continues through a chain. Researchers then examine how much information survives.

Some chains contained only autistic participants. Others contained only non-autistic participants. The remaining chains mixed the two groups.

The autistic-only and non-autistic-only chains passed along similar amounts of information. Mixed chains lost more information as the story passed from person to person.

Participants in mixed chains also reported lower rapport, meaning they felt less comfortable or connected with the people they interacted with (Crompton, Ropar, et al., 2020).

The findings challenged the idea that autistic people are inherently poor communicators.

But there was an important question: would the same result appear in a larger study?

A larger study changed the picture

In 2025, Crompton and colleagues published a larger, preregistered replication involving 311 autistic and non-autistic adults across research sites in the United Kingdom and United States.

The researchers again compared information transfer in autistic-only, non-autistic-only, and mixed groups.

This time, they did not find that mixed groups lost more information.

All three kinds of groups transferred comparable amounts of information in the task (Crompton et al., 2025).

Another study involving 310 adults examined the transfer of mostly visual information needed for a puzzle-building task. It likewise found no statistically significant difference in performance between autistic-only, non-autistic-only, and mixed groups (Wilks et al., 2025).

These later results are important because they directly challenge the idea that information must become less accurate whenever autistic and non-autistic people communicate.

The original finding was not reproduced in the larger studies.

But this does not mean the double empathy problem has been disproved.

Transferring instructions through a group is only one aspect of communication.

A conversation can transmit information accurately while still feeling uncomfortable. Two people may understand each other’s words but misinterpret intentions, expressions, or emotional reactions.

That is why researchers also study rapport.

Getting the information right is not the same as feeling understood

The larger 2025 information-transfer study found a more complicated pattern of rapport than the original experiment.

Non-autistic-only groups generally reported higher rapport than both autistic-only and mixed groups. The difference between autistic-only and mixed groups was less pronounced than the original study might have suggested (Crompton et al., 2025).

Another study of 57 autistic and 51 non-autistic adults found that autistic participants reported lower rapport overall, regardless of whether their conversation partner was autistic. Awareness of the other person’s diagnostic status also mattered in some conditions (Efthimiou et al., 2025).

These results do not establish a universal advantage for matching people by neurotype.

They suggest that the quality of an interaction cannot be measured by a single question.

Did the information arrive accurately?

Did the participants feel comfortable?

Did they enjoy the interaction?

Did they want to spend more time together?

These are different questions, and their answers may differ.

A date could understand everything you say and still not feel connected.

Another person might need you to explain something twice but genuinely enjoy being with you.

Neither experience can be reduced to a communication score.

Why being understood can feel like relief

Think about how much effort you make during an ordinary conversation.

Do you monitor your facial expressions?

Do you remind yourself to make eye contact?

Do you carefully choose when to speak because you worry about interrupting?

Do you pretend to understand an implied meaning because asking questions might seem awkward?

Do you hide your excitement about something because previous people have called it strange?

Some people spend considerable energy adjusting how they communicate to match what others expect.

When that happens, meeting someone who responds comfortably to your natural communication style can feel different.

You may no longer need to calculate every expression or rehearse every sentence.

In interviews with 12 autistic adults in the United Kingdom, Crompton and colleagues found that participants often described spending time with other autistic people as easier and more accepting.

Some felt less pressure to conform to unfamiliar social expectations. Interactions with non-autistic people were sometimes described as requiring more adjustment or effort (Crompton, Hallett, et al., 2020).

These were personal accounts from a small sample, mostly women. The study cannot tell you what all autistic people experience.

A broader systematic review by Watts and colleagues brought together 52 qualitative research records involving 2,872 participants.

Across the studies, many autistic people described feelings of belonging, acceptance, and authenticity when interacting with other autistic people. The review also identified negative experiences and emphasized that shared autism did not automatically produce positive relationships (Watts et al., 2025).

That final point deserves attention.

Finding people who understand important parts of your experience can be valuable.

But sharing a neurotype does not guarantee kindness, compatibility, attraction, or respect.

Two people can both be autistic and have opposing preferences about noise, routines, conversation, physical closeness, or social activities.

They can understand each other’s experiences and still want different things.

And two people with very different brains can develop a satisfying relationship.

Being similar can make some things easier. It does not decide whether two people belong together.

Sharing a neurotype is not the same as sharing a personality

Consider Marcus and Eli, two autistic men in their thirties who meet through a local tabletop gaming group.

Marcus loves organized plans. He enjoys knowing exactly when an event will begin, how long it will last, and what everyone expects.

Eli prefers deciding what to do after seeing how he feels that day.

Both understand why unexpected changes might be difficult for someone. But their own preferences are different.

Marcus becomes frustrated when Eli won’t commit to a plan.

Eli feels constrained when Marcus wants to schedule every activity in advance.

They share an important experience without sharing the same needs.

Now consider Nia, an autistic woman dating Rachel, a non-autistic woman.

Rachel enjoys structured plans and quiet evenings. Nia enjoys occasional spontaneous trips and loves busy public events when they involve her favorite interests.

Their neurotypes differ, but some of their preferences fit together comfortably.

Other preferences require discussion.

Neither pair is automatically more compatible.

A 2026 survey of 142 autistic adults in Australia and New Zealand explored experiences of relationships with other autistic people, including friendships and romantic relationships.

Participants described acceptance and comfort, but also differences in values, interests, and capacities that could create difficulties (Minnell et al., 2026).

The lesson is not that neurotype is irrelevant.

It may matter greatly to how two people experience daily life.

But it is one part of compatibility, not a replacement for learning about the individual.

What about romantic relationships?

Much of the research on the double empathy problem involves unfamiliar people having short conversations or completing tasks.

That leaves an important question for dating:

What happens when people know each other well enough to build a relationship?

A study by Khaw and Vernon, published in a 2026 journal issue, examined romantic relationship experiences reported by 106 autistic adults in the United States.

Participants were divided according to the reported neurotype of a current or recent romantic partner:

  • 32 reported an autistic partner.
  • 46 reported a non-autistic neurodivergent partner.
  • 28 reported a neurotypical partner.

Overall relationship satisfaction was comparable across these groups.

But participants described different kinds of difficulties. Some respondents with autistic partners discussed communication as a shared challenge. Those with neurotypical partners sometimes described communication difficulties more in terms of their own challenges (Khaw & Vernon, 2026).

This is an important contribution because it examines romantic relationships rather than laboratory conversations.

However, it was a single cross-sectional study, meaning participants described their relationships at one point rather than being followed over time.

Only the autistic partners provided information. The researchers did not independently confirm partner neurotypes, and the sample may not represent the broader population of autistic adults.

The study also did not directly test whether the double empathy problem caused the communication difficulties participants described.

So it would be incorrect to conclude that partner neurotype never affects relationship satisfaction.

It would also be incorrect to claim that autistic people necessarily have happier relationships with autistic partners, or that relationships between autistic and non-autistic people are destined to struggle.

The evidence does not establish either conclusion.

Understanding each other takes more than matching labels

A separate UK study interviewed 16 autistic adults who identified as gender or sexual minorities about their romantic relationships.

Participants described the importance of communicating their individual expectations and developing ways of relating that worked for the people involved.

Several reported satisfying relationships with non-autistic partners. Shared autistic experiences could make understanding easier, but did not guarantee compatibility (Ciric et al., 2026).

This is a small qualitative study, not a test of which neurotype combination produces the happiest couples.

It nevertheless illustrates something useful.

The question is not simply, “Does this person have the same kind of brain as me?”

It is also:

“Does this person want to understand me?”

“Am I willing to understand them?”

“Can both of us talk about what we need?”

“Do our ways of living fit together well enough for the relationship we want?”

Those questions apply to every pairing.

An autistic person dating another autistic person.

An autistic person dating someone with ADHD.

Two people with ADHD.

A neurodivergent person dating a neurotypical person.

Two neurotypical people with different family histories or communication habits.

Labels may help explain a difference. They do not tell you how someone will treat you.

The same behavior can tell two different stories

Return to Leah and Dani at the coffee shop.

Dani paused before answering a question.

Leah interpreted the pause as possible discomfort.

Dani was thinking.

Both interpretations made sense from the information each person had.

But only one described Dani’s actual intention.

Now imagine they meet again.

Leah asks another question, and Dani pauses.

This time, Leah notices her own uncertainty instead of immediately changing the subject.

“Are you thinking about your answer, or would you rather talk about something else?”

Dani replies, “Thinking. It takes a minute to organize my thoughts.”

Leah says, “Got it. Take your time.”

Dani might also learn something about Leah.

“I sometimes need a long pause before answering. Does that make it hard to tell whether I’m following the conversation?”

Leah explains that she tends to associate long silences with discomfort.

They have learned something they could not have discovered simply by guessing.

Notice what changed.

Dani did not have to become faster at answering.

Leah did not have to stop caring whether the other person was comfortable.

Both people gained information about what the behavior meant.

This exchange is an illustrative example, not a tested method for preventing misunderstandings.

But it offers a practical approach: when a behavior could have several meanings, ask about the meaning instead of assuming you know it.

What if someone interprets your behavior incorrectly?

Perhaps you have been told that you seem angry when you are concentrating.

Or that you sound uninterested when you are tired.

Or that you are rude because you ask detailed questions.

You can explain your intention without apologizing for having a different communication style.

For example:

“When I’m concentrating, I don’t always show much expression. That doesn’t mean I’m upset.”

Or:

“I ask questions because I want to understand what you’re saying.”

You can also consider whether a small adjustment would help.

Maybe you tell someone that you need time before answering. Maybe you explain that you are enjoying a conversation even though you appear quiet.

These are choices, not requirements to disguise your personality.

You should not have to perform a different personality throughout a relationship just to be treated respectfully.

And you should not expect another person to guess your intentions forever.

Both people can help make the relationship understandable.

Being different does not make someone difficult

Imagine dating someone who communicates more indirectly than you do.

They might introduce an idea by mentioning something they would enjoy rather than asking you to do it with them.

You might hear only the literal information.

They may think you rejected a suggestion you never recognized.

Now imagine the opposite.

You ask directly whether they want to make plans.

They are surprised by how quickly you expect an answer.

You might interpret their hesitation as disinterest. They might simply need more time.

Neither person is necessarily being unreasonable.

You have different expectations about how plans are introduced and decided.

The double empathy problem offers an alternative to immediately labeling one person as too blunt, too sensitive, too confusing, or too demanding.

Instead, you can ask what each person expected.

You may discover that you disagree about something important.

That is useful information too.

Understanding another person’s perspective does not require you to adopt it.

For example, you might understand why someone prefers spontaneous plans and still decide that you need more predictability in a romantic relationship.

They might understand your preference and still decide they need more flexibility.

You can respect each other and recognize that your preferences may not fit.

As the earlier chapter on compatibility explained, a good relationship requires more than attraction or kindness. The way two people live and relate to each other also matters.

Mutual understanding helps you evaluate that fit. It cannot create compatibility where important needs consistently conflict.

If people keep telling you that you came across wrong

Being told repeatedly that your tone was sharp, your face was blank, or your message sounded cold is wearing, especially when none of it matched what you felt.

You can take the report seriously without accepting the verdict attached to it. “That wasn’t what I meant, and I believe that’s how it landed” holds both facts at once. What people saw is real information; their conclusion about your character usually isn’t.

You don’t have to perform a version of yourself that reads correctly to everyone. It is reasonable to say plainly what you meant and let the other person update, rather than treating every misreading as a fault of yours to fix.

Understanding is not the same as agreeing

The double empathy problem can be misunderstood in another direction.

If two people interpret an interaction differently, it may be tempting to say that neither is responsible for what happened.

But not every interaction is simply a misunderstanding.

Consider someone repeatedly making jokes about something their partner has said is painful.

The person making the jokes may claim they intended humor rather than harm.

Their partner may understand that intention and still find the behavior hurtful.

If the jokes continue after the person has been asked to stop, the problem is no longer just uncertainty about what the jokes mean.

Or imagine someone saying they do not want physical contact.

The other person does not need to understand every reason behind that preference.

They need to respect it.

A difference in perspective never turns a no into a yes.

And if someone has told you that your behavior is frightening, intrusive, or unwanted, the appropriate response is not to insist that they have misunderstood your intentions.

Stop the unwanted behavior. Respect their decision. Seek appropriate support if you are struggling to stop.

Your intentions matter when explaining what happened. They do not erase the other person’s experience.

The same principle protects you.

If someone repeatedly dismisses your feelings, ignores your requests to stop, or pressures you into situations you do not want, you do not have to solve the double empathy problem before deciding to leave.

You can understand why someone behaves a certain way and still decide that the behavior is unacceptable.

Both people contribute, but not always equally

There is another important limitation to the idea that understanding goes both ways.

People do not always have equal influence in a social situation.

Imagine an autistic person who has spent years learning the communication expectations of non-autistic coworkers, classmates, friends, and partners.

They may be accustomed to monitoring their expressions, adjusting their words, and suppressing natural behaviors to avoid negative judgments.

That process is called masking, as described earlier in the book.

A non-autistic partner may have had much less experience adapting to autistic communication.

Milton’s original account explicitly recognized that these differences occur within a wider social context, where autistic people may experience repeated misunderstanding and stigma (Milton, 2012).

The first-impression studies described earlier provide evidence that non-autistic observers can judge autistic people less favorably based on brief aspects of their presentation (Jones et al., 2024; Sasson et al., 2017).

So saying that misunderstanding is mutual does not mean both people have carried an equal burden.

A person who has spent years adapting to others may reasonably want a relationship where someone also makes an effort to understand them.

That is not asking for special treatment.

It is asking for reciprocity.

If you are dating someone whose brain works differently

You do not need to become an expert on autism, ADHD, or another person’s diagnosis before you can be a considerate partner.

Begin with the individual.

If your partner avoids eye contact, do not automatically interpret that as dishonesty or disinterest.

If they need time to answer, do not assume they are avoiding your question.

If they express excitement differently from you, do not require them to demonstrate their feelings through your preferred expressions.

Ask what their behavior means to them.

And explain your own experience.

You might say:

“When you get quiet, I sometimes worry that you’re upset. Is that what it means for you?”

Your partner might say:

“No. Usually it means I’m concentrating.”

Both people now have information they did not have before.

You can also be honest about your own needs.

Perhaps you need some verbal reassurance to know that a conversation is going well.

You can explain that without demanding constant performance from your partner.

And your partner can tell you whether that is something they are comfortable offering.

Understanding someone’s neurotype may provide useful context. It is not permission to assign a diagnosis-based explanation to every behavior.

A person with ADHD can be genuinely uninterested.

An autistic person can be affectionate, playful, or comfortable with indirect communication.

A neurotypical person can dislike eye contact or need time to answer.

A person with a history of trauma may be accurately recognizing concerning behavior rather than misinterpreting it.

The individual’s words, needs, and actions matter more than the assumptions attached to a label.

If you're dating someone whose face doesn't match their words

A flat expression, a long silence or a level tone can accompany strong feeling. Reading the face and ignoring the words will reliably give you the wrong answer.

Ask rather than interpret: “I can’t tell how you’re feeling about this. Can you tell me?” Then believe the answer you get, even when it doesn’t match what you thought you saw.

This runs both ways. Your own expressions may be just as unreadable to them, so saying your feelings out loud is not over-explaining. It’s supplying information neither of you can reliably guess.

A practical exercise: what happened, and what did you assume?

Think of an interaction that left you confused.

It could be a recent date, a conversation with someone you liked, or an exchange with a friend.

Choose something relatively ordinary rather than an experience involving threats or abuse.

Take a piece of paper or open a note on your phone.

Write four short answers.

1. What actually happened?

Describe only what you observed.

For example:

“They looked away while I was talking.”

Avoid adding an interpretation such as “because they were bored.”

2. What did you assume it meant?

Perhaps:

“They weren’t interested in the conversation.”

Be honest about your interpretation. You do not need to judge yourself for having it.

3. What else could it mean?

Perhaps the person was thinking, distracted, uncomfortable with eye contact, or simply looking around.

You do not need to generate every imaginable explanation.

Two or three reasonable possibilities are enough to remind yourself that your first interpretation is not the only possible one.

4. Is there something worth asking?

If the relationship matters and the uncertainty is important, a question may help.

For example:

“I wasn’t sure whether you were enjoying that conversation. How did it feel to you?”

Listen to the answer rather than treating it as another clue to analyze.

If the person does not want to discuss it, respect that too.

The purpose is not to extract an explanation from someone who does not wish to give one.

It is to recognize the difference between what you observed and what you concluded.

Try the exercise in reverse

Now think about a time someone misunderstood you.

Perhaps they thought you were angry when you were concentrating.

Or believed you were rejecting an invitation when you were trying to understand the details.

Write down what you intended and what the other person may have perceived.

Then ask yourself:

“Was there information I could have shared that might have helped?”

Sometimes the answer will be yes.

Sometimes you communicated clearly and the other person made an assumption anyway.

You are not responsible for preventing every possible misunderstanding.

But you can take responsibility for the information that only you know about your own intentions.

What to notice when getting to know someone

When you first begin dating, you may focus heavily on whether the other person finds you attractive.

You may wonder whether you said the right thing, interpreted their expressions correctly, or made a good impression.

Those questions can consume a great deal of attention.

There is another question worth asking:

What happens when the two of you misunderstand each other?

Does the other person respond to an ordinary question with patience?

Are they willing to explain what they mean?

Can they acknowledge that your experience differs from theirs?

Do they listen when you describe your perspective?

And do you offer those things in return?

You do not need someone who always understands you immediately.

You need to notice whether understanding becomes possible when both of you make a reasonable effort.

In one U.S. study of 67 autistic and 58 non-autistic adults having brief conversations with strangers, non-autistic participants generally preferred further interaction with non-autistic partners. Autistic participants disclosed more personal information to autistic partners, while their preference for further interaction with autistic partners was only a trend rather than a statistically conclusive finding (Morrison et al., 2020).

Those results cannot predict the success of an individual date.

But they reinforce a useful distinction: communication involves more than whether two people exchanged the right words.

Comfort, familiarity, and willingness to engage also matter.

A person who responds warmly when you clarify something may be easier to get to know than someone who consistently treats your questions as failures.

A person who is curious about your experience may be a better fit than someone who insists that their way of interpreting the world is the only reasonable one.

The same expectations apply to you.

If someone communicates differently, give them room to explain.

If you make an assumption and learn it was incorrect, revise your understanding.

If a difference keeps causing problems, discuss whether both of you can make adjustments you genuinely accept.

And if the relationship consistently requires one person to abandon important needs, recognizing that incompatibility is better than pretending the problem has been solved.

You do not need to read minds

There is a tempting idea behind much dating anxiety: that successful people instinctively know what everyone else means.

They recognize interest immediately.

They never mistake a friendly conversation for flirting.

They always know when to speak, when to wait, and what another person’s expression means.

Real conversations do not provide that level of certainty.

Even people who communicate comfortably can misunderstand each other.

The double empathy problem asks you to replace a familiar question:

“What’s wrong with me, or with them?”

With a more useful one:

“What are each of us understanding differently?”

That question cannot solve every disagreement. It cannot make another person interested in you, and it cannot make an incompatible relationship work.

But it gives you somewhere to begin.

You can learn about another person’s communication style without treating it as defective.

You can explain your own experience without demanding that they share it.

You can notice an assumption before allowing it to decide what someone else feels.

And you can remember that being misunderstood does not automatically mean you failed.

Leah and Dani did not need to become different people to enjoy another coffee together.

Leah needed to learn that Dani sometimes paused to organize an answer.

Dani needed to learn that Leah sometimes interpreted silence as discomfort.

Neither piece of information was available until they talked about it.

Understanding doesn’t require identical brains. It requires recognizing that another person’s experience may differ from yours, and being willing to find out how.

That brings you to the next question: once you recognize that people interpret words and behavior differently, how do you make your own meaning clear?
