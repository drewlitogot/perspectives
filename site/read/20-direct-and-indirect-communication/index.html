Someone says, “You should come over sometime.”

You think that sounds like an invitation.

But is it?

Do they want you to visit? Are they being friendly? Are they interested in dating you? Or is “sometime” simply a pleasant thing to say at the end of a conversation?

You might leave feeling excited, confused, or embarrassed that you don’t know what to make of it.

Then there’s the opposite problem. You tell someone, “I’m getting tired,” expecting them to understand that you’d like to go home. They respond, “Yeah, it’s been a long day,” and keep talking.

You thought you communicated something. They thought they agreed with an observation.

Neither person necessarily did anything wrong.

A great deal of human communication happens in the space between what someone says and what another person understands. Sometimes the intended meaning is obvious to both people. Sometimes it isn’t.

Dating makes this especially noticeable because the difference between friendship, romance, politeness, and rejection can matter enormously to the people involved.

The useful skill isn’t learning to decode every hidden message. It’s knowing when a message might mean more than its words, how to check without putting someone under pressure, and how to make your own meaning easier to understand.

What someone says and what someone means

Direct communication means saying what you intend in words that make your meaning clear.

“I’d like to go on a date with you.”

“I’m tired and want to go home.”

“I enjoy spending time together, but I’m only interested in friendship.”

These statements don’t require much interpretation. The listener might still have questions, but the central meaning is available.

Indirect communication means conveying an idea without stating it completely.

“It’s getting late.”

“We should do something together sometime.”

“I’ve got a lot going on this week.”

The listener uses the situation, the relationship, and previous conversations to work out what might be intended.

The meaning underneath the literal words is called subtext.

Imagine two people sitting in a restaurant.

One says, “It’s pretty noisy in here.”

That could be a simple observation. They might be enjoying themselves and commenting on the room.

But they might also mean, “I’m having trouble concentrating,” or “Can we go somewhere quieter?”

The words alone don’t settle the question.

Now imagine they previously told you that crowded restaurants can cause overload. You have more information. The comment might be a way of telling you that something needs to change.

You can respond to the possibility without pretending to know for certain:

“Would you like to find somewhere quieter?”

Notice the difference between interpreting and assuming.

An interpretation is a possibility you consider.

An assumption is something you decide is true without checking.

You need interpretations to understand conversation. You don’t need to treat them as facts.

Why not just say what you mean?

If direct communication can prevent confusion, why doesn’t everyone use it all the time?

Because communication isn’t only about transferring information. It also affects relationships.

Imagine two friends who have known each other for years. One has begun developing romantic feelings. They worry that revealing those feelings could change their friendship.

A direct invitation might feel risky. A vague suggestion to spend time together gives the other person room to respond without addressing romance at all.

Researchers Pinker, Nowak, and Lee proposed a model of indirect speech in which people sometimes use ambiguity to navigate socially risky situations. A message can suggest something without making the relationship immediately depend on how the listener responds (Pinker et al., 2008).

This is an explanation of how indirect speech can work, not proof of what someone means when they hint.

Indirectness can also be an ordinary way of being considerate.

Suppose you’re visiting someone, and they say, “Well, I’ve got an early morning tomorrow.”

They may be telling you about their schedule.

They may also be indicating that they’d like the visit to end.

A reasonable response is, “Would you like me to head out?”

You don’t need to accuse them of being unclear, and they don’t need to apologize for approaching the subject gently.

Sometimes people are indirect because they’re still figuring out what they want. They may feel uncertain about romance, uncomfortable making a request, or simply accustomed to a conversational style where suggestions are softer than statements.

None of these possibilities makes indirect communication automatically dishonest.

Equally, being direct isn’t automatically rude.

The goal is not to decide which style is morally superior. It’s to understand how each one works and recognize when a different approach is needed.

A hint isn’t a promise

Maya and Erin meet at a community art class.

They spend several sessions talking while they work. Erin remembers details about Maya’s life, laughs at her jokes, and asks whether she’s coming back next week.

Maya begins to wonder whether Erin might be interested in dating her.

That possibility is reasonable.

But Maya cannot conclude that Erin is romantically interested simply because Erin is attentive and friendly.

A person can enjoy your company without wanting romance. They can be warm, curious, thoughtful, and excited to see you again while wanting nothing beyond friendship.

The problem begins when you turn a possibility into a certainty.

You might think:

“She always comes over to talk to me. She must like me.”

Then:

“If she likes me, why hasn’t she said anything?”

And eventually:

“She’s giving me mixed signals.”

But Erin may not have intended any romantic signal at all.

You have information about her behavior. You don’t yet have information about her romantic intentions.

That distinction matters.

It also works in reverse. Someone might genuinely be interested in you while communicating in ways you interpret as ordinary friendliness. You cannot automatically know that either.

There is no universal gesture, facial expression, compliment, or friendly invitation that means romantic interest in every situation. This chapter’s evidence does not establish a reliable formula for interpreting these signals.

The later chapters on showing interest, reading interest, and distinguishing friendship from romance will examine these questions more closely.

For now, remember this:

A hint can give you a reason to ask a question. It does not give you permission to decide the answer.

The three meanings of “maybe”

One particularly confusing part of dating is that people can use uncertain language for different reasons.

Consider three situations.

Situation one: genuine uncertainty.

Andre asks Sam whether they’d like to go to a concert.

Sam says, “Maybe. I need to check whether I’m working that evening.”

Sam has identified a specific obstacle. Andre can leave the decision open without assuming that Sam will attend.

Situation two: a gentle decline.

Andre asks Sam out for coffee.

Sam says, “Maybe sometime,” but doesn’t offer a date or otherwise indicate a definite wish to meet.

Sam may be declining indirectly. Andre does not know precisely why Sam answered that way.

The appropriate response is to accept that there is no plan.

Situation three: uncertainty about the relationship itself.

Andre and Sam have been seeing each other. Andre asks whether Sam wants to call their relationship exclusive.

Sam says, “I’m not sure yet.”

This is a meaningful answer. It tells Andre that exclusivity has not been agreed to.

Andre can decide whether continuing under that uncertainty works for them. They cannot treat Sam’s uncertainty as a promise.

These situations sound similar, but their consequences differ.

The safest general rule is to treat an unclear answer as unclear rather than converting it into the answer you hope to hear.

Someone who wants to make plans can participate in making them. Someone who is unsure can have time to decide. Someone who declines doesn’t owe an explanation.

If you have already asked clearly and the other person isn’t participating, stop pursuing an answer. Repeatedly asking does not create consent or interest.

You don’t need to discover whether a vague response was secretly a rejection. You only need to understand what you have permission to do next.

Literal understanding is not a failure

Perhaps you’ve often been told that you take things too literally.

Someone says, “We’ll have to get together soon,” and you ask when.

Someone jokes that a meeting was “a complete disaster,” and you want to know what actually happened.

Someone says, “Do whatever you want,” and you take the statement at face value, only to discover later that they expected you to understand their displeasure.

These experiences can be frustrating. You may feel that everyone else received a list of social rules that nobody gave you.

For some people, understanding implied meaning requires conscious effort. For others, the interpretation seems immediate. Neither experience tells you everything about the person’s intelligence, empathy, or ability to form relationships.

A meta-analysis by Kalandadze and colleagues examined studies of figurative language comprehension in autistic and non-autistic participants. The autistic groups performed less well on average, but the difference became much smaller and statistically nonsignificant when groups were matched on core language ability (Kalandadze et al., 2018).

The findings were also more complicated than the familiar stereotype that autistic people cannot understand sarcasm. Differences depended on the kind of language being tested and how the studies were designed.

These were primarily language-comprehension studies, not studies of adults interpreting romantic invitations.

They cannot predict whether a particular autistic person understands jokes, flirtation, irony, or indirect speech.

An action-focused review of communication in autistic adults likewise describes both differences and strengths in language processing. It emphasizes that understanding communication in context can demand effort, even when someone performs well on structured language tasks (Marchena et al., 2025).

This distinction matters.

You might understand exactly what a sentence means and still be unsure why someone chose to say it at that moment.

Or you might understand several possible meanings and find it difficult to decide which one the speaker intended.

That is different from not understanding language.

If you prefer precision, you don’t need to abandon it to become a better communicator. You need a way to handle the situations where precision is missing.

The misunderstanding goes both ways

Consider another couple.

Nina is autistic. Her boyfriend, Marcus, is not.

Marcus says, “It would be nice if you planned something for us this weekend.”

Nina hears a general preference. She agrees that it would be nice, but she doesn’t realize Marcus is asking her to take responsibility for arranging an activity.

By Friday, Marcus is upset.

He says, “You knew I wanted you to plan something.”

Nina replies, “I knew you thought it would be nice. I didn’t know you were asking me to do it.”

Marcus feels ignored. Nina feels accused of breaking an agreement she never knew existed.

Neither person’s interpretation is difficult to understand once it’s explained.

Now reverse the situation.

Nina tells Marcus, “I don’t want to go to the party because it’s going to be too loud.”

Marcus assumes she’s anxious about meeting his friends. He reassures her that everyone is friendly.

But Nina wasn’t asking for reassurance. She was explaining that the environment would be difficult for her.

Marcus missed the meaning of a direct statement because he interpreted it through his own expectations.

Both partners can misunderstand. Both can feel certain that their interpretation was obvious.

This is the double empathy problem introduced in the previous chapter: people with different experiences and communication habits can misunderstand each other in both directions.

Williams and colleagues examined conversations involving eight autistic adults interacting with familiar partners and strangers of different neurotypes. Mutual understanding was relatively high across conditions. The researchers described how different assumptions about which information matters in a conversation could help explain misunderstandings (Williams et al., 2021).

A larger study by Crompton and colleagues, involving 311 participants in an information-transfer experiment, did not reproduce an earlier finding that mixed autistic and non-autistic groups lost information more quickly than same-neurotype groups. It did find differences in reported rapport, including an improvement associated with disclosure of diagnostic status (Crompton et al., 2025).

These studies used different methods, and neither establishes what will happen between any two dating partners.

What they do establish is a reason to avoid the claim that cross-neurotype communication is necessarily worse or that one kind of person is responsible for every misunderstanding.

The point of the sculpture-and-glasses picture from the beginning of this book is useful here. You can describe what you see, but you cannot assume that someone standing elsewhere sees exactly the same thing.

And neither person gets to declare their view the only one that matters.

That doesn’t mean every misunderstanding carries equal responsibility. If you state a clear limit and the other person ignores it, the problem is not simply that you communicate differently.

If you answer the question you were actually asked

“Do you want to get dinner sometime?” can be a real question about dinner, or an opening move. Answering the words in front of you is not a failure of comprehension; it’s a reasonable reading that sometimes turns out to be the wrong one.

When the stakes are low, guessing wrong costs little. When they’re higher, a short check is cheaper than a misunderstanding: “I’d like that. Are you asking as a date, or just food?”

People sometimes speak indirectly because a direct version feels exposing. Asking plainly gives them a way out of that bind, so it’s often a kindness rather than an interruption.

Asking for clarity without turning it into an interrogation

A clarification question is a request to explain something you haven’t understood.

It isn’t a demand for the answer you want.

The simplest approach is to identify the uncertainty and ask one specific question.

For example:

“I want to make sure I understand. Are you asking me to plan something for Saturday?”

Or:

“When you say we should go out sometime, do you mean as friends or on a date?”

Or:

“Do you mean you’re not available this weekend, or that you’d prefer not to make plans?”

The third question should only be asked where an existing conversation genuinely calls for clarification. It isn’t an invitation to challenge someone who has already declined.

You don’t need to make every question sound formal. Sometimes “What do you mean?” is perfectly adequate.

But tone and context matter, particularly when two people have different expectations about questions.

The review by de Marchena and colleagues reports that asking questions to confirm understanding can be considered polite in autistic communication, while some non-autistic listeners may interpret the same questions as challenging or rude (Marchena et al., 2025).

That doesn’t make either interpretation correct in every situation.

It does explain why an ordinary question can sometimes start an argument.

Explain the reason for the question

If your partner seems to interpret requests for clarification as criticism, it may help to make your intention explicit.

“I’m asking because I want to understand, not because I’m disagreeing.”

Or:

“I’m not sure which meaning you intended.”

These are practical suggestions, not phrases proven by research to prevent misunderstandings.

You can also ask your partner how your questions sound to them.

“I’ve noticed that when I ask what you mean, it sometimes seems to upset you. How does it come across?”

The conversation might reveal something useful. Perhaps your partner hears repeated questions as skepticism. Perhaps you tend to ask several questions in quick succession when you’re confused. Perhaps they were taught to treat questions about their decisions as disrespectful.

Those are possibilities to explore, not explanations to assign.

If one wording doesn’t work, you can try another.

And if someone asks you to explain yourself, consider answering the question rather than assuming they are looking for an argument.

“I meant I’d like you to make the dinner reservation.”

That sentence can settle a misunderstanding faster than debating whether the original meaning should have been obvious.

Not every uncertainty needs a question

If someone mentions that the weather is pleasant, you probably don’t need to investigate whether they are requesting a walk.

If they say, “I’m going to get another drink,” you don’t have to determine whether they are inviting you to accompany them.

Some ambiguity is harmless.

Ask when the distinction would affect what you do, what you agree to, or how you understand the relationship.

And recognize when asking would put someone under unnecessary pressure.

If a person has already declined your invitation, asking whether they truly meant no is not clarification. It is questioning their decision.

A respectful question makes room for an answer.

It doesn’t make the other person defend one.

Sometimes the answer is that the person doesn’t know

You may hope that a direct question will produce a perfectly clear answer.

But some questions have genuinely uncertain answers.

“Do you want a relationship with me?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Are you feeling distant because you’re upset with me?”

“I’m not sure. I need some time to think.”

“Would you rather we spend more time together or have more time alone?”

“Both, depending on the day.”

These answers might be frustrating, particularly if uncertainty makes you anxious.

But an honest “I don’t know” is more useful than a definite answer someone gives merely to end the conversation.

You can ask about what happens next without demanding an immediate decision.

“Would you like to think about it and talk again next week?”

They can agree, decline, or remain uncertain.

You can also decide how much uncertainty you’re willing to accept.

If exclusivity is important to you and the other person isn’t ready to agree to it, you don’t have to pretend you’re comfortable. You can explain what you need and make decisions about your participation in the relationship.

That is different from trying to make them change their answer.

Clarity tells you where things stand. It cannot guarantee that both people want the same thing.

If you're dating someone who takes you at your word

If your date answers exactly what you asked and misses what you implied, they are not being obtuse or avoiding you. They are responding to the sentence you said.

Say the thing itself rather than a softened version of it: “I’d like to see you again this week” rather than “it’d be nice to do this again sometime”. The second is polite and genuinely ambiguous.

This isn’t a demand that you drop all social cushioning. It means the important parts are worth stating outright, because a hint that goes unnoticed reads to you as rejection and to them as nothing at all.

If you need time to understand

Sometimes the difficulty isn’t figuring out what someone means. It’s having to figure it out immediately.

A conversation may contain several questions, background noise, strong feelings, unfamiliar expressions, and the expectation that you answer quickly.

You may understand the words but need more time to work out your response.

If there is more coming in than you can handle at once, you may experience overload. For some people, overload can lead to shutdown, when speech becomes very difficult.

There’s no requirement to answer an important relationship question at conversational speed.

You can say:

“I heard what you said. I need a little time to work out what I think.”

Or:

“There are several things in that question. Can we take them one at a time?”

Or simply:

“I can’t answer properly right now. Can we return to this later?”

If someone cannot speak at the moment, they may prefer another way to communicate. The important point is to agree on a practical way to resume the conversation rather than treating a temporary inability to answer as agreement.

A partner can help by allowing a pause and not demanding an instant explanation.

That is consideration, not special treatment.

If you sometimes speak before you’ve worked out your meaning

The opposite can also happen.

You might give a quick answer and realize a few seconds later that it didn’t express what you intended.

You might interrupt because a thought feels urgent, or respond to one part of what someone said while missing another.

A study of 179 multilingual adults found that participants with ADHD reported more communication difficulties than comparison participants, particularly around regulating conversational behavior. Findings about nonliteral language were more complicated and depended partly on language proficiency (Köder et al., 2025).

This was a self-report study, not a study of romantic partners, and it doesn’t describe every adult with ADHD.

Still, if the experience sounds familiar, you can use a simple correction.

“That came out differently than I meant. Let me try again.”

A conversation isn’t a courtroom where every sentence becomes permanent the moment you say it.

You can correct yourself.

If you regularly find yourself being interrupted, rushed, or unable to finish explaining something, you can also ask for more room to speak.

Both people’s ways of processing information deserve consideration.

When past experiences change what you hear

A person says, “Can we talk later?”

You immediately think you’ve done something wrong.

Your stomach tightens. You start replaying the last conversation, trying to identify what upset them.

But they might simply want to discuss plans when they have more time.

If you’ve experienced threatening or unpredictable reactions in past relationships, an ambiguous message may feel especially significant.

Research reviewing information processing in people with PTSD has found some evidence of a tendency to interpret ambiguous situations as threatening, although the findings vary across studies and methods (Bomyea et al., 2017).

That evidence concerns PTSD, not everyone who has experienced trauma or rejection. It doesn’t prove that someone with a painful history is misinterpreting their partner.

Sometimes concern is entirely justified.

The useful distinction is between what happened and what you fear it means.

You can ask yourself:

“What did this person actually say?”

Then:

“What am I adding to it?”

For example:

They said, “Can we talk later?”

You inferred, “They’re angry with me and might leave.”

Those are not the same statement.

If the relationship feels safe enough to ask, a simple clarification may help:

“Is this something urgent, or can it wait until tonight?”

You don’t have to dismiss your own concerns. If the person has a history of threatening, intimidating, or punishing you, take that history seriously rather than explaining the concern away as a communication difference.

Clarity can help with ordinary uncertainty. It cannot make an unsafe relationship safe.

Being clear about your own intentions

So far, much of the work has involved understanding what other people say.

But you also have responsibility for what you communicate.

Imagine you’re hoping to date a friend. You keep suggesting activities together while hoping they’ll eventually realize your interest.

They accept the invitations and enjoy themselves.

After several weeks, you’re disappointed that the friendship hasn’t turned romantic.

You might feel that you’ve been making your interest obvious.

But the other person has not agreed to the meaning you assigned to those activities.

At some point, if the circumstances are appropriate and you’re both free to make a choice, honesty is more useful than continuing to rely on hints.

“I enjoy spending time with you, and I’d be interested in going on a date. Would you like that?”

This doesn’t guarantee a yes.

It makes the question understandable and leaves room for a genuine answer.

If the answer is no, accept it. Don’t treat the friendship as a failed negotiation or the other person as someone who withheld a reward.

The same principle applies when you want friendship rather than romance.

“I’d like to keep spending time together as friends, but I’m not looking for anything romantic.”

It’s also useful when you’re unsure what you want.

“I like getting to know you, but I don’t know yet whether I want a relationship.”

People are allowed to want different things. They’re allowed to change their minds. They’re allowed to decide that a relationship isn’t right for them.

Clear language helps everyone make informed decisions.

In a small UK interview study involving 16 gender- and sexually diverse autistic adults, participants described the value of explicitly discussing relationship expectations, needs, and boundaries rather than assuming that conventional dating rules applied to them (Ciric et al., 2026).

An Australian qualitative study of autistic and non-autistic adults also describes communication and differences in expectations around intimacy, including the importance of clarity for some autistic participants (Sala et al., 2024).

These studies offer insight into people’s experiences. They don’t establish that everyone should use identical language or that directness guarantees compatibility.

But they do illustrate something practical: two people can choose how to define their relationship rather than relying entirely on unspoken assumptions.

Direct doesn’t mean harsh

Some people hear “be direct” and imagine a person saying whatever enters their mind without considering the effect.

That isn’t the goal.

Compare these statements:

“You never make sense.”

“I’m having trouble understanding what you’re asking me to do.”

Or:

“You’re being ridiculous.”

“I see this differently, and I’d like to explain why.”

The second statement in each pair communicates a real difficulty without attacking the other person’s character.

You can be direct about a preference, a feeling, or a disagreement while remaining respectful.

You can also be gentle without becoming unclear.

“I’ve enjoyed meeting you, but I don’t want to continue dating.”

That is both considerate and definite.

The research on couple communication provides a useful reminder that directness is not the same as positive communication. A meta-analysis of longitudinal couple studies found generally small associations between broadly positive and negative communication and later relationship quality. It did not test whether direct requests are better than indirect ones (Kanter et al., 2022).

You don’t need to make every interaction intensely serious or eliminate humor, tact, and playfulness.

You do need to communicate important things in a way the other person has a fair chance of understanding.

And if your preferred style is indirect, you can learn to be clearer when circumstances require it without abandoning the rest of your personality.

When you and your partner prefer different styles

Jules and Oliver have been dating for six months.

Both are autistic, but they don’t communicate identically.

Jules prefers short, specific statements and dislikes guessing what a question means.

Oliver enjoys playful exaggeration, makes frequent jokes, and sometimes communicates a preference through hints.

Neither person’s autism tells the other how to interpret every sentence.

One evening, Oliver says, “I guess the kitchen is going to clean itself.”

Jules replies, “No, it won’t.”

Oliver is irritated because he meant that he wanted help cleaning.

Jules is irritated because Oliver could have asked.

Later, they discuss the disagreement.

Oliver explains that he sometimes uses humor to express frustration. Jules explains that this particular joke sounds like a statement rather than a request.

They agree that household tasks will be discussed directly when someone needs help, while jokes are still welcome when both people understand they’re joking.

Nothing about that agreement requires either person to become someone else.

Now consider a different pairing.

Rosa is not autistic. Her girlfriend, Tessa, is.

Tessa often asks Rosa to explain what she means. Rosa initially interprets the questions as disbelief, particularly when she’s describing something upsetting.

Instead of deciding that Tessa is questioning her honesty, Rosa asks why she wants so many details.

Tessa explains that she’s trying to understand which parts of the experience matter most to Rosa.

That gives them something practical to work with.

Rosa can say, “I need you to listen first. I’ll explain the details afterward.”

Tessa can say, “Okay. Tell me when you want questions.”

The value lies in learning each other’s meaning, not assigning one person the permanent role of translator.

It also matters that the work remains mutual.

If you always have to request explanations while your partner refuses to clarify, that’s a problem worth discussing.

If your partner routinely explains their meaning but you dismiss what they say because you prefer your own interpretation, that’s something you can change.

A relationship cannot depend on one person continually hiding their natural communication style through masking while the other makes no effort to understand.

Both people deserve a voice in how communication works between them.

Some things are too important to leave implied

Consider these statements:

“I’m not ready.”

“I don’t want to do that.”

“Please stop.”

There is no puzzle hidden inside them.

A person’s refusal, discomfort, or uncertainty must not be converted into a secret invitation.

This is especially important with physical and sexual intimacy.

Flirting may involve ambiguity. Consent cannot rest on an assumption drawn from ambiguous behavior.

RAINN’s U.S. sexual-violence prevention guidance describes consent as voluntary, clear, and ongoing. Agreement to one activity, or agreement at an earlier time, does not automatically apply to another situation (RAINN, n.d.a).

U.S. legal definitions and details vary by state (RAINN, n.d.b). The later chapters on consent explain these issues more fully.

The immediate practical rule is straightforward.

If you don’t know whether someone wants a particular intimate interaction, ask before acting. If the answer is unclear, wait or stop.

A person who says no does not owe you a more convincing explanation.

A person who is quiet, hesitant, or unable to answer has not supplied permission.

And if you recognize yourself searching for a hidden yes inside someone’s refusal, stop pursuing that interaction. Respect the answer and seek support if you are finding it difficult to do so.

This isn’t a communication game to win. Another person’s autonomy matters more than finding out whether your interpretation was correct.

A small practice exercise: fact, interpretation, question

The next time a conversation leaves you uncertain, try separating it into three parts.

You can write them down or work through them mentally.

1. What did the person actually say or do?

Keep this to something you could describe without guessing at their intentions.

“Alex said they were busy on Saturday.”

2. What interpretation did you add?

“They’re avoiding me.”

Or perhaps:

“They’re interested but want me to suggest another day.”

Notice that both are interpretations.

3. Is a clarification useful and appropriate?

If Alex was responding to an invitation, you can accept that Saturday isn’t available. You don’t need to investigate why.

If you and Alex are already discussing shared plans, you might ask:

“Should we leave Saturday open and plan something another time?”

If they decline or don’t participate in arranging anything, leave the matter there.

Try the exercise with a lower-stakes situation too.

Your date says, “This place is really busy.”

Fact: they commented on the number of people.

Possible interpretations: they’re uncomfortable; they’re surprised; they’re simply making conversation.

Question: “Would you prefer somewhere quieter?”

If they say no, you have your answer.

The point isn’t to become a detective who examines every sentence for secret meaning. It’s to notice when your mind has supplied information the other person hasn’t given you.

One conversation worth having

If you’re already dating someone, ask about communication when neither of you is upset.

“What helps you understand what I’m asking?”

Then share your own preference.

“I do better when plans and requests are stated directly. I don’t always recognize hints.”

Your partner might say they appreciate having time to think, that certain kinds of questions feel uncomfortable, or that they like a little humor before discussing serious subjects.

You can talk about what works for both of you.

You don’t need a formal agreement for every conversation. Even a brief discussion can make your expectations more visible.

And preferences aren’t permanent. People can need different things at different times.

Someone who enjoys playful hints over dinner may need very direct language when tired, stressed, or making an important decision.

Clarity is an act of cooperation

You will misunderstand people sometimes.

They will misunderstand you.

A date might think you’re joking when you’re serious. You might mistake politeness for attraction. A partner might hear criticism in a question that was only meant to establish what happened.

These moments don’t automatically mean that two people are incompatible.

What matters is what happens when the misunderstanding becomes visible.

Can you ask without accusing?

Can you explain without making the other person feel foolish?

Can you accept an answer that isn’t the one you hoped for?

Can you recognize when a person has already told you enough, even if you wish they’d said something different?

Being understood isn’t the same as being agreed with. Someone can understand your feelings perfectly and still want something else.

And understanding another person’s meaning doesn’t require abandoning your own needs.

The practical skill is to make the invisible part of a conversation visible when it matters.

Say what you mean when you can. Ask when you genuinely need clarification. Make room for a different interpretation. Accept that some questions will remain unanswered.

The next chapters will put those skills to work in everyday conversations, shared interests, messages, and misunderstandings.

Then you’ll need them for the most important messages of all: your limits, and theirs.
